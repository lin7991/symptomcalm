Pterygium and Pinguecula: A TCM View of Growths on the Eye

Symptom Guide · 8 min read

Maybe you saw it in a photograph first — a small yellowish bump on the white of your eye, just beside the colored part. Or maybe it is a fleshy film that seems to be creeping, millimetre by millimetre, from the corner of your eye toward your pupil. Either way, the visit ends with a word you have to write down: pinguecula, or pterygium, followed by "it's benign — we'll watch it."

That reassurance is usually the right one. These growths are common and overwhelmingly non-cancerous. But "benign" is not the same as "nothing to think about." One of these two growths can slowly distort your vision, and both tend to flare and irritate in exactly the conditions that caused them in the first place.

Modern ophthalmology understands the mechanics of these growths well, and we will cover that first. What Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) adds is a different question — not "what is this lump?" but "why did this particular spot of tissue decide to grow?"

Before we dive in: this article focuses specifically on these surface growths and what may support the eye surface around them. For the broader picture of how sun exposure affects the eye over decades, see our guide to sunlight and eyes through a TCM lens. For moisture, see dry eye from a TCM perspective; for itching, see eye allergies and TCM.

What Those Growths Actually Are: The Western View

The white of the eye is covered by the conjunctiva — a thin, clear membrane whose job is to keep the surface lubricated and to host part of the eye's local immune defense. Because it is the outermost layer, it takes the full force of everything the environment throws at the eye. Two different things can grow on it.

A pinguecula is a small yellowish-white patch or bump, most often on the side of the eye nearest the nose. Under a microscope it shows what pathologists call elastotic degeneration: the collagen and elastic fibers in that patch of tissue have broken down and rearranged, with proteins and fats deposited in their place. It is, quite literally, sun-aged tissue. A pinguecula does not grow onto the cornea and does not damage vision. Its main effects are cosmetic, plus irritation, dryness, and a gritty foreign-body feeling — especially on windy, dry, or bright days.

A pterygium (from the Greek word for wing) is a fleshy, vascular growth that starts on the conjunctiva and extends onto the cornea. Because it is made of blood vessels and connective tissue, it looks pinkish or red and often has a triangular, wing-like shape pointing toward the pupil. That extension is why it matters: as it pulls on the cornea it can induce astigmatism, and if it reaches the visual axis, it can block vision.

Both growths share a risk profile, and one factor dominates:

  • Cumulative ultraviolet (UV) exposure — the most consistent finding in the research, with a clear dose-response: more lifetime sun, higher odds.
  • Wind, dust, and sand — chronic mechanical and drying irritation of the surface.
  • Dry eye and low humidity — a surface that cannot keep itself moist stays inflamed.
  • Smoke, chemical fumes, and long hours outdoors without eye protection.

Modern care is mostly about managing the irritation — lubricating drops, sun and wind protection, and avoiding triggers — with surgery reserved for when the growth threatens vision, keeps flaring, or bothers the person enough to warrant it. Surgery decisions are not trivial: these growths can return after removal, which is why surgeons use techniques such as conjunctival grafts to lower the odds.

The TCM View: When the Surface Grows a Callus

Think about what happens to the skin on the palm of your hand if you do heavy manual work for years. The tissue thickens. It builds a callus — a locally reinforced patch that exists because that spot kept getting rubbed. Nothing about the callus is malignant or mysterious; it is the direct result of chronic mechanical stress, laid down in the one place the stress kept landing.

That is a remarkably good way to think about a pinguecula or pterygium. The eye's surface membrane keeps getting hit by sun, wind, and dryness — always in the same place, most often the sun-facing side — and it responds by thickening and, in a pterygium's case, by growing a network of small vessels into the area. TCM's classical name for the creeping one captures the effect exactly: 胬肉攀睛, "creeping flesh climbing toward the pupil."

Picture it another way: ivy on a window. While the ivy creeps along the edge of the frame, the view is fine. The problem arrives only if it reaches the glass — and then the center of the view.

What TCM asks next is where a "callus" like this comes from. Its answer is not a single cause but an accumulation pattern, and it names three:

  • Wind-Heat at the surface — the active irritation. Redness, burning, tearing, sensitivity to light and wind: an ongoing disturbance at the outermost layer.
  • Phlegm-Damp congealing — the fixed deposit. In TCM theory, Dampness that is not properly transformed can condense into Phlegm, and Phlegm is the classic explanation for any fixed nodule, lump, or deposit anywhere in the body. A yellowish pinguecula reads as exactly that.
  • Blood stasis with Yin deficiency — the growth that advances. Long-standing stagnation in the fine channels of the surface, on a background of dryness, explains a growth that keeps extending rather than sitting still.

Notice how the TCM patterns map onto the modern picture: a chronically irritated surface (Wind-Heat), a fixed aged deposit (Phlegm-Damp), and an advancing vascular growth (Blood stasis). Different words, same three observations.

Clear boundary: nothing in TCM theory suggests that any dietary or acupressure approach makes a growth disappear. What these approaches address is the environment — irritation, dryness, and the inflammatory conditions the growth keeps reacting to. Whether a growth needs to be removed is a decision for an ophthalmologist, made after examining your eye.

Pattern 1: Wind-Heat at the Surface — The Eye That Keeps Reacting

1. Wind-Heat at the Surface — The Eye That Keeps Reacting

You might recognize this if: your eye is red or pinkish right where the growth sits, it burns or itches, it waters in wind, and it feels worse in sun, dry air, air conditioning, smoky rooms, or after long screen sessions. The growth seems to inflame in episodes — better for a while, then angry again.

The TCM view: this is the active layer of the picture — Wind-Heat disturbing the surface. Wind explains why symptoms shift with weather, wind, and exposure; Heat explains the redness, burning, and sensitivity. In TCM theory it is this repeating irritation that keeps prompting the tissue to respond, which is why calming the surface is seen as the first job, ahead of worrying about the tissue itself.

Supporting approach: cool the surface and stop adding fuel. A cool compress over closed eyes for a minute or two when it flares; lubricating drops during long screen or dry-air exposure; wrap-style sunglasses outdoors; and avoiding hot, spicy, and heavily alcoholic meals during a flare. Traditional cooling additions to the diet include cucumber, celery, mung bean, and chrysanthemum tea. Also keep your hands off it — rubbing or picking at the surface is precisely the irritation that started the problem.

Pattern 2: Phlegm-Damp Congealing — The Yellow Deposit

2. Phlegm-Damp Congealing — The Yellow Deposit

You might recognize this if: the growth looks like a yellowish, slightly raised patch that is not going anywhere; your eyes feel sticky in the morning; you notice heaviness, puffiness, or a muzzy head; and your digestion is sluggish — bloating, a greasy feeling after rich food, loose stools, or a sensation of phlegm in the throat. Your diet may lean toward fried food, dairy, sugar, or regular alcohol.

The TCM view: Dampness is what happens when fluids are not transformed and moved properly; when Dampness sits long enough, TCM describes it condensing into Phlegm. A fixed, yellowish deposit in one spot fits the description almost too neatly. Crucially, in this reading the eye is not the problem — it is the place where a whole-body handling-of-fluids issue has become visible. (Our TCM Basics guide to Phlegm explains that framework in detail.)

Supporting approach: make the fluid system's job easier. Reduce fried food, heavy dairy, refined sugar, and alcohol; build meals around cooked vegetables, light proteins, and traditional Damp-clearing foods such as celery, cucumber, winter melon, barley, and Job's tears (coix seed); keep meals warm and regular rather than iced and erratic; and move daily — walking, cycling, or anything that makes you breathe a little harder helps the body shift fluids.

Pattern 3: Blood Stasis with Yin Deficiency — The Growth That Creeps

3. Blood Stasis with Yin Deficiency — The Growth That Creeps

You might recognize this if: the growth has visible tiny vessels in it, has been present for years, and seems to be extending further onto the cornea at each check-up. Alongside it you may notice dryness that is worse at night, a dry mouth or throat, thin or scanty fluids, occasional night sweats, heat in the palms and soles, and a tendency to stay up late.

The TCM view: Blood stasis — stagnation in the fine vessels of the eye surface — is what makes a growth fixed and slowly extending rather than transient. Yin deficiency is the background that allows it: Yin represents the body's cooling, moistening, and anchoring side, so when it runs low the surface stays dry and inflamed, giving stagnant tissue room to persist. The two together describe a chronic, advancing, vessel-rich growth.

Supporting approach: moisten and move. Favour Yin-nourishing, moisture-friendly foods — pear, white fungus, black sesame, goji berries, cooked greens, adequate fluids through the day — and cut back on late nights, alcohol, smoking, and heavily spicy food, all of which TCM sees as drying. Gentle blood-moving foods used in cooking, such as small amounts of turmeric and ginger, are traditional; if you take blood-thinning medication, check with your clinician first. And keep the surface comfortable: night-time lubrication and a humidifier do a great deal for a dry, stagnant eye.

What May Support Eye Surface Comfort

There is no known way to make these growths shrink at home. What you can do is lower the daily irritation load that keeps the surface inflamed — which is the same advice modern eye care gives, and where TCM and modern practice point in the same direction.

Block the Two Biggest Inputs

  • Sun: wrap-style sunglasses with side coverage, ideally UV400, plus a wide-brim hat. Side coverage matters more than most people expect, because light can reach the eye from the side.
  • Wind and dust: ordinary glasses or sunglasses outdoors on windy days, windows up in the car, and avoid sitting in drafts from fans or air conditioning.

Keep the Surface Moist

  • Preservative-free lubricating drops during dry, windy, smoky, or screen-heavy days — ask a pharmacist or optometrist which type suits you.
  • Warm compresses for a few minutes in the evening, which support the oil glands in the eyelids and help the tear film hold on longer.
  • A humidifier in heated or air-conditioned rooms, especially overnight.
  • Blink deliberately at screens, and follow the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds.

Hands Off

Do not rub, pick at, or apply anything to the growth. No herbal pastes, no "eye washes" that have not been recommended by a clinician, no attempts to scrape it. These growths sit on the thinnest, most delicate surface tissue in the body, and mechanical irritation is exactly what makes them angry.

Gentle Acupressure for the Eye Surface

Use clean hands and keep the pressure light and steady — one to two minutes per point, both sides, once or twice a day. Never press on the eyeball itself, and stop immediately if anything feels sharp or painful.

  • BL-1 (Jingming) — in the small hollow just inside the inner corner of the eye. The classic eye point; use a very light fingertip only.
  • Yintang — midway between the inner ends of the eyebrows. Traditionally calming for the eye area and helpful when irritation makes the whole face feel tight.
  • GB-20 (Fengchi) — in the hollows at the base of the skull. Named for "wind" and classically used when symptoms shift with weather and drafts.
  • GB-37 (Guangming) — on the outer lower leg, about four finger-widths above the ankle bone; the classic distal point for eye conditions.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — below the kneecap, four finger-widths down and one finger-width outward; supports the Spleen, and therefore the fluid-transforming system behind Pattern 2.
  • LV-3 (Taichong) — on the top of the foot between the first and second toe bones; moves Liver Qi, a useful complement to the "clear the eyes" tradition.

No pregnancy-contraindicated points are used here, but if you are pregnant or managing another health condition, talk to your clinician before starting any acupressure routine.

Traditional Teas for the Eyes

Chrysanthemum and goji berry teas are the classic "brighten the eyes" preparations in Chinese daily practice — light, cooling, and easy to sip rather than a remedy to rely on. Lemon balm and peppermint are gentler Western alternatives if you want a cooling cuppa. If you are allergic to plants in the daisy family (ragweed, chamomile, chrysanthemum), skip chrysanthemum.

What Research Says

This is one of the areas where modern research and traditional reasoning line up unusually well — the mechanism modern science describes is, in essence, a callus caused by light.

  • Peripheral light focusing. Work by Coroneo and colleagues describes how light entering the eye from the side — not from the front — is refracted and concentrated onto the nasal side of the limbus, the border where the cornea meets the white of the eye. That spot is precisely where pterygia tend to begin. It is a strong mechanistic explanation for the classic location, and the reason side coverage on sunglasses is worth insisting on.
  • The tissue really is "sun-aged." Histological studies of pinguecula and pterygium consistently describe elastotic degeneration — abnormal, clumped elastic material replacing normal conjunctival fibers — along with increased connective tissue. In plain language: the surface has been remodeled by years of exposure.
  • Geography follows the sun. Prevalence rises with proximity to the equator and with outdoor occupation, and studies in tropical and high-altitude populations report rates far above temperate-climate averages. The pattern across populations matches the UV-dose explanation.
  • Dry eye travels with it. Studies frequently find tear film instability and dry eye signs more often in people with pterygium. Which direction the relationship runs is debated, but the association supports the practical emphasis on lubrication and humidification.
  • Surgery works, but recurrence is real. Simply excising a pterygium and leaving bare sclera has relatively high recurrence rates; using a conjunctival autograft — sometimes with fibrin glue or adjunctive medication — lowers them substantially. This is why surgeons often wait until the growth threatens vision or keeps inflaming before operating: removal is worth doing well, once.
  • Chrysanthemum is being studied in the lab. In vitro work on chrysanthemum flavonoids such as luteolin shows antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity, and some laboratory studies describe effects on new blood vessel formation. This is early-stage cell research, not clinical evidence about eye growths — but it is a reasonable explanation for why the traditional "eye tea" has lasted so long.
Important: none of this research supports the idea that a growth can be dissolved with diet, teas, or acupressure. Prevention here means protection — reducing UV, wind, and dryness — and monitoring means keeping the eye exams your clinician recommends.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:
  • Buy wrap-style sunglasses with side coverage and a wide-brim hat. This is the single most evidence-aligned habit for these growths, and the one that addresses the mechanism directly.
  • Lubricate on the hard days. Preservative-free drops for dry, windy, smoky, or screen-heavy conditions, plus a humidifier at night in heated rooms.
  • Keep your hands off it. No rubbing, picking, or home applications of any kind.
  • Cool the surface when it flares: a cool compress for a minute or two, less alcohol, spicy food, and smoke during a flare-up.
  • If it looks yellowish and sits still, think Damp-Phlegm: lighter, warmer, less greasy food, more daily movement.
  • If it is vessel-rich and extending, that is the pattern to raise with your eye doctor — monitoring is what protects your vision.
  • Keep your exam schedule. Ask at each visit whether the growth has extended onto the cornea and whether astigmatism has changed — those are the numbers that matter.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ Seek medical attention if you notice any of the following:

  • A growth that has reached or is approaching the pupil, or any new blurring, ghosting, or distortion — this is the point at which an ophthalmologist should assess whether the cornea is being affected.
  • Rapid growth, bleeding, a color change, or a newly appearing lump on the white of the eye — rare, but these need to be examined to rule out other conjunctival lesions.
  • Pain, marked redness, discharge, or a foreign-body sensation that does not settle — this may be an infection or a significant inflammation, not the growth itself.
  • Any growth with contact lens wear — lens use plus a surface problem needs prompt review.
  • Sudden flashes of light, a sudden increase in floaters, or a shadow or curtain across your vision — urgent, and unrelated to benign surface growths.
