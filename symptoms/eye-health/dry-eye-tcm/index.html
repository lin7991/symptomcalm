Dry Eye Syndrome: A TCM Perspective on Moisture Balance

Symptom Guide · 11 min read

Your eyes feel gritty, scratchy — like there's sand trapped under your lids. Artificial tears help for twenty minutes, then the discomfort returns. By late afternoon, your vision blurs slightly, and the burning sensation makes you want to close your eyes and not open them again.

This isn't occasional dry eye from a long day at the computer. This is Dry Eye Syndrome (DES) — a persistent condition where your eyes either don't produce enough tears, or the tears they produce evaporate too quickly.

In Western medicine, DES is understood as a dysfunction of the tear film — the complex three-layer moisture layer that protects and lubricates the eye surface. But the question of why the tear film keeps failing often remains unanswered.

Traditional Chinese Medicine offers a different lens. In TCM theory, the eyes are deeply connected to the body's internal fluid economy — specifically to Yin and Blood, the body's moistening and nourishing substances. When these resources run low, the eyes are often the first place to show the effects.

Beyond Dry Eyes: What Makes Dry Eye Syndrome Different

It's worth pausing to distinguish Dry Eye Syndrome from the occasional dry eyes most people experience. Occasional dryness — after a long flight, a poor night's sleep, or a day in a dry, air-conditioned office — is a temporary response to environmental conditions. It resolves when the trigger is removed or with simple countermeasures like artificial tears or rest.

Dry Eye Syndrome, by contrast, is persistent. The tear film — a delicate three-layer system of oil, water, and mucus — doesn't function properly on an ongoing basis. This can happen because:

  • Aqueous deficiency: The lacrimal glands don't produce enough of the watery middle layer
  • Evaporative dry eye: The meibomian glands (the oil-producing glands at the eyelid margin) are blocked, allowing tears to evaporate too quickly
  • A combination of both — which is the most common presentation

From a TCM perspective, this distinction matters. Aqueous deficiency points more strongly toward Yin and Blood deficiency patterns, while evaporative dry eye (with its blocked oil glands) suggests Dampness or Phlegm obstructing the channels around the eyes. Understanding which mechanism is at work can help guide a more targeted approach.

The TCM View: When the Body's Moisture System Runs Low

In TCM, the eyes are said to be "the window of the Liver" — and the Liver is responsible for storing Blood and ensuring its smooth circulation to the eyes. Blood provides the physical nourishment the eyes need for sharp vision, clear focus, and healthy tissue.

But moisture — the tear film itself — is governed by a broader system. Think of the body's fluid economy like a well-maintained irrigation system:

  • Yin is the water volume — the body's cooling, moistening, and nurturing substance that provides the raw material for all fluids, including tears
  • Blood is the enriched nutrient fluid that the Liver sends to the eyes — it carries the building blocks for healthy eye tissue
  • Body Fluids (Jin Ye) are the lighter, more mobile fluids that moisten the surface of the eyes, skin, and mucous membranes
  • Qi is the pump — the energy that transports these fluids to where they're needed, including the eye area

When any part of this system falters, the tear film suffers. Dry Eye Syndrome, in TCM theory, is rarely about a single deficiency — it's usually a picture where the fluid supply is low (Yin or Blood deficiency), the distribution is poor (Qi stagnation), or both.

3 Common Patterns in Dry Eye Syndrome

In TCM clinical thinking, Dry Eye Syndrome tends to cluster into a few recurring patterns. Most people with DES will recognize themselves somewhere in this list.

1. Liver Blood Deficiency — The Eyes Need Nourishment

What this feels like: Dry eyes that are worse after prolonged reading, screen use, or any sustained visual focus. Vision may be slightly blurry or feel "tired." You may also notice brittle nails, pale complexion, scanty periods, or dizziness.

Why it happens: The Liver stores Blood and opens into the eyes. When Liver Blood is deficient, the eyes are starved of the nourishing material they need to stay moist and well-lubricated. This pattern is especially common in people who overwork, sleep poorly, or have heavy menstrual flow.

What may support this pattern: Foods traditionally used to nourish Blood include dark leafy greens, cooked beets, goji berries, liver (in moderation), and bone broth. Acupressure points like LV-3 (Taichong) on the top of the foot between the big toe and second toe, and SP-10 (Xuehai) on the inner thigh, are traditionally used to support Blood circulation to the eyes. Adequate sleep — particularly before midnight — is considered essential, as the Liver cleanses and replenishes Blood during deep rest.

2. Yin Deficiency with Empty Heat — The System Runs Low and Hot

What this feels like: Dry, burning eyes that feel hot rather than just dry. The eyes may look slightly reddened. You may also experience night sweats, a dry throat in the morning, afternoon heat flushes, scanty dark urine, or a sensation of heat in the palms and soles.

Why it happens: Yin is the body's cooling, moistening substance. When Yin is depleted — often through chronic stress, overwork, insufficient sleep, or aging — the system has less coolant. The heat that results (called "Empty Heat" in TCM) dries out fluids further, creating a double effect: less moisture plus more evaporation. This pattern is common in perimenopause and in people who have been on long-term stress hormones.

What may support this pattern: Cooling, moistening foods are traditionally emphasized — cucumber, watermelon, pear, mung bean, tofu, and sesame. Overstimulating foods (spicy, deep-fried, coffee, alcohol) may aggravate the heat component. Acupressure point KID-3 (Taixi) behind the inner ankle bone and KID-6 (Zhaohai) just below the inner ankle are considered foundational for nourishing Yin. Rest, especially in a quiet, cool environment, is part of the traditional approach.

3. Qi and Blood Stagnation with Dampness — The Flow is Blocked

What this feels like: Dry eyes that also feel heavy, achy, or tired. The eyelids may feel sticky or slightly swollen. Artificial tears don't help much. You may notice the eyes feel worse in humid weather or after eating heavy, greasy foods. Tearing may paradoxically occur — watery eyes that still feel dry.

Why it happens: This pattern maps well to evaporative dry eye and meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD). In TCM theory, Dampness (accumulated fluid waste) and Phlegm (thicker turbid matter) can obstruct the fine channels around the eyes, blocking the meibomian glands and preventing the oil layer of the tear film from doing its job. The tears are present, but they evaporate because the sealant layer is missing. At the same time, Qi stagnation — often from prolonged screen use, stress, or poor posture — means the energy needed to clear the obstruction isn't flowing.

What may support this pattern: Warm compresses are the single most practical self-care tool for this pattern — the warmth moves Qi and softens hardened glandular secretions. Aromatics in the diet (ginger, turmeric, fennel, basil) are traditionally used to move Qi and transform Dampness. Reducing dairy and fried foods may help reduce Phlegm production. Acupressure point ST-36 (Zusanli) below the knee supports overall Qi and fluid metabolism, while LV-3 moves stagnant Qi.

Special Considerations: When Dry Eye Syndrome Has Specific Triggers

Dry Eye Syndrome doesn't exist in a vacuum. Several common contexts can tip a borderline case into a chronic one.

Contact Lenses and Dry Eye

Contact lenses sit on the tear film, absorbing water and reducing oxygen flow to the cornea. In TCM terms, the lens itself creates a minor obstruction — it partially blocks the interaction between the Qi of the eye surface and the external environment, and it adds a demand for moisture that the tissues must meet. For someone with a pre-existing Yin or Blood deficiency, contact lenses may push the system past its threshold. Switching to daily disposables, reducing wear time, or transitioning to glasses during high-focus activities may help.

Menopause and Hormonal Dry Eye

The dramatic drop in estrogen and progesterone during menopause affects all moisture-producing tissues, including the lacrimal and meibomian glands. This maps directly to Yin Deficiency with Empty Heat in TCM — the hormones are part of the body's Yin resource, and their decline creates both a moisture shortage and a relative heat state. This is why perimenopausal and postmenopausal dry eye often responds well to Yin-nourishing approaches, including cooling foods, adequate rest, and targeted acupressure points like KID-6.

Sjögren's Syndrome

Sjögren's is an autoimmune condition where the body's immune system attacks moisture-producing glands, including the lacrimal and salivary glands. In TCM, this is understood as a complex pattern involving both Deficiency (the glands can't produce fluids because the underlying resources — Yin and Blood — are depleted) and pathological factors (chronic internal Heat, Blood Stasis, or Dampness-Heat from the autoimmune process itself). Professional TCM guidance is recommended for Sjögren's-related dry eye, as the pattern requires more nuanced individual assessment than self-care alone can address.

Post-Surgical Dry Eye (LASIK, Cataract)

Any surgery involving the cornea or eyelid temporarily disrupts the nerve supply and healing environment of the eye surface. In TCM theory, surgery creates local Qi and Blood Stagnation, and the healing process demands additional resources from the Liver and Kidney systems. Post-surgical dry eye often resolves as the nerves regenerate, but supporting the body with Blood-nourishing foods, adequate rest, and gentle acupressure may support the recovery process.

Daily Practices for Tear Film Support

Beyond pattern-specific approaches, several practices are widely recommended in TCM for supporting the tear film and maintaining eye moisture.

Warm Compress Routine for Meibomian Gland Health

For the evaporative subtype of DES (which accounts for the majority of cases), warm compresses are one of the most evidence-supported self-care tools. A warm, damp cloth applied to closed eyelids for 5-10 minutes liquefies the hardened oils in the meibomian glands, allowing them to flow into the tear film and slow evaporation. In TCM terms, warmth moves Qi and Blood — it clears the local stagnation that prevents the glands from functioning. Gentle massage of the eyelids after the compress (from the outer corner toward the inner corner) helps express the softened oils.

Blink Rate Awareness

People blink about 66% less often during concentrated visual tasks. Each blink spreads a fresh layer of tears across the eye surface and stimulates the meibomian glands. In TCM, incomplete blinking — a common habit during screen use — is like closing the irrigation valve partway: the moisture is released, but not enough to do the job. Setting a timer to consciously blink fully every 20 seconds can help restore the natural pump that maintains the tear film.

Environment Adjustments

Dry, moving air accelerates tear evaporation. In TCM terms, external Wind (drafts, fans, air conditioning) dries the surface fluids and stresses the Wei Qi (defensive energy) around the eyes. Humidifiers, placing plants on your desk, and positioning fans away from your face are small adjustments that reduce this evaporative load. Wearing wrap-around sunglasses on windy days provides a physical barrier against Wind that dries the tear film.

Hydration from Within

TCM thinks about hydration differently — it's not just how much water you drink, but how well your body retains and distributes it. Sipping warm water throughout the day (rather than gulping cold water) is considered more effective for the body's fluid economy, as the Spleen (which manages fluid metabolism) works more efficiently with warmth. Bone broths, light soups, and teas made from moistening herbs — chrysanthemum, goji berry, and licorice — are traditionally used to build the body's deeper fluid reserves.

What Research Says About TCM Approaches to Dry Eye Syndrome

A growing body of research has examined TCM approaches specifically for Dry Eye Syndrome, as distinct from general dry eye symptoms.

  • Acupuncture for DES: A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis (Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine, 8 RCTs, 742 participants) found that acupuncture was associated with improvements in tear film stability (measured by tear break-up time) and subjective dry eye symptoms compared to artificial tears alone. The review noted that acupuncture may stimulate neural pathways that regulate lacrimal gland secretion.
  • Warm compress therapy: A 2019 study (Cornea, n=94) found that consistent warm compress application (10 minutes daily for 4 weeks) significantly improved meibomian gland function and tear film stability in evaporative dry eye patients — supporting the TCM principle that warmth moves Qi and clears local obstruction.
  • Chinese herbal medicine for DES: A 2018 systematic review (BMC Complementary and Alternative Medicine, 12 RCTs) found that Chinese herbal formulas formulated to nourish Yin and Blood were associated with improvements in both objective tear film measurements and symptom scores in DES patients, with the strongest evidence appearing for formulas containing goji berry (gou qi zi) and rehmannia (shu di huang).
  • Omega-3 and tear film health: A 2016 randomized trial (International Journal of Ophthalmology, n=60) found omega-3 supplementation improved meibomian gland function and reduced ocular surface inflammation in DES patients — relevant to TCM as omega-3-rich foods (flaxseed, walnuts, fish) are traditionally classified as Yin-nourishing and moisture-supporting.
  • Stress and dry eye: A 2019 study (Ocular Immunology and Inflammation) found that individuals with higher perceived stress scores had significantly elevated inflammatory cytokine levels in their tears — supporting the TCM observation that emotional states (particularly Liver Qi stagnation) affect eye health and moisture balance.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ While Dry Eye Syndrome is a chronic condition, certain symptoms require prompt medical attention: sudden worsening of dryness in one eye, eye pain that is moderate to severe, sensitivity to light (photophobia), redness accompanied by discharge, a sensation that something is stuck in your eye (especially if you wear contact lenses), or any vision changes including blurred vision that doesn't clear with blinking. These may signal infection, corneal damage, or other conditions that require professional care. TCM is a complementary approach and does not replace a comprehensive eye examination or medical treatment.

Practical Takeaways

Supporting Your Tear Film with TCM

  • Know your subtype: If artificial tears help briefly but dryness returns quickly, you may have evaporative dry eye (likely a Dampness/Stagnation pattern). If your eyes feel gritty and burning, the pattern may be Yin or Blood deficiency.
  • Warm compresses are foundational: For the majority of DES cases involving meibomian gland dysfunction, 5-10 minutes of warm compress therapy daily is one of the most effective self-care tools. Warmth moves Qi and liquefies glandular secretions.
  • Nourish from within: Yin- and Blood-building foods (goji berries, cooked greens, bone broth, cucumber, pear, sesame) support the body's deeper moisture reserves. Avoid oversimulating foods (spicy, fried, coffee, alcohol) if your dry eyes feel hot or burning.
  • Use targeted acupressure: LV-3 (top of foot between big and second toe) moves Liver Qi to the eyes. KID-6 (below inner ankle bone) nourishes Yin. ST-36 (below kneecap, outer leg) supports overall fluid metabolism.
  • Address triggers: Contact lens wear, menopause, and post-surgical recovery all increase moisture demand. Adjusting lens habits, supporting Yin during perimenopause, and resting adequately after eye surgery may help reduce the risk of DES becoming chronic.
