Myopia and TCM: Supporting Eyesight Through the Liver

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

You're watching your child squint at the whiteboard from the back row, or holding a book a little closer than last year. Maybe you're the adult whose prescription seems to thicken every two years, and you've quietly wondered: is there anything I can actually do about this?

You're not alone — and it's not in your head. Myopia, or nearsightedness, is the most common vision condition on the planet, and it's expanding at an astonishing rate. Research published in Ophthalmology projects that by 2050, nearly half the world's population — around 4.8 billion people — will be myopic. East Asia is already there: in parts of China, South Korea, and Japan, 80–90% of young adults are nearsighted.

If you've been told "it's genetic, nothing to do," this article offers a different, complementary lens. Traditional Chinese Medicine doesn't frame myopia as a defect of the eyeball. It frames it as a story of growth and nourishment — a story where the Liver, the Kidneys, and the Spleen each play a role in how your eyes (or your child's eyes) develop and hold up under the demands of modern life.

In TCM theory, clear vision isn't just about the lens focusing correctly. The eyes are the most "nourishment-hungry" tissue in the body — and their health depends on the systems that deliver that nourishment. Think of the eye as a house under construction during childhood: it needs a blueprint, building materials, and a power supply. When all three are strong, the house goes up straight. When one runs short, the structure compensates — and the shape changes.

Why Myopia Is a Growth Story: The Western View

From a modern medical perspective, myopia happens when the eyeball grows too long from front to back (axial elongation). Light entering the eye focuses in front of the retina instead of directly on it, so distant objects appear blurry while close ones stay sharp. The longer the eye, the stronger the prescription needed to correct it.

Critically, myopia is a developmental condition — it usually begins and progresses during childhood and adolescence, exactly when the eye is still growing. Two environmental drivers stand out in the research:

  • Insufficient outdoor time. A landmark school-based trial in China found that adding roughly 40 minutes of outdoor activity to each school day was associated with a significantly lower rate of new myopia over three years, compared with a control group.
  • Heavy near work. Meta-analyses of studies in children report a dose-response association between time spent on near work — reading, screens, homework — and the odds of developing myopia.

The standard management — corrective glasses, contact lenses, and in some cases orthokeratology or low-dose atropine — is well established and important. TCM doesn't replace any of it. What TCM offers is a complementary way of understanding why some eyes cope better than others under the same conditions, and what daily habits may support the developing visual system from the inside.

The TCM View: Eyes Are Nourished, Not Just Focused

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, the eyes are governed by the Liver — the classic saying is that "the Liver opens into the eyes" (肝开窍于目). This doesn't mean the anatomical liver controls vision. It means the Liver system, in TCM theory, stores Blood and is responsible for sending that Blood upward to moisten and nourish the eye. Vision depends on a steady supply of Liver Blood the way a camera depends on its battery: without it, the image gets dim, dry, and unstable.

Before we dive in: For a detailed breakdown of the Liver system and how it nourishes the eyes, check out our TCM Basics article: The Liver in TCM: Your Body's Traffic Controller. And for a deeper look at what "Blood deficiency" actually means, see Blood Deficiency in TCM. This article focuses on how those concepts apply specifically to myopia and the growing eye.

But myopia is a growth condition — and in TCM, growth is the territory of the Kidneys. Kidney Jing (Essence) governs growth, development, and the formation of bones and marrow, including the structures of the eye. The Spleen, meanwhile, is the engine that converts food into Qi and Blood — the fuel that pays for all of it.

So myopia, from a TCM perspective, involves all three systems:

  • Kidney Jing — the blueprint and foundation budget for growth
  • Liver Blood — the material delivery that nourishes the eye tissue
  • Spleen Qi — the power supply that turns food into usable fuel

When a child's eye is growing fast (a growth spurt), the demand on all three systems spikes. If the delivery system is running low — because of poor sleep, overwork, or a diet that doesn't build Blood — the eye's development may not get the support it needs. That's the complementary story TCM tells about myopia: not a flaw in the eyeball, but an imbalance between growth, nourishment, and fuel.

Pattern 1: Liver Blood Deficiency — The Delivery System Runs Low

1. Liver Blood Deficiency — The Delivery Truck Runs Light

You might notice this if: Your eyes feel dry, gritty, or easily fatigued by the afternoon — especially after long days of screens or study. Distance vision may feel "fluctuating": sharp on good days, blurry on tired days. You might also notice brittle nails, a pale complexion, light or irregular periods (in women), or difficulty falling into deep sleep.

The TCM view: Liver Blood is the substance that rises to moisten and nourish the eyes. When it runs low — from insufficient sleep, prolonged visual fixation without rest, or a diet lacking Blood-nourishing foods — the eye tissue gets a thinner supply. The eye still functions, but it's operating on a reduced delivery schedule. In children who study and screen for hours on end, this pattern is especially common: the eyes are working constantly, while the Blood that feeds them is being drained faster than it's replenished.

Supporting approach: Prioritize sleep — in TCM, Liver Blood is restored during deep, restful sleep, especially before midnight. Include Blood-nourishing foods (more below). And build in genuine visual rest: the eyes need breaks where they look far away, not just blink at a screen.

Pattern 2: Kidney Jing Deficiency — The Growth Blueprint Under Pressure

2. Kidney Jing Deficiency — The Foundation Budget Is Thin

You might notice this if: Myopia developed or jumped during a growth spurt — the classic story of a child who was fine in grade school and suddenly needs glasses at 10–13. There may be a family history of myopia. In adults, the pattern shows up as "my prescription keeps creeping" combined with lower back ache, fatigue, or feeling that you don't bounce back the way you used to.

The TCM view: Kidney Jing governs growth and development — it's the constitutional blueprint the body follows while building itself. Growth spurts place a sudden surge of demand on this reserve. In TCM theory, the eyes receive nourishment from Jing-derived marrow, so when the growth budget is stretched thin, the developing eye is one of the first structures to show the strain. This isn't a "deficiency disease" — it's a resource-allocation story, and it explains why myopia so often appears precisely when a child is growing fastest.

Supporting approach: Protect the reserve: consistent sleep schedules, growth-friendly rest, and traditionally Jing-supporting foods such as black beans, walnuts, bone broths, and small servings of animal protein. Growth is expensive — the budget needs to be refilled, not just spent.

Pattern 3: Spleen Qi Deficiency with Eye Overuse — The Fuel Processor Overloaded

3. Spleen Qi Deficiency with Eye Overuse — The Power Plant Can't Keep Up

You might notice this if: This pattern is most common in school-age children and screen-heavy adults. The eyes get tired quickly during study, concentration fades after an hour, and there's often a digestive side: poor appetite, picky eating, bloating, soft stools, or low energy after meals. "My child eats, but doesn't seem to convert food into energy" is a classic parent description.

The TCM view: The Spleen is the body's fuel processor — it transforms food into Qi and Blood, the very resources the eyes depend on. When Spleen Qi is weak and the eyes are simultaneously overworked (hours of near work, minimal breaks), the system faces a double squeeze: production is low and demand is high. The result is a child who is tired, struggles to concentrate, and whose eyes fatigue almost immediately — a setup that, in TCM theory, does not give the growing eye the stable environment it needs.

Supporting approach: Warm, cooked, easily digestible meals — the classic Spleen-friendly diet: porridge, soups, steamed vegetables, ginger. Regular meal times. Short movement breaks between study blocks. The digestive system and the visual system are far more connected than they appear: both run on the same fuel supply.

What May Support Eyesight from a TCM Perspective

None of the following is a substitute for glasses, contact lenses, or regular comprehensive eye exams. Think of these as daily habits that may support the nourishing systems behind healthy vision — especially for children whose eyes are still growing.

Foods Traditionally Used to Nourish the Eyes

  • Goji berries (Gou Qi Zi) — the classic "brighten the eyes" food in TCM. Exceptionally rich in zeaxanthin, a carotenoid that concentrates in the macula. A small handful in tea, oatmeal, or soup is the single easiest daily addition.
  • Cooked dark leafy greens — spinach, kale, broccoli: Blood-building and rich in lutein. Lightly cooked is the traditional preference, as raw salads are considered harder on the Spleen.
  • Eggs — a complete protein that supports both Blood and Jing; the yolk is a top source of absorbable lutein and zeaxanthin.
  • Black sesame seeds — traditionally associated with Kidney Jing and Liver Blood nourishment. Sprinkle on porridge or toast.
  • Warm, cooked meals overall — the Spleen-friendly foundation. Soups, congee-style porridge, and steamed dishes are easier to convert into the fuel the eyes run on.
  • Animal liver (in moderation) — a traditional Blood-nourishing food and a concentrated source of vitamin A, the nutrient directly involved in retinal function. Moderation and quality sourcing matter; not for everyone.

Gentle Acupressure for Eye Support

Self-acupressure is a safe, accessible way to support eye comfort between meals and study blocks. Use clean hands and gentle, steady pressure — 1 to 2 minutes per point, both sides, once or twice a day. Stop if anything feels sharp or painful.

  • BL-1 (Jingming) — just inside the inner corner of the eye, in the small hollow by the nose bridge. The classic "bright eyes" point. Use your fingertips very gently — never press directly on the eyeball.
  • GB-20 (Fengchi) — in the hollows at the base of the skull, where the neck muscles meet the skull. Releases tension that travels up into the eyes.
  • GB-37 (Guangming) — on the outer lower leg, about four finger-widths above the ankle bone. The name literally means "bright light" — traditionally used for eye conditions, and one of the most commonly cited points in eye-related research.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — below the kneecap, about four finger-widths down and one finger-width outward. The classic Spleen/Stomach "energy" point, supporting the fuel processor behind everything else.
  • LV-3 (Taichong) — on the top of the foot, in the depression between the first and second toe bones. Moves Liver Qi and supports the Liver-Blood-to-eyes connection.

The "Look Far" Habit

Modern life trains the eyes to focus at arm's length or closer — all day, every day. TCM's answer is remarkably aligned with current research: the eyes need regular practice focusing at distance. Outdoor time doubles as the best "look far" exercise available:

  • The 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds.
  • Daily outdoor time for children: current research suggests outdoor daylight exposure — not just distance viewing — may play a role in supporting eye development. Aim for an hour a day where practical.
  • Screen hygiene: keep screens at a comfortable distance, take real breaks, and avoid using screens to wind down in the last hour before bed.

What Research Says

Modern research is exploring the territory TCM has mapped for centuries. The evidence is preliminary — frame everything here as "may support," not guarantees:

  • The myopia epidemic is real. A widely cited analysis in Ophthalmology projects that 49.8% of the world's population — nearly 5 billion people — will be myopic by 2050, up from roughly 23% in 2000. The rise is steepest in East Asia, where urbanization, intense schooling, and limited outdoor time are all implicated.
  • Outdoor time matters. A randomized school-based trial in China (JAMA, 2015) added about 40 minutes of outdoor activity to the school day and found a meaningfully lower incidence of new myopia over three years compared with the control group. This is among the strongest environmental findings in myopia research — and it aligns closely with the TCM principle that the eyes need distance, daylight, and movement.
  • Near work shows a dose-response. A meta-analysis of studies in children found that each additional hour of near work per day was associated with higher odds of myopia. In TCM terms: sustained visual fixation "consumes" the resources that nourish the eyes, so rest and distance are not optional extras.
  • Auricular (ear) acupressure is being studied. A handful of small trials have explored ear-seed acupressure for childhood myopia — tiny seeds taped to ear points, a technique with a long traditional history. Some report slower progression or better visual comfort, but the studies are small and the results mixed. This is promising-but-preliminary territory.
  • Zeaxanthin and lutein support the retina. These carotenoids — highly concentrated in goji berries, egg yolks, and leafy greens — accumulate in the macula and are associated with retinal health in observational research. The traditional "brighten the eyes" foods turn out to be objectively rich in retinal-supporting nutrients.
Important: This research is promising but preliminary, and none of it suggests that TCM approaches replace corrective lenses or medical myopia management. Think of these habits as supporting the environment in which your eyes — or your child's eyes — grow and function. Regular comprehensive eye exams remain the foundation of eye care.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:
  • Keep up with eye exams. Children should have regular comprehensive eye exams — school screenings can miss problems. Glasses prescribed by a professional are never optional; they're the foundation.
  • Make outdoor time a daily vision habit. Daylight + distance viewing is the single best-supported lifestyle factor. Aim for about an hour a day for children, where practical.
  • Build the "look far" rhythm. 20-20-20 during study and screen time — set a timer if you need to.
  • Feed the nourishing systems. Goji berries, eggs, cooked leafy greens, black sesame, warm cooked meals — small daily additions, not occasional overhauls.
  • Protect sleep. Liver Blood and Kidney Jing are both restored during deep sleep. A consistent early bedtime is one of the most effective "eye supplements" in TCM theory.
  • Try the 2-minute acupressure reset. BL-1 and GB-20 during study breaks, GB-37 and ST-36 at other times of day — gentle, 1–2 minutes per point.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ Seek professional evaluation if you or your child experience: sudden or rapid vision changes, new squinting or eye-rubbing in a child, headaches associated with near work, double vision, eye pain, a sudden increase in floaters, flashing lights, or any loss of peripheral vision. Children who are struggling at school should have their vision checked — undiagnosed myopia is frequently mistaken for inattention or learning difficulties. TCM is a complementary approach: it does not replace regular comprehensive eye exams, corrective lenses, or the medical management of myopia. Any sudden vision change requires prompt evaluation by an optometrist or ophthalmologist.
