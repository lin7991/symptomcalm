Eye Health After 60: A TCM Guide to Aging Vision

Symptom Guide · 8 min read

Around sixty, the eye stops changing one thing at a time. Reading has been getting harder for years — that part is familiar. But now there is also the glare from oncoming headlights that never used to bother you. Stairs are harder to read in dim light. The eye doctor says the pressure is "a little high, let's watch it," or that there are "early lens changes," or that the macula shows "a few drusen." Nothing is wrong exactly, and yet several things are clearly different.

This is the part of eye aging that rarely gets explained as a whole: not one condition, but a cluster of changes that arrive on the same schedule and interact with each other. Feeling unsteady in low light is not a vision problem in the usual sense — it is a balance problem that starts in the eye. Trouble driving at night is not the same as needing stronger reading glasses, but both come from the same decade.

Because these changes arrive as separate appointments with separate names, it is easy to lose the thread. This article follows the whole thread. We will look at what measurably shifts after 60, why TCM reads those shifts as a story about reserves and supply lines rather than simple wear, and what everyday support looks like at this stage — including the safety pieces (falls, glare, night driving) that matter more after 60 than at any other age.

How this article fits with our other eye guides: we have separate deep dives on single conditions — presbyopia, cataracts, macular degeneration, glaucoma, and night vision. This guide takes the opposite view: not one condition, but the combined aging picture, and what changes about daily life after 60.

What Actually Changes After 60

Ophthalmology describes age-related eye change as a set of functional shifts. Knowing them by name is useful, because they explain symptoms that otherwise feel random.

  • The tear film thins and changes quality. Tear production tends to fall with age, and the oil glands along the eyelid margin (meibomian glands) commonly become blocked or less productive. The result is dryness, burning, and a paradox familiar to many older adults: eyes that water constantly because they are dry, as the lacrimal system overreacts.
  • The lens stiffens and yellows. Stiffening is what makes near focus harder; yellowing shifts colour perception and reduces how much blue light reaches the retina, which affects contrast in dim conditions.
  • The pupil gets smaller and slower. A smaller resting pupil lets in less light, and a slower reflex means it adapts more gradually when you move between bright and dark. This is a large part of why night vision feels different.
  • Dark adaptation slows. Rod cells need longer to regenerate their light-sensitive pigment. Walking from a lit room into a dark street, the eye takes noticeably longer to catch up than it did at forty.
  • Contrast sensitivity falls faster than sharpness. This is the most under-discussed change of all. You may still read the 20/20 line — and still struggle to see a step edge, a curb, or a dark object against a dark floor. Contrast sensitivity, not visual acuity, is the measure most closely tied to falls.
  • The vitreous changes. The gel inside the eye liquefies and shrinks, producing floaters, and raising the chance of a posterior vitreous detachment — usually harmless, occasionally the first sign of a retinal tear.

Read that list again and one thing stands out: almost none of it is about the eyeball wearing out. It is about supply — moisture, light, adaptation speed, contrast. The eye is not broken. The systems that keep it supplied are slowing down.

Why TCM Reads the Aging Eye as a Reserve Story

TCM happens to frame age-related change in almost exactly those terms, which is why the two views sit together unusually well here.

In TCM theory, the eyes are described as the opening of the Liver, nourished by Blood that rises upward — and the sharpness of vision is associated with Kidney Jing, the deep constitutional reserve you are born with. Jing has a particular quality worth understanding: it is a fixed account. It is not replenished by a good meal the way Qi is. It declines on a long curve, and the traditional emphasis is on how it is spent rather than on topping it up.

Think of yourself as the top-floor apartment in a building you have lived in for sixty years. Three things decide whether the view stays clear. How much reserve the building holds (Kidney Jing). How reliably water reaches the top floor (Liver Blood). And how well the boiler converts fuel into heat (Spleen Qi). Aging does not break your window — it changes the supply lines behind it. That is why, in TCM theory, the same few lifestyle levers show up again and again for older eyes: sleep, nourishment, and steady demand rather than bursts.

For the full framework behind that idea, see our guide to the Kidney in TCM and the Liver in TCM. This article stays on what the framework means in practice after 60.

Three Systems That Keep an Older Eye Working

Rather than sorting you into a type, it is more useful to look at the three supply systems separately — because in most people over 60, more than one is under some strain, and each responds to a different lever.

1. Reserve — Kidney Jing (the endowment fund)

What it governs: the sharpness and structural integrity of vision over the long arc of life, plus dark adaptation and the eye's ability to recover from strain.

Signs it is the loudest voice: gradual decline over years rather than months; poorer night vision; floaters or ringing in the ears alongside visual changes; lower back ache, weak knees, feeling colder than you used to; fatigue that sleep does not fully clear.

What traditionally supports it: sleep before midnight (the single most emphasised lever), adequate protein and cooked food rather than raw and cold, black beans, walnuts, black sesame, bone broths, gentle daily movement, and avoiding the habit of spending your last reserve on stimulants to get through the afternoon.

2. Delivery — Liver Blood (the fleet that reaches the top floor)

What it governs: moisture and nourishment arriving at the eye tissue. In TCM theory, the Liver stores Blood and sends it upward to the eyes.

Signs it is the loudest voice: dry, gritty, tired eyes that worsen through the day and improve with rest or sleep; vision that fluctuates (sharper on a good day, blurred when tired); brittle nails, pale complexion, light or scanty periods if still menstruating; difficulty falling asleep; muscle cramps or twitching.

What traditionally supports it: goji berries, cooked dark leafy greens, eggs, dark red or black foods (beets, black beans, mulberries), animal liver occasionally if appropriate, and — importantly — sleep, because Blood is traditionally understood to be restored during deep rest. Screens late in the evening spend this supply rather than rebuild it.

3. Conversion — Spleen Qi (the boiler)

What it governs: turning food into fuel and Blood. When conversion is weak, the delivery fleet has less to carry — so Liver Blood deficiency can start as a digestion problem.

Signs it is the loudest voice: eye fatigue that appears after meals; bloating, loose stools, or a heavy feeling after eating; appetite that is unreliable; bruising easily; feeling cold in the limbs; muscle weakness.

What traditionally supports it: warm cooked meals at regular times, soups, porridges, steamed vegetables, ginger, small frequent portions rather than one large evening meal, and reducing raw salads, iced drinks, and grazing on cold snacks through the day.

A Self-Check: Which Supply Line Is Asking for Attention

Most people over 60 will recognise themselves in more than one of the columns below. That is expected — start with the one that is loudest, because that is where a change in habits shows up fastest.

  • Decline over years, worse night vision, lower back ache, feeling cold: reserve-leaning — prioritise sleep, protein, warmth, and pacing.
  • Dry, gritty eyes that worsen by evening; fluctuating vision; brittle nails: delivery-leaning — prioritise sleep, Blood-nourishing foods, hydration, and fewer late screens.
  • Eye tiredness after meals; bloating; loose stools; cold hands and feet: conversion-leaning — prioritise warm cooked meals at set times and smaller evening portions.
  • Red, irritated eyes; headaches; irritability; worsening with stress: this pattern deserves its own articles — see red eyes in TCM and eye strain.
  • Any sudden change — flashes, a curtain across the vision, sudden loss or double vision — is not a pattern question. See the red flags section below and seek care the same day.

Everyday Support for an Aging Eye

The following are the levers that matter most at this stage. None of them are exotic; the difficulty is consistency, not information.

Food

  • Eat for the retina as well as for the body. Lutein and zeaxanthin are the two carotenoids that concentrate in the macula. Cooked dark leafy greens and egg yolks are the most absorbable everyday sources; cooking greens with a little fat improves uptake.
  • Get enough protein. Older adults commonly eat less protein than they need, which affects tissue repair, muscle strength, and the traditional "fuel" supply at the same time.
  • Fatty fish two or three times a week if you eat fish — a practical source of omega-3 fats, which are relevant to tear film quality.
  • Respect the thirst signal you no longer have. Thirst declines with age, and so does the sensation of dry mouth. Keeping a water bottle in view and drinking at set times is more reliable than waiting to feel thirsty — dryness in the eyes usually mirrors hydration overall.
  • Go easy on the evening meal. A large late dinner is the most common digestive load after 60 and the least well handled.

Light and screen habits

  • Light the room, not just the page. After 60, the eye needs more light to achieve the same contrast. A brighter, evenly lit room with the light coming from behind you is the single easiest change.
  • Cut glare rather than adding brightness. Matt surfaces, shades on lamps, and anti-reflective coatings on glasses all help, because glare hits an aging eye harder than dim light does.
  • Take real screen breaks. For older eyes, looking up and focusing at distance is not just fatigue management — it is the eye practising the flexibility the lens is losing.
  • Check your glasses prescription for the task. After 60, one pair rarely serves reading, screen, intermediate, and outdoor distance equally. A single-lens pair for walking outdoors is worth discussing with your optometrist — see the safety section below.
  • Wear sunglasses outdoors, year round. UV exposure accumulates across a lifetime, and lens changes are among its documented consequences.

Sleep and demand

  • Earlier, more regular nights. In TCM theory, deep sleep is when Blood is restored and the Liver channel is at its most receptive — which is why eye dryness after a poor night is such a consistent observation.
  • Pace rather than push. Bursts of intense visual work followed by long recovery is the pattern that most drains an aging system. Shorter sessions with genuine breaks hold up better than heroic ones.
  • Move every day, gently. Walking, tai chi, or qigong support circulation, balance, and mood. Tai chi in particular has been studied for balance and fall reduction in older adults — which is directly relevant to the eyes, as you will see next.

Safety First: Falls, Night Driving, and Glare

This section is the one that most distinguishes eye care after 60 from eye care at any other age. The issue is not reading small print — it is moving safely through the world.

  • Contrast sensitivity, not sharpness, predicts falls. Research has found that contrast sensitivity — the ability to distinguish an object from its background — is more strongly associated with falling in older adults than standard visual acuity. A person who reads 20/20 can still miss a step edge in poor light.
  • Multifocal lenses and stairs outdoors. A well-known finding is that older adults wearing multifocal (progressive or bifocal) glasses had a higher rate of falls outdoors than those wearing single-lens distance glasses, because the lower part of the lens blurs the ground. If you wear progressives and walk outdoors often, ask about a single-lens pair for walking.
  • Light your home for edges, not for brightness. Well-lit stairs, a night light on the route to the bathroom, and high-contrast marking on the first and last step address the exact thing aging eyes struggle with most.
  • Night driving needs an honest assessment. Glare, halos around lights, and slower dark adaptation make night driving genuinely harder after 60 — and cataract changes worsen all three. If you find yourself avoiding night driving, that is useful information rather than a personal failure, and it is worth raising at your next eye appointment.
  • Know that some eye conditions are silent. Glaucoma typically has no symptoms until field loss is advanced, and early diabetic changes are also silent. This is why routine pressure checks, dilated examinations, and retinal screening at the intervals your clinician recommends are not optional extras — they are the only way these are found early.
  • Check medications for dryness. Many common prescriptions — including some antihistamines, antidepressants, and bladder medications — reduce tear production. If your eyes became noticeably drier after a medication change, mention the timing.
  • Get bleeding or sudden floaters checked the same day. A sudden shower of floaters, flashes of light, or a curtain across part of the vision can signal a retinal tear or detachment and is time-sensitive.

Acupressure for Aging Eyes

Gentle self-pressure is a reasonable daily habit at any age. Use small circles or steady pressure for about one minute per point, both sides, and keep the pressure light — the eye area is delicate, and nothing should ever be pressed onto the eyeball itself.

  • BL-1 (Jingming) — in the hollow at the inner corner of the eye, just above where the eyelid meets the nose. Press gently against the bone, never on the eye.
  • GB-20 (Fengchi) — in the two hollows at the base of the skull between the neck muscles. Often tender with neck tension, which is common in older adults who read or use screens with the head forward.
  • GB-37 (Guangming) — about five finger-widths above the outer ankle bone, on the back edge of the fibula. The point's name translates as "bright light," and it is the most frequently cited point in eye-related research.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — about four finger-widths below the kneecap, just outside the shin bone. Traditionally used to support digestion and energy — the conversion system in practice.
  • LV-3 (Taichong) — on the top of the foot, in the hollow between the first and second toes, about two finger-widths back.
  • KI-3 (Taixi) — in the depression between the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon. Traditionally used where visual decline sits alongside fatigue or lower back ache.

If you have not used these points before, one minute each on the hand and lower-leg points is a gentle way to start, with the eye-area points added once the locations feel familiar.

What Research Suggests

  • Lutein, zeaxanthin, and the AREDS2 trial. The large AREDS2 randomised trial tested a supplement combining lutein, zeaxanthin, omega-3 fats, zinc, and vitamins C and E in people with age-related macular degeneration. Lutein and zeaxanthin were substituted for beta-carotene in the formulation because beta-carotene was associated with an increased risk of lung cancer in participants who smoked or had smoked — a detail worth knowing, since many older adults are former smokers. AREDS-type supplementation is specific to certain stages of macular degeneration identified by an eye professional, not a general-purpose eye vitamin.
  • Contrast sensitivity and falls. Studies in older adults have found contrast sensitivity to be more strongly associated with falls than visual acuity, supporting the practical emphasis on lighting, edge contrast, and home safety.
  • Multifocal spectacle lenses and outdoor falls. A randomised study compared multifocal and single-lens distance glasses in older adults and reported a higher rate of falls outdoors among multifocal wearers, along with better performance on some visually demanding tasks. It is a reminder that the "best" optical solution depends on the activity.
  • Dry eye becomes more common with age. Prevalence studies consistently show dry eye disease rising in older adults, with meibomian gland dysfunction — blockage of the oil glands along the eyelid — a major contributor. This pairs with evidence that omega-3 intake and lid hygiene practices are commonly recommended, though results across trials have been mixed.
  • Acupuncture for dry eye. Several systematic reviews have examined acupuncture for dry eye symptoms in adults, reporting improvements in symptom scores and some tear film measures, while noting that study quality varies and larger well-designed trials are needed. Findings are promising but should be read as preliminary.
  • Tai chi for balance and falls. Large randomised trials have found tai chi programs associated with fewer falls in older adults compared with stretching or conventional balance exercise. Since vision and balance interact directly, this is a relevant companion practice for anyone whose eye changes are affecting confidence on their feet.
  • Dark adaptation and nutrition. Age-related slowing of dark adaptation is well documented, and rod function depends on vitamin A. This supports attention to overall nutritional status in older adults rather than any single supplement; high-dose vitamin A supplementation is not advisable without medical guidance.

Practical Takeaways

  • Think supply, not wear. Most of what changes after 60 is about moisture, light, adaptation, and contrast reaching the eye — not the eye wearing out.
  • Name your loudest supply line. Reserve (decline over years, poor night vision, fatigue), delivery (dry gritty eyes, fluctuating vision), or conversion (eye tiredness after meals, bloating). Start where the signal is loudest.
  • Sleep before midnight and eat warm, regular, protein-sufficient meals. These two levers show up for all three systems, which is why they come first.
  • Light for contrast, not for brightness. Edge lighting on stairs, a night light along the bathroom route, and glare reduction do more for safety than a brighter bulb overhead.
  • Ask about a single-lens pair for walking outdoors if you wear progressives, and factor glare and dark adaptation honestly into night driving.
  • Keep every scheduled eye appointment. Glaucoma and early diabetic change are silent — routine pressure checks, dilated exams, and retinal screening at the recommended interval are the only way they are caught early.
  • Same-day care for sudden change. A shower of floaters, flashes, or a curtain across the vision is time-sensitive.

When to See a Doctor

Age-related eye change is usually gradual and manageable. Some symptoms are not gradual at all. Seek care without delay if you notice any of the following:

  • Sudden loss of vision in one or both eyes — seek emergency care
  • A sudden shower of floaters, flashes of light, or a curtain or shadow across part of your vision — possible retinal tear or detachment, needs same-day assessment
  • Sudden double vision, or vision change with weakness, slurred speech, facial droop, or a severe headache — seek emergency care
  • Eye pain, redness, or halos around lights with nausea — may indicate acute raised pressure in the eye
  • Gradual loss of peripheral vision, or tunnel vision — glaucoma is usually painless and silent until it is advanced
  • Distortion of straight lines or a new blank or blurred spot in central vision — warrants prompt macular assessment
  • A noticeable step down in vision over days to weeks, not years
  • Any new eye symptom if you have diabetes, plus keeping your recommended retinal screening schedule
  • Falls, near-falls, or new avoidance of driving at night — worth raising at an eye appointment and with your doctor, because it may signal more than vision alone
  • An eye that becomes persistently red, painful, or light-sensitive, or discharge that does not settle

TCM approaches — including acupressure, dietary patterns, and rest — are best understood as support alongside regular eye care, not as a substitute for it. After 60, the value of a routine eye examination is that it finds the quiet problems before they announce themselves.
