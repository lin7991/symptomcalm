Watery Eyes and Wind-Cold: A TCM Look at Excess Tearing

Symptom Guide · 6 min read

You step outside on a cold, gusty morning and within a minute your eyes are streaming. Not because you are sad, not because you are tired — just tears, pouring down your cheeks until you have to wipe them away with your sleeve. By the time you reach the car, your vision is blurry with moisture and your eyes feel raw.

If this sounds familiar, you have probably asked yourself the obvious questions. Allergies? Maybe — but it happens in the middle of winter. Dry eye? Strange — your eyes are anything but dry. The truth is that excess tearing, or watery eyes, sits in an awkward middle ground: too much moisture on the surface, yet often tied to the same instability that drives dryness. TCM has a specific way of reading this story — and it starts with Wind and Cold.

Before we dive in: This article focuses on excess tearing — eyes that produce too many tears, especially in cold or windy conditions. If your eyes itch and water seasonally, our guide to eye allergies covers that territory. For the broader framework of Wind and Cold as external influences, see our TCM Basics article on the Six Pathogens. And because the eyes are the window of the Liver in TCM, our guide to the Liver is useful background.

How TCM Sees Excess Tearing

In TCM, the eyes are the opening of the Liver — the organ system that stores Blood, smooths the flow of Qi, and governs vision. Tears are classically understood as a fluid of the Liver: when Liver Blood and Liver Yin are healthy, the eye surface stays moist, stable, and comfortable. When something disturbs that balance, the fluid system of the eye responds — sometimes by drying out, and sometimes by flooding.

The classic trigger for flooding is Wind. In TCM, Wind is the "shape-shifter" among external influences: it arrives suddenly, it moves, and it changes location. That is exactly how watery eyes behave — fine indoors, streaming the moment the wind hits your face. When Wind carries Cold with it, the picture sharpens. Cold has a contracting quality in TCM: it tightens channels and slows movement. Picture trying to wash a window with a hose that is partially kinked — the water is still flowing, but the pressure is wrong and nothing rinses cleanly. Cold, in the same way, constricts the channels around the eyes: the tears are produced, but the smooth drainage and circulation that should keep the surface calm are tightened and inefficient.

There is also a defensive layer to the story. TCM describes a protective energy, Wei Qi, that patrols the body's surface like a security perimeter. The eyes are among the most exposed points on that perimeter — wide open, constantly in contact with the outside air. When Wind-Cold knocks on the door, one possible response is an alarm: a sudden flood of tears, the body's attempt to wash away the intruder and protect the delicate surface.

Three Patterns Behind Watery Eyes

Not all watery eyes are the same. TCM distinguishes three common patterns, each with its own signature and its own support strategy.

Pattern 1: Wind-Cold Invasion

Signature: Tearing that flares suddenly in cold or windy weather, usually with clear, thin tears. It may come with a clear runny nose, sneezing, mild chills, or a sense that your face is tight and cold. Indoors, away from the wind, the tearing settles.

What is happening: Wei Qi has met an external invader at the most exposed surface — the eyes — and is responding with a flood. The focus here is supporting the body's defense and warming the surface so the Cold can disperse.

Pattern 2: Liver-Kidney Yin Deficiency with Internal Wind

Signature: Chronic, often in older adults, or in people who have spent years in front of screens or under heavy stress. Tears appear when you are tired, in bright light, or at night — yet at other times the same eyes may feel dry and gritty. Dizziness, ringing in the ears, a dry throat, restless sleep, and achy lower back often accompany it.

What is happening: Yin is the cooling, moistening aspect of the body. As Liver and Kidney Yin decline, the moisture system of the eye becomes unstable — it swings between too dry and too wet, overcorrecting in both directions. This is the paradox of reflex tearing: a dry, unstable surface that the body tries to rescue with a flood.

Pattern 3: Spleen Qi Deficiency with Dampness

Signature: Tears that feel thick, sticky, or excessive after meals or in humid weather, often with puffy eyelids, a heavy feeling in the head, bloating, loose stools, and brain fog. The eyes may look slightly swollen, as if holding fluid.

What is happening: The Spleen governs the transformation and transport of fluids in TCM. When Spleen Qi weakens, fluids are not processed properly — they pool where they should flow. Dampness accumulates, and the eye area, as a fluid-rich surface, shows the spillover.

Acupressure Points for Watery Eyes

Gentle acupressure is a traditional self-care approach that may support the patterns above. Use light to moderate pressure in small circles for one to two minutes per point, both sides, once or twice a day.

  • BL-2 (Zanzhu) — at the inner end of the eyebrow. A local point for eye comfort and a traditional choice when Wind affects the face.
  • GB-20 (Fengchi) — in the hollows at the base of the skull. One of the best-known points for releasing Wind and easing eye, head, and neck tension.
  • LI-4 (Hegu) — on the back of the hand, between thumb and index finger. A classic point for the face region and for dispersing Wind. (Traditionally avoided during pregnancy.)
  • LV-3 (Taichong) — on the top of the foot, between the first and second toes. Supports the Liver and smooths Qi flow — helpful for the Yin-deficiency-with-Wind pattern.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — below the knee, about four finger-widths below the kneecap. A general point for Spleen and digestive energy, relevant to the Dampness pattern.

Diet and Lifestyle Support

Traditional approaches tailor daily habits to the pattern:

  • For Wind-Cold: warm foods and drinks — ginger tea, warm broths, cooked oats. Wear a scarf or glasses as a wind barrier on cold days, and let a warm hand or compress rest over closed eyes when you come indoors.
  • For Liver-Kidney Yin Deficiency: nourishing, moistening foods — black sesame, walnuts, cooked pear, bone broth. Protect sleep: Yin is restored during deep rest, and screens late at night draw on the same reserve.
  • For Spleen Dampness: warm cooked meals, congee, and regular meal times; reduce cold drinks, raw salads, and excess dairy. Gentle daily movement helps move fluids.
  • For everyone: a warm compress over closed eyes for five to ten minutes can support the tear film, and staying gently hydrated supports the whole fluid system.

What Research Says

Modern eye research offers interesting parallels to these traditional observations, though more high-quality studies are needed:

  • The Tear Film & Ocular Surface Society's international workshop report (DEWS II, 2017) describes reflex tearing — eyes that water excessively can be a response to an unstable or irritated tear film. This parallels the TCM idea of a moisture system that has lost its balance.
  • Environmental studies suggest that wind and low humidity increase evaporation from the eye surface and reduce tear film stability — consistent with tearing that flares in cold, windy conditions.
  • Warm compresses are among the most-studied non-drug approaches for meibomian gland function and tear film stability, supporting their traditional use for eye comfort.
  • Epiphora (excess tearing) becomes more common with age, often linked to narrowing of the tear drainage system — consistent with the TCM pattern of Liver-Kidney Yin decline over the years.

When to See a Doctor

See a doctor or eye care professional if watery eyes come with any of the following:

  • Eye pain, significant redness, or any change in vision
  • Yellow or green discharge, or crusting that suggests infection
  • Tearing that begins right after an injury to the eye or face
  • Swelling of the eyelid or a tender lump near the inner corner of the eye
  • Persistent tearing that lasts for weeks with no clear trigger

Practical Takeaways:

  • Match the pattern: cold-wind trigger points to Wind-Cold; chronic tearing with dry moments points to Yin support; thick tears with puffiness point to the Spleen.
  • Use BL-2 and GB-20 daily — local and Wind-releasing points that are easy to find.
  • Apply a warm compress over closed eyes for five to ten minutes as a simple evening ritual.
  • Protect your eyes from wind with glasses or a scarf on cold days.
  • See a doctor promptly for pain, discharge, injury, or vision changes.
