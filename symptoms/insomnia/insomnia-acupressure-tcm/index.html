Insomnia Acupressure: 4 TCM Patterns & the Pressure Points That Match Each One

Symptom Guide · 9 min read

You're in bed. Your body is exhausted — every muscle feels heavy. But the second your head hits the pillow, your brain wakes up. You replay conversations, plan tomorrow, worry about things you can't control. The clock ticks: 1 AM, 2 AM, 3 AM.

If you've tried general sleep tips without success, there may be a reason: not all insomnia is the same. In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), insomnia is divided into distinct patterns — each with a different cause and a different acupressure solution.

This isn't about pressing random points and hoping for the best. It's about finding your pattern and using the right points for your type of sleep disruption.

How this article differs from general acupressure guides: Instead of a one-size-fits-all list of "5 points for better sleep," this guide is organized by insomnia pattern. Read through each pattern below — the one that sounds like you is likely the one that needs acupressure attention first. For a broader overview of acupressure basics, see our general guide: Acupressure for Better Sleep.

The TCM View: Why Your Insomnia Has a Pattern

In TCM, sleep depends on the Shen (mind-spirit) being able to settle. Think of the Shen as a bird that needs a sturdy branch to rest on at night. When the branch is shaky — whether from accumulated stress, depleted energy, internal heat, or digestive weakness — the bird cannot settle, and sleep is disturbed.

TCM identifies four primary insomnia patterns that you can recognize from your own experience. Each pattern responds best to specific acupressure points.

Pattern 1: Heart-Shen Disturbance — The Racing Mind

You might have this pattern if…

  • You lie in bed with a racing mind — replaying conversations, planning tomorrow, worrying about things you cannot change
  • You feel anxious, restless, or your heart pounds when you try to settle down
  • Your sleep is light and easily interrupted — any noise wakes you
  • You may also experience heart palpitations or a feeling of unease in your chest

In TCM, this pattern reflects the Shen (mind-spirit) failing to anchor because the Heart's housing for the Shen is compromised. When Heart Blood is insufficient or Heart Qi is disturbed, the Shen floats — and so does your ability to sleep.

Key Point: HT7 — Shenmen (神门, Spirit Gate)

Location: On the inner wrist crease, in line with the pinky finger. When you bend your wrist slightly, you'll feel a small hollow just below the pisiform bone (the small round bone at the base of your palm on the pinky side).

Technique: Press with your thumb pad into the hollow. Hold steady, firm pressure for 2-3 minutes per wrist. You should feel a mild, deep ache — that confirms you're on the right spot. Breathe slowly while you press.

TCM rationale: HT7 is the most famous sleep point in all of TCM. It is believed to calm the Shen and anchor the mind. For racing-thought insomnia, this is often the single most effective point.

Supporting Point: PC6 — Neiguan (内关, Inner Gate)

Location: Two finger-widths above the inner wrist crease, between the two prominent tendons. Make a loose fist and you'll see the tendons pop up — PC6 sits between them.

Technique: Press between the tendons for 2 minutes per arm. A circular massaging motion also works well here.

TCM rationale: PC6 opens the chest, regulates Qi, and helps release the tightness and unease that keeps the Shen from settling.

Pattern 2: Liver Fire / Liver Qi Stagnation — The Irritable Insomniac

You might have this pattern if…

  • You wake up between 1-3 AM — like clockwork — and struggle to get back to sleep
  • You feel irritable, easily frustrated, or tense during the day
  • You grind your teeth or clench your jaw at night
  • You have headaches — especially on the sides of your head or behind your eyes
  • The week before your period is especially bad for sleep (if applicable)

In TCM, the Liver is responsible for the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When stress, suppressed frustration, or pent-up anger causes Liver Qi to stagnate, it can generate heat — and that heat rises upward at night, disturbing the Shen. The Liver hour (1-3 AM) is when this pattern tends to manifest most clearly.

Key Point: LV3 — Taichong (太冲, Great Surge)

Location: On the top of the foot, in the depression between the first and second toes, about one finger-width back from where the toes join.

Technique: Press firmly into the V-shaped hollow between the two bones. Hold for 2-3 minutes per foot. This point is often tender — especially during stressful periods — so start gently and increase pressure gradually.

TCM rationale: LV3 is the most important point for moving stagnant Liver Qi. It's considered a "sedating" point — it drains excess energy from the Liver meridian and allows the trapped heat to dissipate, calming the Shen indirectly.

Supporting Point: GB34 — Yanglingquan (阳陵泉, Tomb and Spring)

Location: On the outer side of the lower leg, in the depression just below the knee joint, on the outside of the fibula head.

Technique: Press deeply with your thumb for 2 minutes per leg. This point may feel tender if your Liver Qi is stagnant.

TCM rationale: GB34 is a key point for smoothing the flow of Liver Qi and relaxing the sinews. It supports LV3 by helping release the physical tension that accumulates from chronic stress.

Pattern 3: Heart-Kidney Imbalance — The Hot, Restless Sleeper

You might have this pattern if…

  • You have trouble falling asleep and staying asleep — a double insomnia
  • You feel hot at night — your feet get warm, your palms feel hot, or you wake up sweating
  • You have a dry mouth or throat during the night
  • You're over 40, dealing with chronic stress, or experiencing signs of hormonal change
  • You feel restless — like your body wants to move even when you're exhausted

This pattern is one of the most common in modern life. The Kidney stores Yin (the body's cooling, moistening substance). When Yin is depleted — from aging, chronic stress, overwork, or late nights — the Heart Fire has nothing to balance it. The result: the Heart "floats" with excess heat, and sleep becomes elusive.

Key Point: KI3 — Taixi (太溪, Great Stream)

Location: On the inner ankle, midway between the tip of the medial malleolus (the ankle bone) and the Achilles tendon. It's in a hollow that you can feel when you gently press into the space.

Technique: Press firmly into the hollow with your thumb. Hold for 2-3 minutes per ankle. This point can feel quite deep — it's nestled between bone and tendon.

TCM rationale: KI3 is the source point of the Kidney meridian. It nourishes Kidney Yin, which in turn cools Heart Fire and restores the natural downward flow of Qi that allows sleep.

Supporting Point: HT7 — Shenmen (see Pattern 1)

When Heart-Kidney imbalance is present, combining KI3 (nourish Kidney Yin) with HT7 (calm the Shen) creates a complementary one-two punch. Press KI3 first, then HT7 — the sequence matters.

Pattern 4: Spleen Qi Deficiency / Overthinking — The Exhausted But Wired

You might have this pattern if…

  • You fall asleep easily but wake up repeatedly through the night
  • Your mind loops on the same thoughts — you're mentally tired but can't stop
  • You have poor digestion: bloating, loose stools, or feel heavy after eating
  • You feel mentally foggy during the day and crave sweets or carbs
  • You wake up feeling unrefreshed even after 7-8 hours in bed

In TCM, the Spleen governs digestion and transforms food into Qi and Blood — the raw materials that nourish the Heart and Shen. When the Spleen is weak (common from irregular meals, excessive worry, or a high-sugar diet), it cannot produce enough Blood to anchor the Shen. The mind has nowhere to rest, so it wanders — looping on the same thoughts.

Key Point: ST36 — Zusanli (足三里, Leg Three Miles)

Location: On the outer side of the lower leg, about four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width away from the shinbone toward the outside. Flex your ankle and you'll feel a muscle bulge — it's right there.

Technique: Press deeply with your thumb or use the knuckle of your index finger for a firmer press. Hold for 2-3 minutes per leg. A mild ache or sensation traveling down the leg is normal and positive.

TCM rationale: ST36 is a major point for building Qi and Blood. In TCM, it strengthens the Spleen and Stomach so they can produce enough energy to nourish the Heart and stabilize the Shen.

Supporting Point: SP6 — Sanyinjiao (三阴交, Three Yin Intersection)

Location: On the inner lower leg, about four finger-widths above the tip of the inner ankle bone, just behind the shinbone.

Technique: Press firmly for 2 minutes per leg. This point is known for being sensitive.

⚠️ Pregnancy caution: SP6 is traditionally considered contraindicated during pregnancy. If you are or may be pregnant, skip this point.

TCM rationale: SP6 is where the Spleen, Liver, and Kidney meridians intersect. It nourishes Blood, calms the Shen, and supports the digestive system — making it an excellent companion to ST36 for this insomnia pattern.

Daily Acupressure Sequence

Pick one pattern that sounds most like you. Do the key point + supporting point for that pattern, 2-3 minutes per point, on both sides. Do this once in the evening (ideally right before bed) and optionally once more during the day if you can.

Quick-reference by sleep problem:
• Racing thoughts at bedtime → Pattern 1 (HT7 + PC6)
• Waking at 1-3 AM → Pattern 2 (LV3 + GB34)
• Hot, sweaty, restless sleep → Pattern 3 (KI3 + HT7)
• Wake repeatedly, foggy digestion → Pattern 4 (ST36 + SP6)

What the Research Says

Modern research has begun investigating the effects of acupressure on sleep quality, with promising but still emerging results.

A 2020 meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews (10 RCTs, 685 participants) found that acupressure was associated with significant improvements in sleep quality, particularly in adults over 40. The analysis noted that the effects were most consistent when acupressure was applied at points traditionally used for calming the Shen (such as HT7).

A 2016 systematic review in Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine examined 10 studies on acupressure for insomnia specifically. Seven of the 10 studies reported that acupressure improved sleep quality compared to control groups, though the authors noted variability in study design and point selection.

Some studies suggest that the mechanism may involve modulation of the autonomic nervous system — reducing sympathetic (fight-or-flight) activity and promoting parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) tone. PC6 and HT7 are among the most studied points for this effect.

It's important to note that while these findings are encouraging, acupressure is best understood as a complementary self-care tool — not a replacement for medical evaluation of chronic insomnia.

When to See a Doctor

Danger signals: If your insomnia has persisted for more than 4 weeks, or if it's accompanied by chest pain, shortness of breath, severe depression, sudden weight changes, or thoughts of self-harm — please see a medical provider. Acupressure is a gentle self-care technique that may support better sleep, but it should not delay diagnosis of underlying medical conditions.

Also consult a doctor before using acupressure if you are pregnant, have a bleeding disorder, are taking blood-thinning medication, or have varicose veins or skin conditions at the acupressure sites.

Practical Takeaways

Getting started with acupressure for insomnia:
  1. Identify your pattern — use the pattern descriptions above to find the one that matches your sleep disruption style
  2. Start with the key point — 2-3 minutes per side, steady pressure, before bed
  3. Add the supporting point after 2-3 nights if needed
  4. Be consistent — results build over weeks, not overnight. It's like unwinding a knotted rope: each session releases one twist
  5. Combine with good sleep hygiene — acupressure works best as part of a sleep-supportive routine (consistent bedtime, no screens 30 min before bed, cool room)
  6. Consider the broader picture — explore our full Insomnia guide for more TCM-based sleep strategies

Acupressure puts the control back in your hands — literally. By matching the right points to your specific insomnia pattern, you're not just treating a symptom; you're working with your body's logic to create conditions where sleep can happen naturally.
