Sulfite Sensitivity: A TCM View of Wine Flares and Reactions

Symptom Guide · 9 min read

You poured one glass of wine. Twenty minutes later your face is hot, your temples are throbbing, and your chest feels a little tight. You turn the bottle around: Contains sulfites. Mystery solved — so you switch to a "sulfite-free" bottle, and the same thing happens again.

That second glass is the most useful clue you have. Sulfites are real, and a small number of people genuinely react to them. But the same sentence sits on almost every bottle on the shelf — red, white, cheap, expensive — which means it cannot be the thing that separates the wine that hurts you from the wine that doesn't.

The reason is that a sulfite reaction is usually not an allergy at all. It is a load story: an added preservative arriving in a total daily dose, meeting a clearing system that is already busy with something else. Because it works that way, the Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) lens is unusually good at describing it — not because TCM has a word for sulfite, but because TCM is very good at describing what happens when a load arrives and the exits are crowded.

Before we dive in: this article focuses on sulfites specifically — an added preservative, which makes the biology different from other food reactions. For plant-made compounds, see Salicylate Sensitivity. For the amine side of reactivity, see Histamine Intolerance. For the protein-based immune-memory picture, see Food Allergies and the Spleen-Damp Connection. And for the body's alarm threshold itself, see Mast Cell Activation.
The honest version up front: sulfite sensitivity is real but uncommon, and it is concentrated heavily in people who already have asthma. If you have asthma and notice tightness, wheeze, or a new cough after wine, dried fruit, or restaurant food, that is a medical conversation — not a diet experiment. Everything below is educational, and none of it replaces your inhaler, your doctor, or an emergency plan.

What Sulfites Actually Are — Added, Not Grown

Here is the single most useful thing to understand, and it is the thing most articles skip.

Some food reactions are to chemistry the plant made: salicylates are plant hormones, a plant's own signalling molecules. You are eating the plant's message.

Sulfites are not that. Sulfites are chemistry that we added. They are preservatives and anti-browning agents, produced in factories and put into food at specific points in processing. If salicylates are the plant's own stress memo, sulfites are the bubble wrap the food industry put around it — you are not reacting to the grape, you are reacting to the packaging.

On a label, sulfites hide behind a family of names: sulfur dioxide, sodium sulfite, sodium bisulfite, sodium metabisulfite, potassium metabisulfite — or E220 to E228 in the EU numbering system.

They are added for three jobs:

  • Stopping browning — cut fruit, dried fruit, and pre-cut potatoes keep their colour.
  • Stopping microbial growth — wine and cider stay stable in the bottle instead of turning to vinegar.
  • Holding colour and "freshness" — packaged and processed foods look younger than they are.

Now the important consequence: because sulfites are added at processing stages rather than grown in food, your exposure is not really "a food." It is a processing layer that runs underneath many foods at once. Almost nobody counts it, and that is precisely why reactions feel random.

The Five Doors: Where Sulfites Enter Your Food

Sulfites do not arrive from one direction. They enter the food supply at five distinct points, and each door delivers a different dose.

The DoorTypical foodsWhy sulfites are there
1. The harvest and packing shedFresh grapes shipped long distances, cut fruit trays, peeled potatoesApplied as an anti-browning dip or a fungicide during shipping
2. The drying rackDried apricots, raisins, figs, dates, shredded coconut, dried mushroomsPreserves colour and stops spoilage in a warm drying environment — by far the highest concentrations in the food supply
3. The barrel and bottling lineWine, cider, dessert wines, some beersAntimicrobial and antioxidant — keeps the wine stable and protects it from oxidation
4. The packaging plantPre-shredded or pre-cut potatoes, packaged fries, pickles, condiments, vinegar, sauces, dried soup mixesUsed as a processing aid to hold texture, colour, and shelf life
5. The restaurant lineSalad bars, pre-cut fruit platters, pre-prepared fries, batch soupsKeeps prepped food looking fresh through a long service day — and it is almost never labelled on a menu

Door 5 is why so many people describe their sensitivity as unpredictable. A restaurant meal can carry a load you never had a chance to see or read. Your own kitchen is a much quieter environment than you think — which is why the same dish often behaves completely differently at home.

The regulatory clue that helps you: in the United States, sulfites must be declared on the label once they reach 10 parts per million (ppm) as total sulfur dioxide. Below that, no declaration is required. Which means the label tells you whether you crossed a legal threshold, not how much you are getting — and a "Contains sulfites" statement on a 20 ppm wine and a 250 ppm wine reads exactly the same.

Why a Sulfite Reaction Isn't (Usually) an Allergy

This is where the frustration usually starts. You react, you get tested, the tests come back clean, and everyone — including you — starts to doubt the whole thing.

That is expected, because in most sulfite reactions the immune system's memory arm is not the driver. Instead, three different things can be happening at once:

  • An irritant reflex. When sulfites meet acid in the mouth and stomach, sulfur dioxide gas is released. That gas is inhaled in tiny amounts and lands on irritant receptors in the airway. In people with reactive airways, this can flip a switch that tells the airway to narrow — a reflex, not tissue damage.
  • A genuine immune response, in a minority. Some people do mount a true antibody-type reaction to sulfites. This is the smaller group, and it is why sulfite sensitivity is not a single mechanism.
  • A clearing bottleneck. Your body breaks sulfite down with an enzyme called sulfite oxidase, which converts sulfite into harmless sulfate as the final step of processing sulfur-containing amino acids. How fast that exit runs is part of the story — and so is how much is queued at it.

Think of it as a drain rather than a poison. There is nothing wrong with water going down a drain. But if you keep pouring from five taps at once — wine at dinner, dried fruit at breakfast, packaged snacks in the afternoon, a restaurant lunch, plus an already-stressed system — the sink backs up long before the water itself is a problem. Reactions scale with dose and with the state of your exits. Not with the purity of any single food.

One thing not to do: do not read the enzyme paragraph above and conclude you have a rare metabolic disorder. Severe sulfite oxidase deficiency is a very serious, very rare genetic condition that presents in infancy — it is not the explanation for an adult who gets flushed after a glass of Malbec. The everyday version of this story is about load versus capacity, nothing more dramatic.

How TCM Reads an Added Preservative: The Clearing-System Bottleneck

Let us be precise about what a TCM perspective can and cannot do here. TCM has no concept of a sulfite molecule. It was never designed to identify one. What it does have is a very old, very detailed vocabulary for describing a sudden load arriving and a clearing system responding — and that is exactly the shape of this problem.

Read the reaction's signature and the vocabulary selects itself:

  • The reaction is sudden, moving, and changeable — a flush that comes and goes, an itch that wanders, tightness that builds and lifts. TCM's name for responses with that quality is Wind.
  • The reaction is often hot — a red face, hot ears, a pounding head, bloodshot eyes. TCM reads that as Heat, and Heat that rises toward the head.
  • The reaction is often constrictive — a narrow chest, a throat that feels tight, a cough that will not settle. TCM reads that as Lung Qi failing to descend: airflow that should drop downward smoothly is instead reversing upward.

Now, what makes a system vulnerable to a load in the first place? In TCM, the organ system whose job is keeping everything moving freely — clearing stagnation, smoothing the flow — is the Liver. Its function is often summarised as "free and easy movement" of Qi. When that movement is already restricted (stress, poor sleep, skipped meals, alcohol), pressure builds, and pressure that sits still turns into Heat. You can read more on this mechanism in our primers on the Liver in TCM and Qi Stagnation.

The analogy that makes it click: imagine five highways feeding into a single toll booth. Each individual road can be completely clear. The problem is never any one road — it is the queue that forms at the booth. This is why an added preservative can feel unbearable on Friday and invisible on Tuesday, and why the food that hurt you last week may be fine today.
Where the modern and the traditional descriptions actually meet: research has found that inhaled sulfur dioxide triggers cough and reflex narrowing of the airway by stimulating vagal nerve fibres — the response is blocked by anticholinergic medication, which points to a nerve reflex rather than a burn. TCM's description of "rebellious Qi" and "Lung Qi not descending" is describing the same architecture: a signal that flips a switch, and a switch that answers before any tissue is harmed. Two languages, one reflex.

Three Ways a Sulfite Load Shows Up

Not everyone reacts the same way, and the pattern you recognise matters more than the label on the bottle. Below are three pictures traditionally associated with this kind of reactivity. Note that these run through different territory from the salicylate picture, which tends to live in the sinuses, the gut's heat, and the skin — here the signatures are the Liver's rising heat, the Lung's failed descent, and the Spleen's processing backlog.

1. Liver Qi Stagnation Turning to Heat Rising — The Pressure Valve

You might have this if: you get the classic wine reaction. A throbbing headache in the temples or one side of the head, starting roughly 20 to 60 minutes after drinking. A hot, red face and warm ears. Bloodshot or gritty eyes. Restlessness, irritability, or a short fuse. It is worse when you drink on a stressful day, on poor sleep, on an empty stomach, or when you have had too much coffee and not enough food.

TCM view: Qi that should move freely gets stuck, and stuck Qi generates Heat, which rises to the head. The preservative did not create the pattern — it turned up the pressure on a valve that was already stiff. This is why the same glass behaves differently at the end of a calm weekend and at the end of a bad workday.

Traditionally used approaches: cooling, downward-moving, Qi-freeing foods rather than more heat and more stimulants; acupressure at the points listed below; eating before drinking rather than instead of eating; and counting alcohol itself as part of the load, because it is. If headaches follow reliably on alcohol, that is information worth discussing with a doctor.

2. Lung Qi Failing to Descend with Wind — The Narrowing Airway

You might have this if: your reaction happens in your breathing. Chest tightness, a dry ticklish cough, a wheeze or whistle on the exhale, a throat that feels narrowed or "closing a little." It shows up after wine, dried fruit, pickles, or restaurant food, and especially with cold air, exercise, or a respiratory infection alongside it. You may already have asthma, or a history of exercise-induced tightness.

TCM view: the Lung governs Qi descending — airflow should drop downward smoothly, like a well-hung curtain. Here an irritant arrives and the movement reverses instead, with Wind riding on top of it. That reversal is the TCM description of a spasm. It is also, mechanically, a nerve reflex — which is why it can feel disproportionate to how much you actually consumed.

Traditionally used approaches: supporting the Lung's descending function with warm fluids rather than iced drinks, steam, gentle long-exhale breathing, and the points listed below. Important: if you have asthma, this pattern is not a self-care matter. Diet and acupressure are complementary; they never replace your prescribed reliever or your action plan.

3. Spleen Dampness from the Processed-Food Load — The Processing Backlog

You might have this if: your reaction is digestive and heavy rather than hot. Cramping or urgency, loose or sticky stools, bloating, nausea, and a foggy head that arrives an hour or two after eating. Your symptoms seem to track with how much packaged, dried, preserved, or reconstituted food you have eaten across the whole day — not with one identifiable dish. There is no strong burning or bitterness.

TCM view: the Spleen performs best on simple, warm, freshly cooked food. A diet heavy in preserved, dried, packaged, and long-shelf-life food is heavy to transform — so residue accumulates as Dampness, and Dampness reads as heaviness and fog. Notice the contrast with the salicylate picture, which tends to run hot: there the gut signature is Damp-Heat. Here the signature is weight and sluggishness, and the traditional direction is warming and drying rather than cooling.

Traditionally used approaches: warm cooked meals, ginger or fennel with food, fewer dried and packaged foods, and the points listed below. If your reaction includes hives that move around, that is a Wind-on-the-skin picture rather than a Spleen one — our companion guide to salicylate reactions covers that territory in detail.

Wine, Dried Fruit, Packaged Food: Building Your Personal Trigger Map

Because sulfite load is spread across doors rather than concentrated in one villain, the useful exercise is not "which food is bad" — it is "what does my typical day add up to." Here is a rough map of where the heaviest loads sit.

A practical thought about the wine row: if you only ever react to red, and white does not bother you, that is a genuinely useful clue — and it points away from sulfites. Red wines generally carry more of a flavanol called quercetin, from the grape skins. A 2023 study proposed that a quercetin metabolite can slow the enzyme that clears alcohol's breakdown product, acetaldehyde, which would explain a red-wine headache with or without sulfites in the picture. It is a hypothesis rather than settled fact, but it fits the everyday observation that "sulfite-free" wine still causes trouble for a lot of people.

Practical Ways to Lower the Sulfite Load

Two principles first, because they change what you actually do.

First: find your threshold, not zero. Very few people need a sulfite-free life. Most people need their typical day to sit below a personal line. Chasing zero means giving up food you enjoy for no measurable gain.

Second: count the day, not the dish. If the mechanism is a queue at one exit, then four small sources matter more than one large one — and the day you react is usually the day several taps were open.

Where it is easy to lower the load without losing anything that matters:

  • Dried fruit. Switch sulfured apricots for unsulfured brown ones, and sulfured raisins for fresh grapes or unsulfured varieties. This is the single largest lever for most people, because dried fruit carries the highest concentrations in the food supply.
  • Wine. Smaller pours, more water alongside, always with food rather than on an empty stomach, and a lower-sulfite bottle when you can find one — while remembering that low-additive wines still contain some, and that quercetin and alcohol itself remain part of the picture.
  • Potatoes and fries. Whole potatoes instead of pre-cut and pre-shredded; cooking them at home removes a processing-aid load entirely.
  • Packaged sauces, pickles, and condiments. Fresh aromatics — garlic, ginger, herbs, lemon, vinegar you control — replace several small hidden doses with one predictable one.
  • Restaurant meals. A short, non-dramatic question works: "Do you use sulfites on the prepped potatoes or the cut fruit?" Kitchens usually know, and asking costs nothing.

And support the clearing side, which is the half almost everyone ignores:

  • Favour warm, cooked, simple meals on days you know a load is coming.
  • Eat before you drink, and keep water going alongside the wine.
  • Protect sleep — in TCM, sleep is the main window in which the Liver's flow is restored, and it is also when a lot of ordinary metabolic housekeeping happens.
  • Try two or three minutes of long-exhale breathing before a meal or a glass. A longer exhale engages the same vagal territory the reflex runs through, and it is a pleasant thing to do regardless of mechanism.
  • Keep a simple log for two weeks: what, how much, when, and how you felt twenty minutes later. Patterns in your own data beat any generic food list, including the table above.

A Simple Acupressure Set

Acupressure is the at-home cousin of acupuncture: firm, steady finger pressure on the same points, no needles. It is traditionally used as a supportive practice — something you do alongside the load-lowering steps above, not instead of medical care. What Research Suggests

Sulfite sensitivity has been studied for decades, mostly in people with asthma, and the findings are more modest than the internet suggests.

  • How common it is. A 2012 review of adverse reactions to sulfite additives reported that most studies find a prevalence of sulfite sensitivity of roughly 3% to 10% among people with asthma who ingest these additives. An earlier formal challenge study in an asthma clinic estimated a figure of about 3.9%. The consistent theme: real, but uncommon, and strongly concentrated in people with pre-existing airway reactivity — particularly those on steroid-dependent asthma care.
  • How it acts. Work going back to the 1960s established that inhaled sulfur dioxide is a potent trigger of reflex narrowing in the airway, and later research identified stimulation of vagal bronchopulmonary nerve fibres as the main driver — a cough and constriction reflex rather than tissue injury. Anticholinergic medication blocks much of the response in healthy adults and only part of it in people with asthma, which is one reason individual sensitivity varies so much.
  • The enzyme behind the exit. Sulfite oxidase is a molybdenum-dependent enzyme that converts sulfite into sulfate — the final step in breaking down sulfur-containing amino acids. Inherited deficiency of this enzyme system causes a severe, rare disorder of infancy and childhood. It is worth knowing about precisely so it is not confused with an ordinary adult reaction to a glass of wine.
  • The red-wine headache twist. A 2023 study from UC Davis proposed that quercetin, a flavanol concentrated in red grape skins, is metabolised into a compound that inhibits ALDH2 — the enzyme that clears acetaldehyde. The authors suggested this accumulation could explain red-wine headaches independently of sulfites. It remains a hypothesis awaiting human trials, but the practical implication is immediate: "sulfite-free" is not the same as "will not bother me."
  • The label threshold. US regulations require that sulfites be declared when they reach 10 ppm total sulfur dioxide, and set ceilings that reflect how much is actually allowed — roughly 2,000 ppm for certain dried fruits, around 350 ppm for wine, and about 50 ppm for processed potatoes.
What this means for you: the evidence supports a load-and-threshold model, not a poison model. Your reaction depends on how much arrived, how reactive your airways already were, and how busy your clearing system was that day. It also means the label is a threshold disclosure, not a dose — which is why two bottles carrying the same sentence can behave completely differently. TCM-informed habits here are supportive and complementary: they may help you lower the load and support the clearing side, and they are not a substitute for medical evaluation, especially where breathing is involved.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  • Track, don't guess. Two weeks of notes — what, how much, when, and how you felt twenty minutes later — will tell you more than any food list online.
  • Count the day, not the dish. Sulfite load adds up across five doors. The reacting day is usually the day several taps were open.
  • Know your pattern. Hot flush and head pain point to the Liver's rising Heat; tight chest and dry cough point to the Lung's failed descent; heaviness and fog point to the Spleen's backlog. Match the support to the pattern, not to the bottle.
  • Lower the load where it is free. Unsulfured dried fruit, whole potatoes, fresh aromatics, smaller pours with food and water.
  • If you have asthma, read any breathing change as a medical conversation — keep your reliever with you and review it with your doctor rather than adjusting anything yourself.
  • Support the clearing side. Warm cooked meals, sleep, hydration, and a few minutes of long-exhale breathing before a meal or a drink.

Which of the three pictures sounds most like you — the pressure valve, the narrowing airway, or the processing backlog? That answer is where a useful next step starts, and it is often the thing a food list can never tell you.

When to See a Doctor

Sulfite reactions are usually mild and dose-shaped, but they can occasionally be serious. Get emergency help immediately if you experience:

  • Difficulty breathing, wheezing that does not ease, or a sensation of the throat closing — this is a medical emergency, not something to wait out.
  • Swelling of the lips, tongue, throat, or face, or a voice that suddenly sounds different.
  • Dizziness, faintness, or a rapid drop in blood pressure, which can indicate anaphylaxis. Use any prescribed adrenaline auto-injector and call emergency services.
  • Chest pain, a racing or irregular heartbeat, or collapse.
  • Severe abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, or a widespread rash that appears quickly.

See your doctor or an allergy specialist soon if you notice:

  • Breathing changes after eating or drinking that are new, recurring, or getting stronger.
  • A reaction that happens in a child, or in anyone with diagnosed asthma.
  • Hives lasting more than six weeks, or hives with joint pain, fever, or swelling.
  • Digestive symptoms such as black stools, vomiting blood, unexplained weight loss, or persistent pain — these are never something to manage with diet alone.
  • Any suspicion of anaphylaxis in your history, even if it was mild. You should have a written plan.

Anything involving medication changes, elimination diets during pregnancy, or long-term restriction should be supervised by a qualified healthcare provider or dietitian. TCM-informed habits are complementary and do not replace medical evaluation, diagnosis, or care.
