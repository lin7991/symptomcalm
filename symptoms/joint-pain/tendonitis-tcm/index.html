Tendonitis and TCM: Overworked Sinews and the Liver's Lubrication

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

The first sign is small. A twinge when you pick up the kettle. A dull ache at your elbow after a long day of typing. Your wrist protests when you grip a doorknob. You rest it for a day or two, it settles — then it comes back the moment you use it again. Over weeks, the same tendon keeps nagging: clicking, creaking, burning, refusing to settle no matter how many ice packs you rotate.

That tendon is one of the hardest-working structures in your body. Every repetition of a movement is a tug on its fibers — and when the tugging outpaces the repair, the tissue becomes irritated and thickened. Western medicine groups these conditions under tendonitis or tendinopathy. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) reads the same story through a different lens: it asks not just where the tendon hurts, but why this particular tendon has run out of resilience — and what the body's deepest supply lines have to do with it.

Before we dive in: this article focuses on tendon pain from overuse — the classic "tennis elbow," "golfer's elbow," Achilles, patellar, and wrist tendon issues. For the broader map of joint pain and its TCM patterns, see our Joint Pain Overview. For the deeper concepts behind the patterns below, our TCM Basics guides to the Liver and Blood Deficiency explain the foundation — here we focus on recognizing and supporting your specific tendon pattern.

The Western View: Tendons Under Repetitive Load

A tendon is the tough, rope-like band that anchors muscle to bone — the cable that transmits every bit of force your muscles generate. When that cable is worked past its capacity to repair itself, the tissue becomes painful, thickened, and tender. The classic trigger is a load spike: a new workout routine, a weekend of unaccustomed DIY, a promotion that doubled your typing hours, a sudden increase in running mileage. Tendons respond slowly to change — collagen turnover is measured in months, not days — which is why tendon pain lingers long after the activity that started it has stopped.

One important detail from modern research: the old idea that tendon pain is always "inflammation" (the "-itis" in tendonitis) has been refined. Imaging of chronic cases often shows collagen disorganization and failed healing rather than classic inflammatory cells — which is why many clinicians now prefer the term tendinopathy. The name stuck, but the understanding has shifted: this is a tissue that has lost its ability to keep up, not simply a tissue that is on fire. The most common sites tell the story of modern life: the lateral elbow (tennis elbow), the medial elbow (golfer's elbow), the Achilles, the patellar tendon (jumper's knee), and the wrist tendons that work a keyboard all day.

Conventional care typically includes relative rest, ice, physical therapy with graded loading, and in persistent cases injections or surgery. For most people, the frustration is the persistence — the tendon that improves for a week and then flares again, for months on end.

The TCM View: Overworked Sinews and the Liver's Lubrication

In TCM theory, tendons, ligaments, and connective tissue are grouped under the term sinews (筋, jin), and the organ system that governs them is the Liver (肝). The classic teaching is twofold: the Liver stores Blood, and Blood nourishes the sinews — "the Liver governs the sinews" (肝主筋). When Liver Blood is abundant, sinews stay supple, elastic, and well lubricated. When Blood runs low, the same tissue turns dry, brittle, and vulnerable to the wear of daily repetition.

Here is the analogy that makes it click: think of a tendon as a cable running through a pulley system, and Liver Blood as the oil. A well-oiled cable glides through thousands of cycles a day without complaint — typing, lifting, walking, gripping. Run the oil low — through overwork, poor sleep, chronic stress, erratic meals, or plain aging — and the same cable starts to fray. It creaks. It catches. It finally complains. And where does the wear show up first? At the busiest friction points: the elbow, the wrist, the heel, the knee. The tendon that hurts is the cable that has been running dry at the highest-traffic junction.

This is why TCM does not stop at "which tendon" — it asks why this tendon has run dry. In practice, three patterns sit behind most stubborn tendon pain, and each one calls for a different kind of support. The same medical diagnosis can live on different patterns in different people, which is why a one-size-fits-all protocol so often misses.

Pattern 1 — Liver Blood Deficiency: The Dry Cable

How it feels: stiffness and creakiness more than sharp pain. The tendon is tight in the morning or after rest, then gradually "warms up" with use. There may be clicking or popping, a slow-to-recover quality, and an overall sense that the limb needs extra time to get going. This pattern often travels with other signs of low Blood: dry or tired eyes, brittle nails, fatigue, and — in people who menstruate — lighter or delayed periods. Nighttime cramps in the calf or foot are a classic companion.

The TCM reading: the oil level is low. Blood that should be moistening the sinews is being diverted or is simply not being produced in sufficient quantity — the classic profile described in our TCM Basics article on Blood Deficiency, showing up at the tendon instead of at the eyes or nails.

What traditionally may help:

  • Blood-nourishing foods: dark leafy greens, beets, eggs, bone broth, black sesame, goji berries, and cooked red dates in Chinese dietary therapy — warm, cooked meals rather than raw and cold ones.
  • Acupressure: SP-6 (Sanyinjiao, inner ankle — nourishes Blood; skip during pregnancy), ST-36 (Zusanli, below the knee — builds Qi and Blood), and LV-3 (Taichong, between the first and second toes — the Liver's own source point). One to two minutes of gentle small circles per point, both sides.
  • Warm up before use, always. Five to ten minutes of warmth and gentle movement before the activity lets the dry cable glide instead of snap.
  • Protect sleep. In TCM theory, Blood is replenished during deep rest — the tendon that never gets a full repair cycle keeps fraying.

Pattern 2 — Qi and Blood Stagnation: The Friction Burn

How it feels: a fixed, localized ache at one specific spot — not a diffuse soreness. The tendon is tender to pressure, aches with use, and may have mild swelling. It often starts after a load spike: a new exercise, a weekend project, a travel day of suitcase lifting. There is a characteristic rhythm: stiff after sitting, eases as you move, then aches again as fatigue builds.

The TCM reading: the channel at the tendon site is jammed. Qi and Blood are moving, but not through this spot — like a highway interchange where traffic backs up at one on-ramp. The overworked tissue cannot clear its own waste products, so the local area stays stuck, tight, and irritable. This is the same mechanism as the Qi Stagnation and Blood Stasis concepts in TCM Basics, localized at a tendon under repetitive load.

What traditionally may help:

  • Warmth plus gentle movement — the "move the jam, don't hammer it" rule. Heat before activity, light channel-moving exercise (walking, easy cycling, gentle range of motion at the affected joint), and never aggressive stretching into pain.
  • Acupressure to move the channel: LI-4 (Hegu, in the web of the hand — moves Qi and relieves pain; skip during pregnancy), LV-3 (Taichong — moves Liver Qi), and TB-5 (Waiguan, on the outer forearm, two finger-widths above the wrist crease — right on the forearm extensor line that tennis elbow lives on).
  • Light massage along the channel, not deep poking at the painful spot — the goal is circulation through the area, not more irritation.
  • Food-level support: turmeric and ginger as everyday seasonings; both have traditional warming, moving roles and are studied for anti-inflammatory activity.
  • Modify the load, not the whole body. Rest the specific motion that spikes the tendon (change grip, pace, or tool) while keeping the rest of you moving.

Pattern 3 — Damp-Heat: The Overheated Swamp

How it feels: hot, red, swollen, and throbbing — the tendon looks angry. It feels warm to the touch, is worse with heat and at night, and often follows an acute overuse event. There may be a heavy, achy, "full" quality to the discomfort, and the flare can feel disproportionate to what you actually did.

The TCM reading: overuse generates internal Heat at the tendon site, and if fluid metabolism is already sluggish, that Heat combines with Dampness into a swamp: the tendon is simultaneously inflamed and waterlogged. Dampness makes the swelling stick; Heat makes it burn. This is the classic Damp-Heat Bi pattern — joint and tendon pain with a hot, swollen, sticky character.

What traditionally may help:

  • Cooling and draining foods: celery, cucumber, mung beans, barley, and watermelon — and a firm pause on alcohol, spicy food, and fried food, which in TCM theory stoke Damp-Heat.
  • Acupressure: ST-40 (Fenglong, below the knee — the classic "resolve Dampness" point), SP-9 (Yinlingquan, just below the knee on the inner shin — drains lower-body Dampness), and LI-11 (Quchi, at the elbow crease — clears Heat).
  • Cold compress in the acute phase — unlike the other two patterns, this one genuinely calls for cooling, and only for a few days while the flare settles.
  • Relative rest of the affected area — the tendon needs to drain before it can be loaded again.

⚠️ Important: a hot, red, swollen tendon deserves a medical check. Infection, rupture, and gout can all present this way, and they need different care entirely.

Pattern-Matched Self-Care: What You Can Do at Home

Whatever your pattern, a few universal habits are safe, sensible, and aligned with both the TCM and the modern rehab playbooks. They are complementary practices — bring them to your healthcare provider and physical therapist, not instead of them.

  1. Load management is the foundation. Every serious approach agrees: tendons adapt to load slowly. The useful rule of thumb is the 48-hour window — after a demanding session, the tendon needs recovery time before the next one, and increases in load should be gradual, not heroic.
  2. Warm first, move second. Five to ten minutes of warmth (compress, shower, heated wrap) before using the tendon — the oil analogy works in practice: a warmed cable glides better than a cold one.
  3. Meet your master point: GB-34 (Yanglingquan). The depression just below the outside of the knee is called the master point of the sinews in TCM — a classic point for tendon issues anywhere in the body. One to two minutes of gentle pressure on both sides, daily, is a tendon-friendly habit.
  4. Match your points to your pattern: nourish with SP-6 and ST-36 (dry cable), move with LI-4 and TB-5 (friction burn), cool and drain with ST-40 and LI-11 (overheated swamp).
  5. Eccentric loading, guided by a professional. Physiotherapist-guided eccentric exercises have the strongest evidence base for Achilles and patellar tendinopathy — a Western protocol that dovetails beautifully with the TCM principle of graded, gentle loading rather than rest-only or force.
  6. What NOT to do: aggressive stretching of an inflamed tendon; heat on a hot, swollen tendon; "no pain, no gain" pushing; or total immobilization. Modern rehab and TCM theory agree: the tendon needs graded load, not extremes.

What Research Says

Tendon pain is well studied, and several threads of research connect with the TCM reading:

  • The terminology shift: chronic tendon pain is increasingly called tendinopathy because imaging often shows collagen disorganization and failed healing rather than classic inflammatory cells. This matches the TCM emphasis on deficiency and stagnation as the root — the tissue has run out of capacity, not just "become inflamed."
  • Eccentric exercise: Alfredson's classic protocol and later randomized trials found eccentric heel-drop training associated with meaningful improvement in Achilles tendinopathy, with comparable results using heavy-slow resistance training in patellar tendinopathy (Kongsgaard and colleagues, 2009). This is the evidence backbone for "graded loading" — the same principle as the TCM "warm up, move gently, build gradually" logic.
  • Acupuncture for lateral epicondylitis: systematic reviews, including a Cochrane review of acupuncture for tennis elbow, suggest possible short-term pain relief, though the quality of the evidence is moderate and effects are strongest when acupuncture is combined with exercise.
  • Curcumin and turmeric: several small trials report modest pain reduction with turmeric/curcumin in inflammatory musculoskeletal conditions; framed as food-level support, this aligns with the traditional use of turmeric as a moving, warming herb.
  • Load, not rest, is the treatment signal: tendon research consistently supports gradual load progression over prolonged rest — the collagen needs mechanical stimulus to remodel. The TCM tradition arrives at the same place: move what is stuck, nourish what is dry, and let the tissue rebuild under gentle demand.

None of this replaces diagnosis. Tendon pain shares its territory with nerve compression, joint pathology, and referred pain — imaging is often needed to tell them apart, and the treatment paths diverge.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ Seek medical evaluation promptly if you experience any of the following:

  • A sudden "pop" followed by weakness or inability to use the muscle — a possible tendon rupture (most famously the Achilles)
  • A hot, red, swollen tendon, with or without fever — possible infection or other acute condition
  • Pain at rest or at night that you cannot explain
  • Tendon pain that began after a fall, a direct injury, or a visible deformity
  • No improvement after 2–3 weeks of consistent self-care, or steady worsening
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness spreading through the limb
  • Tendon pain while taking certain medications (some antibiotics and other drugs are associated with tendon issues) or with poorly controlled diabetes — check with your doctor

Tendon pain is stubborn, but it is not mysterious. If your symptoms are not settling, a healthcare provider can confirm what is actually going on — and a physical therapist can build the graded loading plan that modern research supports. TCM is a complementary lens: it works best alongside, not instead of, conventional diagnosis and care.

Practical Takeaways:
  • Think of tendons as cables and Liver Blood as the oil — when the oil runs low, the fraying shows up at the busiest friction points: elbow, wrist, heel, knee.
  • Three patterns sit behind most stubborn tendon pain: the dry cable (Liver Blood Deficiency — stiff, creaky, worse after rest), the friction burn (Qi and Blood Stagnation — fixed ache after a load spike), and the overheated swamp (Damp-Heat — hot, red, swollen).
  • Match your care to your pattern: nourish, move, or cool — the wrong tool for the wrong pattern can make it worse.
  • Warm up before use, every time; manage load with the 48-hour window; let a physical therapist guide eccentric loading.
  • GB-34 (Yanglingquan) is the master point of the sinews — a daily minute of acupressure there is a tendon-friendly habit.
  • A hot, swollen, or suddenly weak tendon needs a medical check, not a self-care routine.
  • TCM is a complementary lens — pair it with your doctor's and physical therapist's guidance, not instead of them.
