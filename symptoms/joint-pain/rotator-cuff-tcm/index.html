Rotator Cuff Pain: A TCM View of a Shoulder Under Load

Symptom Guide · 11 min read

It shows up in the most ordinary gestures. Reaching for the seatbelt. Reaching into the back seat. Pulling a sweater over your head. Lifting a pan off a high shelf. And then, at night, when you roll onto that side — a deep, boring ache that wakes you and refuses to let you settle.

Rotator cuff pain is one of the most common shoulder complaints there is, and one of the most confusing. The scan says "tear" and the pain disappears on its own. The pain is gone and the scan still says "tear." Sometimes rest makes it worse, sometimes exercise makes it worse, and the same routine that helped a friend does nothing for you.

The reason the confusion is so persistent is that the rotator cuff is not a single structure with a single failure mode. It is a team of four tendons that must work together, passing through a doorway only a few millimetres wide. That makes this less a story about damage and more a story about load — how much demand is arriving, how much capacity is available, and what happens in the gap between the two.

This article looks at rotator cuff pain through that lens, and then through the lens of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), which has its own long-standing way of describing shoulders that hurt under load.

Where this article sits: This guide focuses specifically on the rotator cuff — the four tendons that stabilise the shoulder joint. For a wider map of shoulder pain (channels, weather sensitivity, referred patterns), see our general guide to shoulder pain in TCM. The Four-Tendon Team: What the Rotator Cuff Actually Does

Start with the engineering, because it explains almost everything about how this injury behaves.

The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body — a shallow ball sitting on a shallow socket, deliberately built for range rather than stability. The rotator cuff is what makes that trade-off workable. Four muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis) send tendons that wrap around the head of the upper arm bone and pull it into the socket as you move.

Picture a tent held up by guy ropes on four sides. The pole is the arm bone. The ropes are the four cuff tendons, each pulling in a different direction — up, out, in, back. As long as tension is shared, the pole stays centred and the whole structure moves freely. Lose tension on one rope and the pole starts drifting with every gust. The shoulder does not dislocate; instead the ball rides slightly high in the socket, and something starts rubbing that should not be rubbing.

That "something" is the second half of the anatomy lesson. Above the cuff tendons sits the acromion, a bony roof. Between the two lies the subacromial space — the doorway — and running through it is a fluid-filled cushion, the bursa. In a healthy shoulder there is clearance. In an irritated shoulder the doorway narrows: the tendon swells, the cushion swells, the space shrinks, and the tendon gets pinched with every overhead movement. This pinch is conventionally called impingement.

Which tendon pays the price most often? The supraspinatus — the one that runs directly through the narrowest part of the doorway, and the one that fires when you raise your arm to the side or reach forward. That is why the classic presentation is pain in the top and front-side of the shoulder, worst at the end of a reaching motion and worst at night.

Two details worth knowing: first, the cuff is not a cushion — it is a stabiliser, which is why strengthening it changes the mechanics rather than just the pain. Second, cuff tendons have a relatively poor blood supply near their attachment point, and tendon tissue repairs slowly by design. Both facts shape what recovery realistically looks like: less a switch than a slow rebuild of capacity.

The Western View: Load, the Doorway, and Wear

Modern practice has moved a long way from the idea that rotator cuff pain is simply a mechanical tear that must be fixed. Three findings drove that shift, and understanding them makes the TCM perspective easier to place.

Not all tears hurt. Imaging studies consistently find rotator cuff tears in a substantial share of adults who have no shoulder pain at all — and that proportion climbs steadily with age. A tear on a scan is therefore a finding, not automatically the explanation. This is why clinicians increasingly ask not "what does the scan show?" but "what can this shoulder do, and what makes it worse?"

Exercise performs as well as surgery for most non-traumatic cases. In a widely cited randomised trial, people with shoulder impingement were assigned to subacromial decompression surgery or to a placebo procedure — and the surgical group did no better. Meanwhile, trials of progressive exercise for rotator cuff related shoulder pain show meaningful improvements in pain and function, which is why structured exercise is generally recommended as the first line rather than the last resort.

Load is a ratio, not a number. The same overhead lift that is trivial for one shoulder can be overloading for another — depending on tendon quality, shoulder-blade control, sleep, age, and how quickly the demand arrived. Physiotherapists describe this as the balance between load and capacity. Pain appears when load exceeds capacity, and the way out is either to lower the load for a while, to raise the capacity, or — usually — both.

What this means practically: the useful questions are not "is it torn?" but "which movements load it?", "how much can this shoulder tolerate today?", and "what is keeping capacity low?" That last question is where TCM has something to contribute.

The TCM View: Sinews Need Fuel and Free Flow

In TCM theory, tendons and ligaments are grouped under the concept of the sinews, and the sinews are traditionally described as nourished by Liver Blood and anchored by Kidney Essence. Our guide to tendon pain in TCM explores that lubrication framework in detail — the short version is that TCM treats a tendon as a structure that needs both material (Blood and Essence: the lubricant and the raw supplies) and movement (freely flowing Qi: the delivery route).

A tendon under load needs both at once. Fuel without flow is a well-stocked warehouse with a blocked road. Flow without fuel is a clear road with nothing being delivered. This is why two people with the same scan finding can have completely different experiences: the structural picture is identical, the supply-and-delivery picture is not.

Then there is the loading itself. In TCM theory, repetitive strain is understood as a local stagnation of Qi and Blood — flow that should be smooth, churned and jammed in one place. The traditional image is a rope pulled repeatedly across the same edge: the fibres at that exact spot fray while the rest of the rope stays sound. And because the shoulder is where several channel pathways converge (the Large Intestine, Small Intestine, San Jiao, Lung and Gallbladder channels all pass through or near it), a stagnation here can register across a surprisingly wide area — down the outer arm, up into the neck, even into the hand.

The third ingredient is Cold and Dampness. Cold is described as contracting and slowing — the classic image of a garden hose stiffening in freezing weather. Dampness is described as heavy and lingering, like moving through water. A shoulder that aches deeply in cold, wet weather and loosens after a hot shower is showing exactly this pattern in TCM terms.

Put the pieces together and you get a framework that maps neatly onto the modern load-versus-capacity model. Excess load shows up as stagnation. Lowered capacity shows up as deficiency of the nourishing side. And the environment — cold, damp, immobility, poor sleep — shapes how quickly either one appears.

Pattern 1 — Qi and Blood Stagnation From Overload

Who it tends to fit: People whose pain arrived with demand. A new gym programme, more overhead work, painting a ceiling, moving house, a busy swim season, or a job that keeps the arm above shoulder height. Pain that started sharp and localised and has since spread into an ache.

What it may look like: A specific, fixed point of pain at the top or front of the shoulder, worse with a particular movement and better with rest. Stiffness after holding one position. Possibly a knot or tender band in the surrounding muscle. The shoulder "catches" at a particular angle.

Analogy: a rope pulled across the same sharp edge, day after day. The fibres at that exact spot fray long before the rest of the rope shows any wear.

What traditionally may support it: The traditional direction is to move Qi and Blood — which means gentle, regular, pain-free motion rather than either total rest or pushing through. In TCM theory, stagnation responds to flow: slow arm circles, pendulum swings, shoulder rolls, warmth before activity, and a temporary reduction in the specific movement that provoked it. Rest alone can deepen stagnation, which is why "take two weeks off" so often leaves the shoulder stiffer than before. Pair this with the graded loading section below, and let the pain signal guide the pace.

Pattern 2 — Cold-Damp in the Sinew Channels

Who it tends to fit: People whose shoulder is weather-sensitive — noticeably worse on damp, cold, windy days and better in warmth. Often worse first thing in the morning and after sitting still. There may be a history of sleeping with the arm exposed to a draught, or working in cold environments.

What it may look like: A heavy, deep, diffuse ache rather than a sharp pinpoint. The shoulder feels stiff and reluctant for the first thirty minutes of the day. Warmth — a hot shower, a heat pack, a warm layer — reliably eases it. Cold reliably worsens it.

Analogy: a hinge that stiffens in cold weather. Nothing is broken; everything is simply slower and more resistant until warmth returns.

What traditionally may support it: Warming and drying are the traditional direction here: keeping the shoulder covered in cool weather, applying warmth before movement rather than after, and — in traditional practice — moxibustion (gentle warming with mugwort) and warm-needle approaches, both specifically associated with Cold patterns. Foods traditionally described as warming (ginger, cinnamon, leeks, lamb, and cooked rather than raw meals) are used to support the interior side. Avoid the sequence of prolonged cold exposure followed by sudden heavy use — that is the pattern where a cold shoulder becomes an injured one.

Pattern 3 — Liver Blood and Kidney Essence Deficiency

Who it tends to fit: People whose shoulder has been aching for months or years, often with a gradual rather than sudden onset. Age is a factor, but so is a long stretch of overwork, poor sleep, chronic stress, or a depleted feeling generally. Frequently accompanied by dry eyes, brittle nails, light sleep, or a deep fatigue that rest does not clear.

What it may look like: A persistent deep ache with weakness rather than sharp pain, and a sense that the shoulder tires quickly. Reduced tolerance for loads that used to be easy. Slow recovery after any exertion. Possibly clicking or grinding with movement.

Analogy: a maintenance crew with too few materials and a long road to travel. The road might be clear, but the crew cannot keep up with wear — so every pothole takes longer to fill than it used to.

What traditionally may support it: This is the pattern where the emphasis shifts from clearing to nourishing — and where patience matters. TCM tradition favours building the supply side: adequate protein, cooked whole foods, dark leafy greens, black sesame, goji berry, bone broth, eggs, and genuinely protected sleep (the deepest nourishment happens at night, in TCM theory as in modern physiology). Crucially, this pattern usually does not respond to more effort; it responds to steady, moderate, progressive loading plus better inputs — and it is measured in months. Pattern 4 — Phlegm-Damp and the Swollen Cushion

Who it tends to fit: People with a boggy, swollen quality to the pain, often on a background of heaviness, sluggish digestion, a coated tongue, or a tendency to retain fluid. Frequently overlaps the bursitis picture.

What it may look like: A deep, dull, diffuse ache rather than a pinpoint. Swelling or a feeling of pressure in the shoulder. Markedly worse at night, especially lying on that side. Pain that radiates down the outer arm. Motion limited more by discomfort than by a mechanical block.

Analogy: a waterlogged cushion squeezed into a small gap. The cushion itself is not torn — it is thick, heavy and in the way, so every movement compresses something already irritated.

What traditionally may support it: The traditional direction is to transform Phlegm and drain Dampness. In practice that means reducing the inputs TCM associates with both — alcohol, refined sugar, fried and very rich food, and excess dairy — with a few weeks as a trial. Foods traditionally used to drain Dampness (pearl barley, mung bean, celery, winter melon, leafy greens) are added at the same time. Locally, gentle movement within a pain-free range keeps fluid moving; prolonged stillness tends to make this pattern feel worse rather than better. Rebuilding Load Capacity: A Graded Approach

Whatever pattern fits, the long-term answer for a rotator cuff is the same: restore the balance between load and capacity. What follows is general education, not a prescription — a physiotherapist or clinician who can actually examine your shoulder should guide the details, and anyone with a recent injury, significant weakness, or a suspected full tear should be assessed before loading.

1. Find the pain-free range first

Before strengthening anything, establish what the shoulder currently tolerates. Move slowly through small arcs — pendulum swings, slow shoulder rolls, lifting the arm to the side to the point just before pain begins. Use a simple rule: discomfort that settles within minutes is acceptable; pain that builds during the movement, or lingers into the next day, means the load was too high.

2. Start with isometrics rather than movement

Isometric work — holding a gentle contraction against a fixed resistance without moving the joint — is often the most tolerated entry point for a painful tendon. Two examples: press the back of your hand outward against a wall (external rotation hold) with the elbow tucked at your side; or press the palm inward against a door frame (internal rotation hold). Hold 20–30 seconds at low effort, several times a day. The goal is to reintroduce load without provoking the doorway.

3. Build the external rotators — the neglected half

In most shoulders under strain, the external rotators (infraspinatus and teres minor) are the weak link, while the internal rotators at the chest and front of the shoulder are over-trained by daily life. External rotation work with a light band — elbow at your side, forearm rotating outward — tends to be both the most useful and the least provocative. Keep it slow, keep the range small, and resist the urge to add resistance quickly.

4. Include the shoulder-blade team

The cuff and the shoulder-blade muscles work together. Rows, gentle blade squeezes, and wall slides teach the blade to sit in a position where the doorway has clearance. If the blade is not moving well, the cuff is doing a job it was not designed to do alone — which is one reason shoulder problems so often recur.

5. Manage the two things that quietly raise the load

  • Sleep position. Night pain with a rotator cuff is often positional. Sleeping on the opposite side, or on your back with a small pillow under the affected elbow, can meaningfully reduce the next day's pain. If you must lie on that side, a pillow supporting the arm in front of the body keeps the shoulder from rolling forward and compressing the doorway.
  • The overhead habit. Temporarily move the tasks that provoke it — store items lower, use a step stool with the arm close to the body, adjust the workstation so the mouse and keyboard sit below shoulder height. This is not "rest"; it is lowering load while capacity rebuilds.

Acupressure points traditionally used around the shoulder

Gentle self-pressure for 60–90 seconds per point, once or twice a day, pressing to a mild comfortable ache rather than pain. These are traditionally used for shoulder-region obstruction and are supportive self-care, not a replacement for medical treatment.

  • LI-15 (Jianyu) — at the top-front of the shoulder, in the hollow that appears when you raise the arm. The best-known local point for shoulder pain and restriction.
  • SI-9 (Jianzhen) — behind the shoulder, about a finger-width above the armpit fold when the arm hangs. Traditionally used for posterior shoulder pain and for pain radiating down the outer arm.
  • SI-11 (Tianzong) — in the hollow of the shoulder blade, roughly midway between the inner edge and the outer corner. A classic point for shoulder-blade and posterior shoulder ache.
  • TE-14 (Jianliao) — at the back of the shoulder, in the depression behind and below the bony point of the shoulder. Traditionally used when raising the arm is the painful movement.
  • GB-21 (Jianjing) — midway between the base of the neck and the tip of the shoulder. Traditionally used for shoulder tension that tracks up into the neck and for stress-held tightness.
  • LI-4 (Hegou) — in the web between thumb and index finger, on the hand. A distal point traditionally used to move Qi in the upper body, including the shoulder.
Safety note: LI-4 and points in the lower abdomen and inner leg are avoided in pregnancy; if you are pregnant, skip LI-4 and speak with a qualified practitioner before using acupressure at all. Do not press directly on an acutely swollen, hot or injured shoulder, and stop any exercise that increases pain beyond mild discomfort. Seek assessment rather than self-managing if you have had a fall or injury with sudden loss of movement, or if you cannot lift the arm at all.

What Research Suggests

The research here is unusually useful, because it tells us both what works and what does not — and the picture lines up well with the TCM emphasis on flow, fuel and environment over time.

  • Exercise is the first-line approach. Randomised trials and systematic reviews of progressive exercise for rotator cuff related shoulder pain report clinically meaningful improvements in pain and function, with supervised and self-managed programmes both showing benefit. This is the strongest single evidence statement in this area.
  • Surgery for impingement did not outperform placebo. In a well-known randomised trial (CSAW) and a parallel trial in Finland, subacromial decompression produced no better outcomes than placebo surgery for impingement-type shoulder pain. This substantially reshaped how clinicians think about the "pinched tendon" narrative and reinforced the load-versus-capacity model.
  • Scans and symptoms do not always match. Imaging studies repeatedly find rotator cuff tears in a meaningful proportion of adults with no shoulder symptoms, with prevalence rising with age. This is why clinicians increasingly base decisions on function and irritability rather than imaging alone.
  • Acupuncture for shoulder pain. Systematic reviews of acupuncture for rotator cuff related shoulder pain and shoulder impingement have reported short-term pain reduction compared with sham or waiting-list controls, though the quality of evidence is generally low to moderate and blinding is inherently difficult. The most defensible reading is a possible short-term pain-relief effect rather than a change in tendon structure.
  • Warmth and local circulation. Studies of thermal therapies and manual techniques around the shoulder have examined local blood flow and tissue oxygenation, and warmth is a long-standing clinical staple for stiff, cold-sensitive joints. The evidence is modest, but the traditional use of warmth (including moxibustion) for Cold-type patterns has a rational basis in increased local blood flow and reduced muscle guarding.
  • Sleep and pain sensitivity. Poor sleep is consistently associated with increased pain sensitivity and worse recovery in musculoskeletal conditions, which supports the TCM emphasis on protected sleep as part of nourishing the sinews rather than an optional extra.
What this means for you: the highest-value actions are unglamorous — progressive loading, better sleep, less provocation, warmth when cold aggravates, and patience measured in weeks and months. The evidence is clearest on load; TCM's contribution is the parallel question of why one shoulder's capacity rebuilds faster than another's.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  • Think load, not damage. Ask which movements load it and how much it tolerates today — a more useful question than chasing a scan result.
  • Keep it moving, gently. Pain-free motion within tolerance usually serves a shoulder better than total rest, which tends to leave it stiffer.
  • Start with isometrics, then build external rotation. Low-effort holds first, then slow band work with the elbow at your side.
  • Do not skip the shoulder blade. Rows, squeezes and wall slides give the cuff a better doorway to work through.
  • Fix the night. Protect the shoulder while you sleep — it is often the single biggest lever on next-day pain.
  • Warm it when cold makes it worse, cover it in cool weather, and warm up before loading rather than after.
  • Feed the rebuild. Protein, cooked whole foods, dark leafy greens and protected sleep are the supply side of tendon capacity.
  • Give it months, not days. Tendon tissue rebuilds slowly by design; expecting a two-week turnaround is the most common reason people conclude that nothing works.

When to See a Doctor

See a healthcare provider — promptly if the following applies — rather than self-managing:

  • You cannot lift the arm at all, or the shoulder looks deformed after a fall or injury (possible dislocation, fracture, or full-thickness tear)
  • Sudden severe weakness appeared after an injury or a heavy lift
  • The shoulder is hot, red or swollen, or you have a fever (possible infection)
  • Pain is unrelenting at rest or at night and not eased by any position
  • Numbness, tingling or loss of power in the arm or hand, or pain radiating below the elbow together with neck symptoms
  • The pain followed significant trauma, or you have a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss, or night sweats
  • No improvement after several weeks of sensible load management and gentle strengthening
  • You have diabetes — shoulder conditions behave differently and often need tailored management
