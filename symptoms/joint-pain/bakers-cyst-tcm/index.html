Baker's Cyst: A TCM View of Fluid Behind the Knee

Symptom Guide · 8 min read

You first notice it when you sit — or when you try to bend your knee all the way. There's a soft, grape-sized bulge in the hollow behind your knee that wasn't there a few months ago. It isn't exactly painful. It's more like a full, tight pressure, as if something is ballooning gently at the back of the joint. By evening, after a day on your feet, it feels bigger. In the morning it seems smaller.

You look it up, and the phrase you get back is strange: Baker's cyst. It sounds ominous. It isn't a tumor, and it isn't a separate disease. It is a pocket of joint fluid that has found its way to the back of your knee — and it is almost always a message from somewhere else in the joint.

The usual advice is reassuring and slightly unsatisfying: rest, ice, elevate, maybe a compression sleeve, and if it really bothers you, a doctor can drain it with a needle. But a lot of people find that within weeks to months the bulge quietly returns, because draining a pocket doesn't change the reason the pocket filled in the first place.

That's where a Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) lens can add something useful. TCM agrees with the anatomy — a Baker's cyst really is fluid in a bursa behind the knee. What it adds is a set of questions the standard protocol tends to skip: why is this knee producing more fluid than it can clear? And why is it collecting here, in this particular corner, rather than somewhere else? Those two questions are the whole article.

Before we dive in: this article focuses specifically on the popliteal (Baker's) cyst — the soft swelling behind the knee. What a Baker's Cyst Actually Is (In Plain English)

Your knee is not a dry hinge. It is a sealed, fluid-filled compartment. The joint capsule holds a small amount of synovial fluid — the slick, egg-white-like lubricant that keeps cartilage gliding instead of grinding. That fluid is not static. It is produced, circulated, and reabsorbed continuously, like a fountain that is always being topped up and always draining away.

Behind the knee — in the soft hollow called the popliteal fossa — sits a small fluid sac, or bursa. In many people, this sac communicates with the inside of the knee joint through a narrow channel. The channel behaves a little like a one-way valve: it lets fluid move out of the joint more easily than it lets fluid move back in.

Now put those two facts together with a third one:

  • The knee produces more synovial fluid when it is irritated — by a meniscal tear, cartilage wear, osteoarthritis, an inflammatory joint condition, or a previous injury.
  • The overflow is pushed backward through that valve-like channel into the bursa behind the knee.
  • Because the channel favors outflow over inflow, fluid accumulates. The sac inflates like a water balloon tied to a slow leak.

That is a Baker's cyst. It is technically called a popliteal cyst, and it was described by the surgeon William Baker in the 1870s. It is common in adults roughly between their mid-30s and 70s, and it is very often discovered by accident — a person gets an MRI for something else, and there it is. A large share of popliteal cysts are found alongside meniscal tears or knee osteoarthritis, which is exactly what you would expect from the mechanism above.

Which brings us to the most important sentence in the article: the cyst is a symptom, not the problem. It is the visible result of a joint whose fluid economy has gotten out of balance. If the underlying knee irritation is still there, the pocket will simply refill.

The TCM View: A Sump Behind the Knee

TCM has no word for "Baker's cyst," exactly — but it has a very old way of thinking about fluid that lands on this problem with unusual precision.

Start with a modern analogy. Picture the knee as a pressurized hydraulic system: fluid is manufactured, circulated, and drained at a steady rate. Now picture a small sump — the lowest point of the system, where liquid naturally collects when it has nowhere else to go. In a well-maintained building, the sump stays empty. It only fills when the system upstream is producing more fluid than the drainage can handle.

In TCM theory, fluid in the body is not inert. It is called Jin-Ye (often translated as "body fluids"), and it is meant to be warmed, transformed, and moved continuously. When fluid stops moving well — when it becomes heavy, sticky, or pools — TCM describes the result as Dampness. You don't need a whole new vocabulary to follow this: think of Dampness as water that has settled because the current slowed down. (Our TCM Basics guide to Dampness explains the concept in depth if you want it.)

Two more pieces complete the picture:

  • The back of the knee is a channel crossroads. Several meridian pathways — including the Bladder and Kidney channels — travel through the popliteal fossa, the same soft hollow where the cyst forms. The deepest point of that hollow is the well-known acupoint BL-40 (Weizhong), traditionally associated with clearing stagnation in the region. When TCM practitioners talk about fluid collecting "at a low point on a channel," this is what they mean: gravity and pathway anatomy conspire to make certain places collect first.
  • The knee is governed by the Liver and Kidney in TCM theory. The Kidneys are said to govern bone; the Liver governs the sinews and tendons. The knee, where bone and tendon work together under load, is viewed as their shared territory. When Kidney and Liver resources run low — as they tend to with age, overwork, or long-term wear — the joint is described as less resilient and slower to clear the disturbance that keeps it irritated.

Put together, the TCM reading of a popliteal cyst is: a low point on the body's fluid network is filling because the fluid is heavy or stuck (Dampness), because there is obstruction in the area (Qi and Blood stagnation, often from an old injury), or because the underlying terrain is depleted and cold (Liver-Kidney deficiency with Cold-Damp). Which of those dominates changes what traditionally tends to help.

What's Driving the Fluid? Finding the Upstream Story

Because a Baker's cyst is downstream of something, the most useful question is: what is the knee doing that it wasn't doing before? Here are the common upstream drivers, and how the TCM lens reads each one.

  • A meniscal tear or old twist injury. This is the classic setup. The meniscus is the knee's internal shock absorber, and a tear keeps the joint mildly irritated — which keeps fluid production turned up. In TCM terms this is a pattern of Blood Stasis from trauma: an incompletely cleared local injury that leaves a fixed, stationary obstruction.
  • Osteoarthritis and cartilage wear. As cartilage thins, the joint surfaces become less smooth, and low-grade irritation becomes the new normal. TCM would add a terrain layer here: the degenerative side is described as Liver-Kidney deficiency, and the swelling side as Dampness accumulating where flow is already sluggish.
  • Inflammatory joint conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, gout, or psoriatic arthritis. These produce genuinely heated, reactive joints and often larger fluid volumes. Here TCM speaks of Damp-Heat — fluid plus inflammation, which is a different situation and a different conversation.
  • Overuse and loading. Deep squatting, kneeling work, long runs, or standing all day increase joint fluid turnover. If the drainage side of the equation can't keep pace, fluid nudges into the popliteal sac. TCM reads overuse as Qi and Blood stagnation in the channels around the joint, sometimes with Dampness settling into the emptied-out spaces.
  • Simply being discovered by accident. Some popliteal cysts are incidental findings with no clear trigger, and the knee feels fine. In those cases there may be nothing to fix — only something to monitor.

This is the practical payoff of thinking in upstream terms: two people can have the same bulge behind the knee and need completely different emphasis. One is dealing with an unhealed injury; the other with an aging, dry, deficient joint. Same pocket, different story.

One pattern to rule out first: if the knee behind the swelling is hot, red, markedly swollen, and you feel feverish or unwell, this is not a self-care situation. A hot, angry joint can point to infection or an inflammatory flare that needs prompt medical attention. This article is about the common, calm, chronic bulge — not an acutely inflamed knee.

Three Patterns Behind a Popliteal Cyst

In TCM theory, the same visible swelling can arise from different internal patterns — and identifying yours is what makes any self-care specific rather than generic. Read the three descriptions below and notice which one makes you nod. Most people recognize one clearly, with a little of a second.

Pattern 1 — Dampness Pooling in the Channel: The Slow Drain

What it feels like: the swelling is soft and somewhat diffuse — more of a doughy fullness than a distinct marble. The knee feels heavy rather than sharp. It's noticeably worse in humid, damp, or rainy weather, and worse after long periods of standing. You may also notice heaviness in the legs, a muzzy head in the morning, loose or sticky stools, and a slightly thick coating on the tongue.

In TCM theory: this is the classic picture of fluid that is not being transformed and moved efficiently. The Spleen is described as the organ system that "transforms and transports" fluids; when it is under-supported, fluid becomes heavy and settles at low points. There may be no injury story at all — just a body that is holding water a little too well.

Traditionally, the emphasis is: keeping the system moving — warm cooked food over cold and raw, less sugar, dairy, and alcohol, and steady gentle movement rather than long stretches of sitting.

Pattern 2 — Blood Stasis from an Old Injury: The Leak That Keeps Refilling

What it feels like: you can point to a moment. A twist on the stairs, a sports collision, a fall, a torn meniscus on an MRI. The cyst appeared around that time or after it, and it is stubborn — it comes back after being drained, and it may be noticeably more uncomfortable at night or with certain movements. The swelling may feel more fixed and localized than diffuse, and the knee may catch or click.

In TCM theory: this is Blood Stasis — a local obstruction left behind by an injury that never fully cleared. The body keeps responding to it with more fluid. The cyst is not the injury; it is the knee's ongoing reaction to an injury that is still, in a sense, unfinished business.

Traditionally, the emphasis is: clearing the local stagnation — gentle movement that improves circulation without re-irritating the joint, warmth, and in-clinic work such as acupuncture or Tui Na where a practitioner can address the obstruction directly. Crucially, an unresolved structural injury such as a meniscal tear deserves proper medical evaluation.

Pattern 3 — Cold-Damp on a Depleted Terrain: The Cold, Creaky Knee

What it feels like: this one is chronic and gradual. The knee is stiff in the morning and takes a while to "warm up." Everything is worse in cold and damp weather and better with warmth. The ache sits deep in the joint. You may be past your mid-40s, and the knee has been noisy or achy for years. Cold hands and feet, a dull lower-back ache, and fatigue that builds through the day often come along for the ride.

In TCM theory: Cold-Damp has settled into a joint whose underlying resources are low — the Liver-Kidney "terrain" that TCM associates with bone and tendon. Cold, in this framework, contracts and slows; when combined with a depleted base, fluid moves sluggishly and pools. This pattern frequently travels with knee osteoarthritis.

Traditionally, the emphasis is: warming and nourishing — cooked, warming foods, keeping the joint covered in cold weather, gentle strength work for the muscles around the knee, and consistent (not heroic) movement. Warming therapies such as moxibustion are traditionally used here, though never on skin that is hot, red, numb, or broken.

Which Pattern Fits You? A Quick Self-Check

Use this as a rough compass, not a diagnosis. The goal is to notice which column has the most checkmarks next to it.

Traditional Approaches That May Support Fluid Movement

None of the following is a claim about erasing a cyst. Think of them as ways of supporting the two things that matter: keeping fluid moving and not irritating the joint that is producing the extra fluid.

Food and drink

In TCM dietary thinking, fluid that is heavy and slow responds best to food that is warm, cooked, and easy to process:

  • Traditionally used to support fluid movement: adzuki (red) beans, pearl barley, Job's tears (coix), celery, cucumber, winter melon, and small amounts of ginger in cooking.
  • Traditionally reduced: iced drinks, excessive raw foods, heavy sugar, rich dairy, fried food, and alcohol — all described as making fluid heavier and harder to move.
  • If the knee runs cold and stiff: cooked root vegetables, black beans, walnuts, and warming spices such as ginger, cinnamon, and a little black pepper.
  • If the knee runs hot and angry: cooling, draining foods such as cucumber, celery, mung bean, and barley water — and skip alcohol and chili while things are flared.

Warm fluids rather than iced ones, sipped steadily through the day, are the simplest version of this principle.

Acupressure — with an important safety note

Some points traditionally used in this context sit on or near the knee and lower leg. Press with the pad of a thumb or finger in small, gentle circles for one to two minutes per point, on both legs, once or twice a day. You are looking for a mild, pleasant ache — never sharp pain.

  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — about four finger-widths below the kneecap, just outside the shin bone. Traditionally used to support digestion and the body's fluid-transforming capacity.
  • SP-9 (Yinlingquan) — on the inner lower leg, in the hollow just below the inner knee. Traditionally used for Dampness in the lower body.
  • GB-34 (Yanglingquan) — on the outer lower leg, in the hollow below the head of the fibula. Traditionally associated with the sinews and with the free movement of the joint.
  • KI-3 (Taixi) — in the hollow between the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon. Traditionally used to support the Kidney's role in the lower body.
  • BL-57 (Chengshan) — in the middle of the calf muscle, below the bulge. Traditionally used for calf heaviness and cramping.
Do not press on the cyst itself. The popliteal fossa behind the knee carries the popliteal artery, the popliteal vein, and the tibial nerve, all sitting fairly close to the surface. Deep pressure in that hollow is not advisable — and pressing directly on a swollen, tense sac is a bad idea regardless. Work above and below the knee, on the points listed above. Skip all self-massage on skin that is hot, red, broken, numb, or recently operated on. If you are pregnant, avoid strong pressure on points with traditional cautions such as SP-6 and LI-4, and speak with a qualified practitioner first.

Movement, elevation, and the calf pump

  • Use the calf muscle pump. Your calf works as a second heart for the lower leg, pushing venous and lymphatic fluid upward every time it contracts. Ankle pumps — flexing and pointing the foot, 20–30 times, several times a day — are the simplest way to keep things moving, especially when you sit for long stretches.
  • Elevate when you can. Legs up above heart level for 10–15 minutes in the evening gives fluid a chance to drain with the help of gravity.
  • Keep the knee moving within a comfortable range. Full-range, low-load movement — walking on flat ground, cycling, gentle knee bends — keeps synovial fluid circulating instead of pooling. Complete rest tends to make a knee stiffer and more achy, not better.
  • Strengthen the support system. The quadriceps and hamstrings take load off the joint surfaces. Wall sits, straight-leg raises, and step-ups are the usual starting points, once a healthcare provider has cleared you for them.
  • Warmth, traditionally. A warm compress above and below the knee on cold, stiff days is a classic TCM habit. Do not apply heat to a knee that is hot, red, or acutely swollen.

What Research Says

The research here is mostly descriptive rather than interventional — useful for understanding why the cyst behaves the way it does:

  • Most popliteal cysts connect to the joint. Ultrasound and imaging studies have repeatedly found that the great majority of Baker's cysts communicate with the knee joint through a small channel with valve-like behavior — which is precisely why they tend to refill after they are drained.
  • They travel with knee problems. Imaging studies consistently find popliteal cysts alongside meniscal tears and knee osteoarthritis, supporting the view that the cyst is a downstream consequence rather than an independent condition.
  • Many are found incidentally and stay quiet. Because they show up on MRI scans done for other reasons, researchers have noted that a substantial share of cysts cause no meaningful symptoms — a useful counterweight to the alarm the word "cyst" can provoke.
  • Addressing the underlying knee issue is the usual first step. Clinical guidance generally favors conservative care for the joint itself rather than attacking the pocket directly — an approach that happens to line up with the TCM instinct to look upstream.
  • Rupture can imitate a blood clot. Case reports describe ruptured popliteal cysts producing calf pain, swelling, and warmth that closely resemble a deep vein thrombosis. This is exactly why a suddenly swollen, painful calf deserves proper imaging rather than guesswork.
  • Acupuncture and acupressure for knee osteoarthritis. Some small trials suggest possible short-term improvement in knee pain and function with acupuncture or acupressure, though study quality varies and results are mixed. The evidence is preliminary, not conclusive.
  • Elevation, compression, and the calf pump. Standard rehabilitation guidance for venous and lymphatic return — the one place where traditional "keep the water moving" advice and modern physiotherapy agree almost word for word.
Practical Takeaways
  • Reframe the bulge: a Baker's cyst is a symptom of something happening inside the knee, not a problem living in the sac. Ask what the knee is doing differently — and when it started.
  • Find your upstream story: was there a twist, fall, or diagnosed tear (Blood Stasis)? Years of wear and stiffness (Cold-Damp on a depleted terrain)? No injury at all, just humidity and heaviness (Dampness)? Your pattern points to your levers.
  • Keep the fluid moving: ankle pumps several times daily, legs elevated in the evening, and comfortable full-range motion. Sitting still all day is the enemy of a quiet knee.
  • Match your plate to your pattern: warm cooked food and less sugar, dairy, and alcohol for Dampness; gentle cooling and draining foods during a hot flare; warming foods for a cold, stiff knee.
  • Press around, never on: use ST-36, SP-9, GB-34, KI-3, and BL-57 above and below the knee — keep fingers off the popliteal fossa and the cyst itself.
  • Take the red flags seriously: a one-sided swollen, warm, painful calf or a hot, red, feverish knee means see a doctor now, not later.
  • If the swelling came with knee pain, our companion guide on knee pain from a TCM perspective goes deeper into the joint-level patterns — and the joint pain overview maps the whole territory.

When to See a Doctor

Most popliteal cysts are benign and quiet. But several situations need professional evaluation — and a few need it quickly:

  • A suddenly swollen, warm, painful calf — especially in one leg only, with or without redness. This pattern can indicate a deep vein thrombosis, and a ruptured cyst can look almost identical. Get it assessed urgently.
  • Sudden sharp pain behind the knee followed by swelling spreading down the calf, or bruising appearing behind the knee or around the ankle — signs a cyst may have ruptured.
  • A knee that is hot, red, markedly swollen, and tender, particularly with fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell — a joint like this needs prompt medical attention.
  • The knee locks, catches, or gives way, or you cannot fully straighten it — possible meniscal or ligament involvement.
  • You cannot bear weight on the leg, or the knee is unstable under you.
  • The swelling is growing steadily, is larger than a few centimeters, or feels firm rather than soft.
  • Swelling or pain in the knee is accompanied by unexplained weight loss, night pain that wakes you, or fever — these need a proper workup.
  • The bulge appeared after knee surgery or an injection — worth having your surgeon or clinician take a look.

None of these red flags is a reason to abandon the gentler habits above — but they are a reason to have a healthcare provider in the loop. TCM is a complementary perspective, and it works best alongside proper medical assessment, not instead of it.
