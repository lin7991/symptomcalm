Loss of Smell (Anosmia) and TCM: When Scent Disappears

Symptom Guide · 10 min read

You pour your morning coffee and wait for that first warm wave of roasted bitterness. Nothing arrives. The liquid is hot, it is brown, and it is completely blank. Later, you open the trash to check whether it needed to go out, and you realize you are guessing. Then one evening you walk into the kitchen and cannot tell whether the gas burner was switched off — and that is when the fear really lands. Your sense of smell is not a luxury. It is a safety system.

Maybe this started after a bad cold, a flu, or a viral illness everyone around you called “just a cold.” Maybe it faded slowly over a year or two, so gradually that you blamed your coffee brand. Maybe it vanished in an eyeblink after you bumped your head. However it happened, the experience is strangely isolating: there is no pain, no rash, no swelling — nothing anyone can see. People nod sympathetically and then say something like “well, at least it isn’t serious,” not understanding that food has lost an entire dimension, that your own home smells like nothing, or that a memory can no longer be triggered by a scent.

There is also a quieter question that many people carry around: is this permanent? Western medicine has a partial and honest answer to that — one that depends heavily on why your smell disappeared. And Traditional Chinese Medicine offers something different again: not a promise, but a way of reading the nose as part of a larger system, which often points toward practical, low-risk habits worth trying while you wait for answers.

What this article is (and isn’t): This is a guide to the lived experience of a lost or reduced sense of smell and the TCM perspectives traditionally associated with it. It focuses specifically on the nose and the sense of smell. If you want the wider map — how TCM relates all five senses to the organs — see our companion guide to the five senses in TCM, and for the Lungs’ broader roles, our overview of the Lungs in TCM. Nothing here replaces a medical evaluation — a lost sense of smell should always be looked at by a clinician first.

The Western View: How Smell Actually Works — and How It Fails

To understand why TCM says what it says about the nose, it helps to be precise about the modern picture. Smell begins high inside the nasal cavity, in a small patch of tissue called the olfactory epithelium. Molecules floating off your coffee, your shampoo, or the rain must dissolve in the thin layer of mucus covering that tissue. They then lock onto receptor cells — roughly six million of them per nostril — which fire a signal upward through the skull’s cribriform plate into the olfactory bulb, and from there to the brain.

One detail explains a lot of the emotional weight of this condition. The olfactory pathway runs almost directly into the limbic system — the brain’s memory and emotion centre — before it reaches conscious awareness. That is why a scent can detonate a memory in a tenth of a second, and why losing smell so often feels like losing access to part of your past rather than losing a sense.

When the sense of smell goes missing, clinicians usually sort the cause into two broad buckets:

  • Obstructive (conductive) loss. Something physically blocks the air path: swelling from a cold, allergies, nasal polyps, chronic sinus disease, a deviated septum. The receptors may be perfectly healthy — the molecules simply never arrive.
  • Sensory (sensorineural) loss. The receptor cells or the wiring itself is damaged: by a virus, by a head injury, by aging, by certain medications, or as part of a neurological condition. Here the doorway is open but the signal is not being generated or carried.

Here is the genuinely hopeful part, and it is why smell training exists as a therapy rather than a folk remedy. Unlike most neurons in the adult body, the olfactory receptor cells are continuously replaced. The olfactory epithelium regenerates, which means the system retains some capacity to rebuild — slowly, over months, and usually only if it is being used.

Two facts worth knowing before anything else: first, anosmia (total loss) and hyposmia (partial loss) are both common after viral illness, and in many people they improve over weeks to months without intervention. Second, and more importantly, a persistent loss of smell can be an early signal of conditions that need medical attention — it is one of the earliest recognised signs in some neurological diseases, and sudden loss can follow head trauma or stroke. Evaluation comes first. Everything below is complementary, not a substitute.

A Different Lens: The Nose as the Lung’s Doorway

In TCM theory, the nose is described as the opening — the orifice — of the Lungs. This is not a metaphor for anatomy so much as a statement about function: the same system that moves air and defends the body’s surface is also responsible for the doorway through which scent arrives. When the Lungs are clear and their Qi moves smoothly, the nose is open, the passages are unblocked, and scent registers easily. When they are not, the nose is the first place you notice it.

The most useful analogy is a doorway with an intercom.

The doorway analogy: For a visitor to reach you, three things must work. The door itself must be clear — nothing wedged in the frame. The intercom wiring must carry the signal — a buzzing, faint, or dead line means no message arrives. And there must be someone at the desk to recognise the visitor — the signal has to be received and interpreted. In TCM terms: the nose is the door, the Lung Qi is the wiring, and classical texts go further, describing the Heart as governing the sense of smell — the receiving desk. Three points of failure, three very different experiences of “I can’t smell anything.”

This framework is why TCM does not have one answer for a lost sense of smell. A nose blocked by congestion, a nose that is clear but registering nothing, and a nose that is dry and irritated are three different situations — and in TCM theory they point toward different patterns, each with its own traditional approach.

A quick note on Qi, since the term does a lot of work here. Think of Qi as the body’s operating system: not a substance you could put in a jar, but the organising activity that lets circulation, digestion, immunity, and sensation happen in an orderly way. Where Qi moves freely, fluids move freely. Where Qi stalls, fluids pool — and pooled fluids in the upper airways are, in TCM’s language, exactly what a stuffed, scentless nose feels like.

Pattern 1: Wind-Cold or Wind-Heat at the Surface (The Door That Snapped Shut)

What it feels like

The loss of smell arrived with a cold or flu. Your nose is blocked or streaming, your head feels full, you may have sneezing, a scratchy throat, mild chills or a low fever, and a dull ache across the forehead or cheeks. If the discharge is thin and clear and you feel cold, TCM would lean toward Wind-Cold; if it is thicker, yellow, and you feel hot and irritable, toward Wind-Heat.

How TCM reads it

An external influence has arrived at the body’s surface and congested the nasal passages. Lung Qi is not moving freely, and the doorway is physically blocked. The receptor cells may be entirely fine — the message just cannot get through the door.

Traditionally used support

Warming, aromatic kitchen herbs (fresh ginger, scallion, perilla leaf), steam inhalation with a single drop of eucalyptus or peppermint oil, plenty of warm fluids, rest, and keeping the neck and feet warm. Acupressure at LI-4, LI-20 beside the nostril, and GB-20 at the base of the skull is traditionally used when the nose is congested. Cold drinks, ice, and heavy dairy tend to be set aside during this phase.

Pattern 2: Phlegm-Damp Obstructing the Orifice (The Clogged Drain)

What it feels like

This is the slow version. Mucus is thick, sticky, and constant — often dripping down the back of your throat. There is pressure across the sinuses, a foggy head, a feeling of heaviness, and smell that is muffled rather than absent. Digestion is often sluggish too: bloating, loose stools, a coated tongue, fatigue after eating. Chronic sinus trouble frequently lives here.

How TCM reads it

In TCM theory, the Spleen is responsible for transforming food and fluids into usable energy. When that process is underpowered — through a diet of cold, raw, sweet, and rich foods, or long-term fatigue — fluids are not fully converted and instead accumulate as Dampness, which thickens into Phlegm. Phlegm is heavy and sticky and tends to settle in the upper cavities. The doorway is not just shut; something is wedged in it.

Traditionally used support

Warm, cooked, simple meals; reducing dairy, sugar, fried food, and iced drinks; aromatic herbs traditionally used to transform Dampness such as tangerine peel, perilla, mint, and Job’s tears (coix seed); steam inhalation; and acupressure at ST-40 on the lower leg — a point classically associated with Phlegm — plus LI-20 and Yintang between the eyebrows. Daily walking matters here: Dampness is stagnation as much as fluid.

Pattern 3: Lung Qi Deficiency (The Faint Signal)

What it feels like

Your nose is not particularly blocked, yet smell is dim. You may have a weak or trailing voice, feel breathless climbing stairs, catch every infection going around, feel tired in a way that sleep does not fully repair, and notice that the loss of smell has lingered long after the illness itself cleared. There may be a history of allergies or repeated colds.

How TCM reads it

The doorway is open, but the wiring is underpowered. Lung Qi has not recovered its strength, so the function of the nose stays quiet. In TCM theory this is a question of reserve rather than blockage — which is why pushing harder through it, or trying to “clear” a nose that is not congested, tends to go nowhere.

Traditionally used support

Gently nourishing food: rice porridge, pear, white fungus, lotus root, cooked vegetables, almonds, and small amounts of good-quality protein. Gentle breathing practices — slow nasal breathing with a long exhale, or simple qigong — are traditionally used to rebuild Lung Qi; our guide to breathing exercises for Lung Qi walks through the mechanics. Acupressure at LU-9 at the wrist, CV-17 on the chest, and ST-36 below the knee is commonly used. Pacing, and protecting sleep, is part of the approach rather than an afterthought.

Pattern 4: Lung Dryness and Empty Heat (The Scorched Sensor)

What it feels like

The nose is dry rather than blocked — sometimes cracked, sometimes mildly crusted. The mouth and throat are dry too, especially at night. There may be a dry tickly cough, night sweats, warm palms and soles, thirst, or a feeling of heat in the afternoons. Smell may be reduced, or distorted rather than lost: coffee smells burnt, fruit smells chemical, a perfume smells like cleaning fluid. This pattern is often seen after a long viral illness or in dry climates and overheated indoor air.

How TCM reads it

Odour molecules must dissolve in the thin film of moisture covering the olfactory tissue before they can be detected. (A dry sponge cannot hold water — a dry sensor cannot hold a scent.) In TCM theory, prolonged illness, dryness in the environment, and depletion of the body’s moistening fluids leave the nasal passages without that film. Where Cold contracts and Phlegm blocks, Dryness thins — a different failure mode with a different traditional response.

Traditionally used support

Moistening foods: pear, honey in warm water, white fungus, sesame, cooked greens, and adequate fluids through the day. Humidifying the bedroom, limiting alcohol and excess caffeine, and avoiding smoke and strong chemical fumes all make obvious sense here. Acupressure at LU-9, KI-6 just below the inner ankle, and LI-20 is traditionally used for a dry nose. If dryness comes with unexplained weight loss, persistent fever, or joint symptoms, that combination deserves a medical work-up rather than self-care.

A Second, Quieter Pattern: When Smell Left After a Head Injury

If your sense of smell disappeared immediately after a blow to the head, a fall, or a car accident, this is a medical situation first and a TCM question second. In TCM theory, trauma is often described in terms of Blood Stasis — circulation that has been disrupted and does not fully resume — and traditionally that picture calls for hands-on work with a qualified practitioner rather than self-directed kitchen remedies. The reason to be firm about this is timing: head injury with smell loss, headache, confusion, drowsiness, or fluid from the nose or ears needs urgent assessment.

Smell Training: Where Modern Practice and TCM Tradition Converge

Of everything on this page, one practice has the strongest modern support and is also remarkably close in spirit to what aromatic traditions have done for centuries. It is called olfactory training, and it is refreshingly simple.

  • Choose four distinct scents from different categories. The set used in the original research — rose, lemon, eucalyptus, clove — was chosen because the four together cover floral, citrus, resinous, and spicy. Any four clearly different, familiar, safe scents work; essential oils on a cotton pad are the usual method.
  • Sniff each one for about 20 seconds, twice a day, morning and evening. Concentrate: try to recall what the smell was like when you could perceive it fully. That act of attention appears to matter.
  • Keep going for at least 12 weeks. The studies that show benefit are measured in months, not days. Regeneration is slow by design.
  • Rotate your set every 12 weeks to keep the stimulus novel.

What makes this interesting from a TCM point of view is not a coincidence in ingredients — it is the logic. TCM has a long tradition of aromatic herbs used for the nose and the upper airways: citrus peel, mint, perilla, and warming spices among them. Four centuries of aromatic practice and a modern rehabilitation protocol arrived at roughly the same four categories. The mechanism differs in each framework’s language — activity-dependent regeneration on one side, moving Qi and opening the orifice on the other — but the instruction to the reader is identical: keep presenting scent to the nose, daily, patiently.

Acupressure: Points Traditionally Used for the Nose

Acupressure is the self-directed cousin of acupuncture: steady finger pressure on the same points, for one to two minutes each, with slow breathing. It is low-risk, free, and easy to batch into a routine. The points most commonly associated with the nose and sinuses:

  • LI-20 (Yingxiang) — in the crease beside each nostril, level with the midpoint of the nostril. The local point for nasal congestion and a blocked doorway.
  • Yintang (EX-HN3) — between the eyebrows. Traditionally used for sinus pressure and to settle a foggy head.
  • LI-4 (Hegu) — in the webbing between thumb and index finger. The general point for the face and head, traditionally used for anything affecting the nose.
  • GB-20 (Fengchi) — at the base of the skull, in the hollows either side of the neck. Traditionally used when an external influence is involved.
  • ST-40 (Fenglong) — halfway down the outer lower leg. The classical point associated with Phlegm.
  • LU-9 (Taiyuan) — at the wrist crease on the thumb side. The point associated with the Lungs’ underlying strength.
  • KI-6 (Zhaohai) — just below the inner ankle. Traditionally used for dryness of the throat and nose.
Safety notes for acupressure: LI-4 is traditionally avoided during pregnancy, as are the strong points on the lower leg and ankle — if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, check with a qualified practitioner first. Do not press over broken, infected, or recently operated skin, and do not press into a site after facial or sinus surgery without medical clearance. Stop if a point causes sharp pain rather than a dull, spreading sensation. And keep pressure gentle: this is not a technique where force equals results.

What Research Suggests

It is worth separating what is well supported from what is promising but uncertain, because a lost sense of smell has a habit of attracting confident claims.

  • Olfactory training: this is the best-supported self-directed practice in this article. Randomised trials and meta-analyses, particularly in people with smell loss after viral infection, suggest modest improvement compared with no training. Effects are real but usually partial, and the mechanism is generally explained as activity-driven regeneration of olfactory receptor neurons.
  • Recovery after viral illness: most people who lose smell after a viral infection recover at least partially over weeks to months, though the proportion and the timeline vary widely between studies and between viral variants. Persistent loss beyond several months is less common — and more likely to need specialist input.
  • Acupuncture and acupressure for smell: small studies — including work on post-viral olfactory dysfunction — have reported improvement in smell function after acupuncture. Sample sizes are generally small, and blinding a needle intervention is difficult, so these findings are best described as interesting rather than established.
  • Zinc and supplements: this is a good example of why it pays to check. Controlled trials of zinc for smell loss have mostly failed to show benefit, and prolonged intranasal zinc has been linked to harm to the sense of smell itself. Some studies suggest that certain nutrients matter for nerve health in general, but the specific supplement-and-olfaction evidence is weak.
  • Smell loss and neurological disease: research consistently associates early, unexplained smell loss with neurodegenerative conditions including Parkinson’s disease, where it is often present years before movement symptoms. This association is one of the strongest reasons a persistent anosmia deserves a proper medical look rather than a purely DIY approach.
  • Conventional avenues for obstructive causes: where the blockage is mechanical — polyps, chronic sinus disease, structural narrowing — medical or surgical management is well established and genuinely capable of restoring airflow. TCM is not in competition with that; it is complementary.

Notice the pattern: the modern evidence and the traditional advice converge on the same practical instruction — keep the nose clear, keep it moist, keep presenting aroma to it daily, and be patient over months rather than days.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  1. Get the medical picture first. Sudden loss, one-sided loss, loss after head injury, or loss alongside neurological symptoms all need clinical assessment. A lost sense of smell is also an early marker for some conditions, which is exactly why it should not be self-managed in isolation.
  2. Start smell training today. Four distinct scents, 20 seconds each, twice daily, twelve weeks minimum, rotating your set every three months. This is the single best-supported practice here.
  3. Identify which pattern fits you. Congestion with a cold points toward Wind at the surface. Thick mucus, heaviness, and sluggish digestion point toward Phlegm-Damp. Fatigue and a weak voice with a clear but unresponsive nose point toward Lung Qi deficiency. Dryness with night sweats points toward Dryness and Empty Heat. Match the approach to the pattern rather than trying everything.
  4. Batch your acupressure. LI-20, Yintang, and LI-4 twice a day for one to two minutes each, adding ST-40 for Phlegm, LU-9 for weakness, or KI-6 for dryness. Skip the pregnancy-contraindicated points if applicable, and never press over damaged skin.
  5. Protect your safety while smell is reduced. Check that smoke and gas alarms work, label leftovers with dates rather than trusting your nose, run the extractor fan, and ask someone else to judge food that is questionable. This is the part of anosmia that carries real risk.
  6. Keep the nose warm, moist, and clear of irritants. Warm cooked meals, adequate fluids, steam when congested, humidification when dry, and no smoking — simple measures that support every pattern described above.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ Seek emergency medical care immediately if your loss of smell follows a head injury — especially with a severe headache, confusion, drowsiness, vomiting, or clear fluid running from the nose or ears; if it comes with facial droop, weakness on one side, slurred speech, or sudden vision changes; or if it comes with difficulty breathing or swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat.

See a healthcare provider if:

  • The loss lasts more than two to four weeks without a clear explanation such as an active cold
  • It affects only one nostril, or comes with persistent nasal obstruction, frequent nosebleeds, or pain in one sinus area
  • It started gradually and steadily rather than suddenly, especially with progressive change over months
  • It is accompanied by tremor, slowing of movement, changes in handwriting, memory changes, or mood change
  • It follows a new medication, or you have a condition known to affect smell, such as diabetes, thyroid disease, or an immune condition
  • You have chronic sinus symptoms, nasal polyps, or allergies that are no longer responding to your usual management
  • Symptoms of smell distortion (things smelling burnt, chemical, or rotten) appear, or smells that others do not notice
  • It is affecting your appetite, weight, mood, or safety at home — or you are simply unsure what is causing it
