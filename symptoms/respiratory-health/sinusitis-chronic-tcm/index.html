Chronic Sinusitis Through a TCM Lens

Symptom Guide · 10 min read

When “Acute” Becomes “Chronic”

You had a cold six weeks ago. The fever faded, the sore throat passed, but something in your sinuses never quite cleared. There is a persistent pressure behind your cheekbones — not quite painful, but always present. Your nose runs clear fluid some days, thick yellow discharge on others. You use a saline rinse, try a decongestant, and it helps for a few hours. Then the heaviness returns.

If this pattern sounds familiar, you are not alone. Chronic sinusitis affects an estimated 11–12% of the adult population in Western countries, making it one of the most common chronic health conditions. In medical terms, it is defined as inflammation of the sinus cavities lasting twelve weeks or longer despite treatment. In real terms, it means months of low-grade discomfort, disrupted sleep, recurring headaches, and the nagging feeling that your body is not fully recovering from a simple cold.

The standard approach — saline rinses, intranasal steroids, antibiotics for acute bacterial episodes — manages symptoms but often fails to address why the sinuses remain vulnerable. This is where Traditional Chinese Medicine offers a complementary perspective. Rather than focusing solely on the current inflammation, TCM asks: what condition in your body allows the sinuses to remain congested even when there is no active infection?

For a broader overview of how the Lung system works in TCM, see our guide to Lung Qi. This article focuses specifically on the chronic pattern of sinusitis and the practical approaches that may support long-term sinus health.

Why Standard Approaches Often Fall Short for Chronic Cases

From a Western perspective, chronic sinusitis differs from acute sinusitis in both duration and underlying mechanism. Acute sinusitis is typically driven by a viral or bacterial infection that resolves with time or antibiotics. Chronic sinusitis involves persistent inflammation of the sinus mucosa, often without a clear infectious trigger. Contributing factors may include nasal polyps, deviated septum, fungal elements, allergic rhinitis, or simply a hyper-reactive inflammatory response.

First-line treatments include nasal corticosteroid sprays, saline irrigation, and, in some cases, endoscopic sinus surgery to improve drainage. Antibiotics are reserved for acute exacerbations but are not recommended for chronic sinusitis without clear evidence of bacterial infection.

The limitation of this approach is that it treats the sinus tissue itself as the problem — the inflammation must be suppressed, the drainage improved, the obstruction removed. But in many chronic cases, the inflammation recurs because the underlying conditions that produced it have not changed. TCM offers a different framework: rather than asking “what is inflamed?” it asks “why does the inflammation persist?”

An analogy: Think of a chronically damp basement. You can run a dehumidifier (nasal spray) every day, and the basement will feel drier while the machine is running. But if the foundation has a crack that lets groundwater seep in, the dampness will return as soon as the dehumidifier stops. TCM looks for the crack in the foundation — the systemic imbalance that keeps the sinuses vulnerable.

The Spleen-Lung Connection in Chronic Sinusitis

In TCM theory, two organ systems are primarily involved in chronic sinusitis: the Lung and the Spleen.

The Lung governs the skin and body surface (the Wei Qi, or defensive layer) and opens into the nose. When Lung Qi is strong, the nasal passages remain clear, and external pathogens are easily expelled. When Lung Qi is weak, the nose becomes a point of vulnerability — the first place pathogens settle after entering the body.

The Spleen is responsible for transforming food into Qi and Blood, and for managing fluid metabolism. When the Spleen functions well, fluids move through the body efficiently and are eliminated properly. When the Spleen is weak — often from poor diet, overthinking, or chronic stress — fluids accumulate and become turbid. This turbid fluid, called Dampness in TCM, tends to rise upward and settle in the sinuses.

The connection between the two is critical: the Lung provides the defense and the clearing mechanism (descending Qi that moves fluids down and out), while the Spleen determines how much Dampness the body produces in the first place. Chronic sinusitis is the clinical picture of an overwhelmed Lung trying to clear more Dampness than the Spleen should be producing.

Western parallel: This Spleen-Lung interaction mirrors the modern understanding of the gut-lung axis. The gut microbiome influences immune responses in the respiratory tract, and digestive health impacts mucosal immunity. A 2020 review in Frontiers in Immunology noted that alterations in gut microbiota composition have been associated with chronic respiratory conditions, including chronic rhinosinusitis. While the mechanisms differ in their details, both frameworks see a connection between digestion and respiratory health.

Three TCM Patterns of Chronic Sinusitis

While the underlying mechanism often involves the Spleen-Lung axis, chronic sinusitis can present in several distinct TCM patterns. Recognizing which pattern fits your experience helps determine the most relevant approach.

Pattern 1: Spleen Qi Deficiency with Lingering Dampness

Spleen Deficiency with Dampness

You might have this pattern if: Your sinus congestion is persistent but low-grade — not completely blocked, but never fully clear. The mucus is whitish or clear but sticky. You feel fatigued, especially after meals. Your digestion is sluggish: you bloat easily, your stools are loose or unformed, and you have a sense of heaviness in your body. Your tongue may look pale with a white, greasy coating.

TCM view: The Spleen is failing to transform fluids efficiently. Dampness accumulates in the body and rises to the head, settling in the sinus cavities. This is not an active infection — it is a chronic state of fluid stagnation that creates an environment where pathogens can settle.

Traditional approach: Strengthening the Spleen to address the root cause. Dietary changes are central: warm cooked foods, soups, congee (rice porridge), ginger tea. Avoiding raw foods, cold drinks, dairy, and excessive sweets. Herbal formulas that support the Spleen and transform Dampness, such as variations of Liu Jun Zi Tang (Six Gentlemen Decoction), may be considered by a qualified practitioner.

Pattern 2: Lung Qi Deficiency with Weak Wei Qi

Lung Qi and Wei Qi Deficiency

You might have this pattern if: Your chronic sinusitis is marked by frequent colds that always settle in your sinuses. You are sensitive to drafts, cold weather, and wind. You feel short of breath easily, have a weak or soft voice, and sweat spontaneously with minimal exertion. Your sinus symptoms worsen with cold exposure and improve with warmth. You may have a pale complexion and feel tired much of the time.

TCM view: The Lung Qi is too weak to maintain a strong defensive barrier (Wei Qi) around the body. External pathogens penetrate easily, and the Lung cannot expel them fully. The sinuses become the depot where these incompletely expelled pathogens accumulate, leading to chronic inflammation.

Traditional approach: Strengthening Lung Qi and supporting the Wei Qi. In TCM theory, the interplay between Lung Qi and external defenses determines the body's ability to ward off respiratory infections. A qualified TCM practitioner may recommend formulas traditionally used to support respiratory resilience, along with lifestyle adjustments that protect against wind and cold.

Pattern 3: Damp-Heat Stagnation in the Sinus Cavities

Damp-Heat Stagnation

You might have this pattern if: Your chronic sinusitis has periodic flare-ups with thick, yellow, or green discharge. Between flare-ups you feel some congestion but not the acute symptoms. You experience facial pressure, particularly when leaning forward. You may have a sensation of heat in your face, bad breath, and a bitter taste in your mouth. Your tongue may have a yellow, greasy coating.

TCM view: Dampness has accumulated in the sinuses over time, and the stagnation has generated Heat. This creates a cycle: the Heat thickens the fluids, making them harder to clear, and the stagnant fluids provide fuel for the Heat to persist. Acute flare-ups occur when external triggers (a cold, allergens, dietary indiscretions) add to the existing burden.

Traditional approach: Clearing Damp-Heat while supporting the body's drainage mechanisms. Cooling, aromatic herbs that open the nasal passages and dry Dampness are traditionally used. A qualified practitioner might consider formulas containing Xanthium sibiricum (cang er zi) and Magnolia biondii (xin yi hua), which have a history of use for sinus congestion in TCM.

Mixed patterns are common: In chronic sinusitis, it is normal to have a primary pattern (e.g., Spleen Deficiency producing Dampness) with periodic shifts into a secondary pattern (e.g., Damp-Heat during an acute flare-up). This is why working with a qualified practitioner matters — the treatment strategy shifts between flare-ups and maintenance phases.

Pattern-Specific Dietary Strategies

Diet is one of the most accessible tools for supporting sinus health from a TCM perspective. The right foods depend on which pattern fits your experience:

For Spleen Deficiency with Dampness

  • Prioritize: Warm cooked foods — soups, stews, congee (rice porridge), steamed vegetables, ginger tea, cinnamon, and small amounts of roasted grains like barley or millet.
  • Reduce or avoid: Raw salads, cold drinks (including iced water), dairy products, excessive fruit (especially cold-natured fruits like watermelon or oranges), sugar, and refined carbohydrates.
  • Beneficial ingredients: Job's tears (coix seed / yi yi ren), pumpkin, sweet potato, lotus root, and small amounts of lean meat in broths.

For Lung Qi Deficiency

  • Prioritize: Foods traditionally believed to support Lung Qi — pears (steamed or poached), white fungus (snow fungus), almonds, honey, oatmeal, and well-cooked root vegetables.
  • Reduce or avoid: Spicy foods that may scatter Qi, excessive raw foods, cold drinks, and foods that create Dampness (dairy, fried foods, sugar).
  • Beneficial ingredients: Astragalus root (huang qi) can be simmered in soups or broths — it is one of the most widely used herbs in TCM for supporting Wei Qi. A slice of dried astragalus simmered in chicken or vegetable broth for 30–40 minutes adds a subtle earthy flavor and is traditionally used for respiratory resilience.

For Damp-Heat Stagnation

  • Prioritize: Cooling and drying foods — leafy greens (celery, watercress, Chinese greens), cucumber, mung bean soup, daikon radish, bitter melon (in moderation), and green tea.
  • Reduce or avoid: Fried foods, greasy foods, alcohol, spicy or pungent foods (which can generate Heat), and foods high in sugar.
  • Beneficial ingredients: Chrysanthemum tea (ju hua), dandelion greens, and barley water are traditionally used to help clear Damp-Heat from the head and face.

Acupressure and Self-Care for Chronic Sinusitis

Acupressure offers a gentle, accessible way to support sinus drainage and respiratory function between flare-ups. These points can be pressed daily, not just when symptoms are active:

  • LI20 (Ying Xiang / Welcome Fragrance) — In the groove on either side of the nostrils. Press gently for 1–2 minutes, breathing deeply. This is the classic point for opening the nasal passages.
  • GB20 (Feng Chi / Wind Pool) — In the hollows at the base of the skull, between the neck muscles. This point is traditionally used to release external Wind and clear head congestion. Especially relevant for the Lung Qi Deficiency pattern (frequent colds).
  • ST36 (Zu San Li / Leg Three Mile) — Below the knee, about four finger-widths down from the kneecap, one finger-width outward from the shin bone. This point is traditionally used to strengthen the Spleen and support Qi production. Press daily regardless of symptoms for the Spleen Deficiency pattern.
  • LU7 (Lie Que / Broken Sequence) — On the thumb side of the forearm, about 1.5 inches above the wrist crease, in the depression above the radial bone. This point is on the Lung channel and is traditionally used to support the descending function of Lung Qi, helping move fluids down and out.
  • REN22 (Tiantu / Sky Prominence) — In the hollow at the base of the throat, above the breastbone. Press gently for 30 seconds. This point is traditionally used to help clear Phlegm from the upper respiratory tract.

Steam Inhalation with a TCM Twist

Steam inhalation is a well-known home remedy for sinus congestion. From a TCM perspective, you can enhance it by adding aromatic herbs traditionally believed to open the nasal passages. Add a few drops of peppermint or eucalyptus essential oil to a bowl of hot water, drape a towel over your head, and inhale the steam for 5–10 minutes. The aromatic, dispersing quality of these herbs aligns with the TCM strategy of using aromatic substances to resolve Dampness and move stagnation.

Environmental Factors That May Perpetuate Chronic Sinusitis

In TCM theory, the environment and the body are in constant interaction. For chronic sinusitis, several external factors may contribute to the persistence of symptoms:

  • Mold and damp living environments: Living or working in a damp environment adds an external Dampness burden to any internal Dampness pattern. A dehumidifier in humid climates, or addressing mold issues in bathrooms and basements, can reduce the external load on your sinus system.
  • Air conditioning and drafts: In TCM theory, the Lung is particularly vulnerable to Cold and Wind. Sitting under an air conditioning vent for hours, sleeping in a draft, or driving with the window down on a cold day can allow Wind-Cold to penetrate the Wei Qi and settle in the sinuses.
  • Seasonal transitions: Chronic sinusitis often worsens during spring and autumn — the transitional seasons when Wind is most prevalent in TCM theory. Being mindful of layered clothing and protecting the back of the neck (GB20 area) during these seasons may help reduce flare-ups.
  • Air quality: Air pollution, smoke, and strong chemical fumes directly burden the Lung system. Using an air purifier in high-pollution environments or during wildfire season may reduce the daily load on your respiratory defenses.

What Research Suggests

Research on TCM approaches for chronic sinusitis has been growing, though larger and more rigorous trials are needed:

  • A 2020 systematic review in Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine examined 15 randomized controlled trials on Chinese herbal medicine for chronic rhinosinusitis. Formulas containing Xanthium sibiricum (cang er zi), Magnolia biondii (xin yi hua), and Angelica dahurica (bai zhi) showed potential for improving symptom scores compared to conventional therapy alone, though the authors emphasized the need for larger trials with standardized outcome measures.
  • A 2018 clinical study in Annals of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology found that adding acupuncture to standard saline irrigation showed greater improvement in chronic rhinosinusitis symptoms than saline irrigation alone, with participants reporting better nasal airflow and reduced facial pressure.
  • Laboratory studies have explored the anti-inflammatory properties of individual herbs commonly used for sinus conditions. Xanthium sibiricum extracts have demonstrated anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial activity in vitro, while Magnolia biondii has shown mast cell-stabilizing effects in some experimental models.
  • A 2021 review in Frontiers in Pharmacology examined the role of gut-lung axis interactions in respiratory health, noting that the relationship between digestive function and respiratory immunity has parallels in both traditional and modern medical frameworks.

This research is promising but preliminary. TCM approaches should be used as part of a comprehensive strategy alongside conventional medical care.

Practical Takeaways

Start here: Five steps you can take today to support chronic sinus health:
  1. Identify your dominant pattern using the descriptions above. Is your congestion accompanied by fatigue and poor digestion (Spleen Deficiency), frequent colds and sensitivity to wind (Lung Qi Deficiency), or yellow discharge with facial heat (Damp-Heat)? This determines where to focus.
  2. Adjust one meal a day. If Spleen Deficiency fits, replace one cold meal (salad, sandwich) with a warm cooked meal (soup, congee, stew) for one week and notice changes in your sinus congestion and digestion.
  3. Press ST36 daily. This point supports both Lung and Spleen function. Two minutes on each leg, once in the morning, whether you have symptoms or not. Consistency matters more than pressure intensity.
  4. Check your environment. Address one environmental factor this week — run a dehumidifier if your home is damp, or move your desk away from a direct air conditioning vent.
  5. Support respiratory resilience. For a deeper look at how TCM supports lung function, see our guide to Lung Qi and our article on building immune resilience from a TCM perspective.

When to See a Doctor

While TCM offers supportive approaches for chronic sinus health, some symptoms require medical evaluation. Please seek medical attention if:

  • Your symptoms persist longer than 12 weeks despite self-care measures
  • You develop a high fever (above 101°F / 38.3°C) with sinus pain
  • You experience vision changes, double vision, or swelling around the eyes
  • You have a severe headache that does not respond to over-the-counter pain relief
  • You notice blood in your nasal discharge
  • You develop neck stiffness, confusion, or difficulty breathing

If you are currently taking antibiotics, nasal corticosteroids, or other medications, do not stop or change your dosage without consulting your healthcare provider. TCM approaches can be used alongside conventional treatment, but always inform your doctor of any complementary therapies you are exploring.
