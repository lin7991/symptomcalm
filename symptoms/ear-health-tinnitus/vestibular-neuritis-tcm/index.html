Vestibular Neuritis in TCM: Recovering From a Sudden Spin

Symptom Guide · 12 min read

You woke up and the room was turning. Not the light, seasick head-rush of standing up too fast — this was full rotational spinning, the kind that pins you to the mattress because every attempt to sit up throws the world sideways. Within an hour you were nauseous. By evening you could not walk unaided. Three days later the worst of it has passed, but the floor still feels like the deck of a boat and you are afraid to turn your head too quickly.

If that sounds like your week, you may be in the middle of vestibular neuritis — inflammation of the vestibular nerve, usually on one side, often arriving after a cold, flu, or other viral illness. It is one of the most frightening things the inner ear can do to a person, and one of the most misunderstood in its aftermath: the violent spinning subsides, but the ground can stay unreliable for weeks or months.

This guide walks through the four phases of that experience — the acute storm, the recalibration window, the rebuilding stage, and the long tail — and where TCM theory offers a complementary, practical perspective for the recovery period. One thing up front: the first phase belongs to medical assessment, not self-care. We will be clear about where that line sits.

Already reading about dizziness? This article is specifically about a one-sided vestibular nerve event and the recalibration that follows. For the broader map of dizziness patterns, see Vertigo and Dizziness: TCM Insights into Balance. If your episodes come with fluctuating hearing loss and ear fullness, see Ménière's Disease in TCM. If the dizziness arrives alongside head pain, light sensitivity, or visual aura, see Vestibular Migraine. And for the underlying concept, our TCM Basics guide to Internal Wind explains why TCM groups sudden, moving symptoms under a single heading.

Why Vestibular Neuritis Isn't Ménière's, Migraine, or "Just Dizziness"

These conditions share a symptom and almost nothing else. The differences matter, because they change what your recovery looks like and who you need to see.

Feature Vestibular neuritis Ménière's disease Vestibular migraine
Onset Single, sudden event over hours Recurrent episodes, sometimes with warning Recurrent, often with headache
Duration Continuous for days, then fading 20 minutes to a few hours, then normal Minutes to days
Hearing Usually normal Fluctuating loss, tinnitus, fullness Usually normal
Between episodes Unsteadiness can linger for weeks Often symptom-free Often symptom-free
Common triggers Recent viral illness Salt, fluid shifts, stress Stress, sleep loss, skipped meals, hormones, alcohol

The single most important structural detail about vestibular neuritis is that it is usually a one-sided event. One vestibular nerve stops carrying its signal well. The other ear keeps working normally — which, paradoxically, is exactly the problem.

The Calibration Problem: Two Gyroscopes, One Control Room

Picture your balance system as a pair of gyroscopes — one inside each inner ear — both wired into a single control room in the brainstem. At rest, they send identical readings, and the control room stays calm.

Now imagine one gyroscope going quiet. The control room does not read "silence." It reads the mismatch. If the left gyroscope says moving left and the right one says still, the brain splits the difference and concludes that the world itself is rotating. That conclusion is what you feel as relentless, room-spinning vertigo.

A second way to picture it: a stereo with one speaker blown. The surviving speaker still works perfectly — but every sound now seems to come from one side, and your brain has to relearn how to locate the source.

Here is the encouraging implication. Recovery from vestibular neuritis is not mainly about the nerve. It is about the control room: the brain gradually re-weighting the surviving signal, damping the mismatch, and rebuilding trust in the ear that still works. In the medical literature this process is called vestibular compensation, and it is the target of vestibular rehabilitation. It is also why lying perfectly still for weeks can slow you down — the control room only recalibrates when it receives new data.

Where TCM Fits: Wind at the Front, Resources at the Back

TCM has a category for symptoms that arrive suddenly, spin, and shift like weather: Internal Wind. In TCM theory, anything that moves abruptly, wobbles, or comes and goes without warning — tremor, tics, sudden dizziness — is grouped under Wind. Vertigo that appears over hours and rotates the whole visual field is the textbook example.

The ears themselves are described as the "window" or gateway of the Kidney system, while mental clarity is linked to the marrow and the body's deep reserves (Jing). Put those two ideas together and a pattern emerges that matches what people actually experience:

  • The front end is Wind — sudden, rotational, moving. Disturbance at the sensory gateway.
  • The middle stretch is turbidity — the heavy-headed fog, nausea, and land-sickness that follow the storm, traditionally read as Phlegm-Damp clouding the senses.
  • The back end is resources — how much Qi, Blood, and reserve you can spend on the rebuilding work the brain has to do.

That progression — storm, fog, rebuild — is also, conveniently, the actual clinical timeline of vestibular neuritis. Which is why this article is organized by phase rather than by pattern. In each phase, the TCM contribution changes.

Phase 1 — The Acute Storm (First 24 to 72 Hours): Medical Assessment Leads

What is happening: continuous severe vertigo, nystagmus (the eyes beating involuntarily to one side), nausea and often vomiting, and an inability to stand or walk without support. Hearing is typically unaffected. If hearing is affected, the picture may be labyrinthitis rather than vestibular neuritis — a distinction a clinician makes, not a search engine.

The medical priority in this phase is ruling out a stroke. Acute vestibular syndrome — the sudden onset of continuous vertigo with nausea and unsteady gait — can also be caused by a circulation problem in the brainstem or cerebellum. Emergency clinicians use a bedside set of eye tests known as HINTS (head impulse, nystagmus type, test of skew) precisely because those signs can separate a peripheral inner-ear cause from a central one more reliably than an early scan in some situations. Ask for an urgent evaluation. Same day, not next week.

Depending on the assessment, a clinician may offer medication for nausea and dizziness, intravenous fluids if you cannot keep anything down, and in some cases a short course of corticosteroids. This is a clinical decision — there is genuine debate in the literature about how much benefit steroids provide — and it is not something to arrange for yourself.

Seek emergency care immediately if vertigo comes with any of the following: sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body; facial droop; slurred speech or trouble swallowing; double vision; a severe or thunderclap headache; new difficulty walking or loss of coordination; confusion, fainting, or loss of consciousness; vertigo with fever and a stiff neck. These can point to a stroke, bleeding, or a serious infection — none of which are self-care problems.

What TCM Does Not Do in This Phase

It is worth saying plainly: this is not the moment for herbal formulas, self-acupressure routines, or "pushing through." When you are actively spinning, the body is in a dysregulated state and the risk of a fall is real. TCM's contribution during the acute storm is essentially nil — and any practitioner who tells you otherwise is not being straight with you.

What may support comfort while you wait for medical care is unglamorous but useful:

  • Reduce the mismatch. Lie still in a dim, quiet room. Fix your gaze on one stationary object rather than a moving screen or a busy room. Closing your eyes often helps in this phase; later, it will not.
  • Clear a safe path. Remove rugs and clutter between your bed and the bathroom, keep a light on at night, and avoid stairs without help. Falls, not the nerve itself, cause most acute harm.
  • Do not drive, cycle, cook on a hob, or use power tools until a clinician has cleared you.
  • Sip, do not gulp. Small amounts of warm water or diluted ginger tea may sit better than cold drinks, and repeated vomiting is a reason to seek care for fluids.
  • Gentle PC-6 pressure only while seated or lying down — two finger-widths above the wrist crease on the inner forearm, between the two tendons. In TCM, PC-6 is traditionally used for nausea. It is a small comfort measure, not a substitute for anti-emetics, and you should never press it while standing or driving.

Phase 2 — The Recalibration Window (Days 3 to 14): Turbid Phlegm Misting the Head

By the end of the first week, the spinning usually stops being continuous. What replaces it is stranger and harder to explain to other people: the ground feels spongy, the head feels packed with cotton, and certain environments become unbearable. Supermarket aisles. Busy patterns. Scrolling on a phone. Quick head turns. Cars, lifts, escalators. You are no longer spinning — you are unreliable.

At the same time, the brain is doing its most important work: slowly down-regulating the mismatched signal and re-weighting the healthy ear. This is the window where recovery is genuinely influenced by what you do.

The TCM Reading: Phlegm-Damp Misting the Head

In TCM theory, the Spleen transforms food and fluid into usable Qi and Blood, and also keeps fluids moving. When the Spleen's transforming function is sluggish — from a viral illness, antibiotics, disrupted eating, exhaustion, or a run of heavy food — fluids can gather into what TCM calls Phlegm-Damp: turbidity that rises to cloud the "clear orifices" of the head.

The classic signature is almost a word-for-word description of this phase: heavy head, foggy thinking, nausea, a sense of swaying, worse in damp weather and after heavy or greasy meals. If you have ever felt worse after a takeaway and better after broth, you have felt the pattern from the inside.

What traditionally tends to help in the "fog" phase

In TCM theory, this phase responds to lightening and drying — not to rich rebuilding food. The temptation is to eat "nutritious" heavy meals. For a digestive system that is already struggling to transform fluids, that can make the fog denser.

Reduce for a couple of weeks: fried and greasy food, heavy cream and cheese, very sweet desserts, alcohol, iced drinks, and large late meals.

Favour instead: warm cooked food in smaller, more frequent portions. Rice or millet congee, clear broths with ginger, steamed vegetables, a little fish or chicken, barley, and ginger or mint tea. Bland is not boring here — it is lighter work for the system.

The Most Counter-Intuitive Rule of This Phase: Move

When the world feels unreliable, every instinct says be still. Rest is protective in Phase 1. In Phase 2, prolonged stillness works against you, because the brain's recalibration depends on receiving movement information and noticing that the mismatch is survivable.

Vestibular rehabilitation programmes formalize this into specific exercises — but the principle itself is simple, and both traditions agree on it: short, frequent, graded exposure, just at the edge of comfort.

  • Walk indoors for two minutes, several times a day, touching a wall if needed. Increase slowly.
  • Practise slow, deliberate head turns while seated, then while standing, then while walking — small ranges first.
  • Keep your eyes on the horizon, not on the floor, when walking a straight line. Balancing on your vision alone slows recalibration.
  • Stop before nausea escalates. A 3-out-of-10 wobble repeated daily does more than a 9-out-of-10 session once.

In TCM terms this is the practical half of the same insight: gentle, regular movement keeps Qi and fluids moving, while long stillness lets turbidity settle. The two frameworks are describing one body.

Phase 3 — Building (Weeks 2 to 8): Where TCM and Vestibular Rehab Quietly Agree

This is when most people begin to notice real improvement — and when the second problem usually appears: fatigue. Walking the dog that used to be nothing now leaves you flattened for the rest of the day. Screens are tiring. Crowds are exhausting. Concentration slips. That is not weakness of character. It is the energy cost of running a brain in recalibration mode.

The Conventional Framework

Vestibular rehabilitation in this phase typically involves three components: gaze stabilization (keeping your eyes locked on a target while your head moves), habituation (repeated, controlled exposure to the specific movements that provoke symptoms, until the brain stops over-reacting), and balance training (progressively narrowing your base of support, changing surfaces, and reducing visual reliance). Systematic reviews of this approach in unilateral peripheral vestibular dysfunction generally support it, with the strongest gains in the early weeks.

The TCM Reading: Rebuilding the Reserve

Where Phase 2 was about clearing turbidity, Phase 3 is about replenishment. In TCM theory, sustained recovery work draws on Spleen Qi (the fuel extracted from food), Blood (which nourishes sensory and nervous tissue), and Kidney essence (the deepest reserve). The observable signature of the deficiency pattern is that particular flatness after modest effort, poor stamina, and slow recovery from a busy day.

Rebuilding habits that both frameworks would recognize as sensible

Eat to build: warm, cooked, protein-forward meals at regular times. Eggs, fish, chicken, bone broth, cooked greens, black beans, black sesame, goji berries, and a little whole grain. Skipping meals spikes the instability.

Think of sleep as structural, not optional: the brain consolidates compensation during sleep. A consistent sleep window matters more than a perfect one.

Do not empty the reserve: no alcohol while you are still unstable, no caffeine see-saw, no intense training sessions that leave you wrecked for two days.

Keep the challenge small and daily: three short rehab sessions beat one heroic one. This is the same advice a vestibular physiotherapist gives, for the same reason.

A Shared Table: What Each Rehab Component Is Actually Doing

Rehab exercise Conventional reading TCM reading
Gaze stabilization (eyes fixed on target, head moving) Retrains the vestibulo-ocular reflex so vision stops jittering Settles residual Wind and restores coordination between the senses and the sensory gateways
Habituation (repeated exposure to provoking movements) Reduces the brain's exaggerated response to safe motion Clears the "turbidity" that has settled over the head — familiar motion replaces an alarm response

Phase 4 — The Long Tail (Beyond 8 Weeks): Residual Dizziness and Decompensation

Many people are close to normal within one to three months. Others are left with something subtler: a lingering sensitivity to motion, crowds, or busy environments; a wobble on uneven ground; a nervous hyper-awareness of their own balance. Clinicians have a name for the persistent functional version of this picture — persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD) — in which the brain stays on high alert about balance long after the original injury has settled.

The useful concept here is decompensation. The system has a margin. Push it past that margin — a run of bad sleep, alcohol, another illness, a stressful month, an unusually demanding week — and symptoms can briefly return even though nothing new is wrong with the ear. This is well recognized after vestibular injury, and it is why the everyday habits matter more than any single intervention.

The TCM Reading: Residual Wind and an Unsettled Shen

Two threads come to the front in this phase:

  • Residual Wind that has not fully settled — the intermittent, weather-linked wobble, worse when tired.
  • Kidney-Liver Yin depletion with an unsettled Shen — the hyper-vigilant, "always checking my balance" mental state that keeps the alarm on. In TCM theory, chronic vigilance is understood as consuming the very reserves the body needs to complete its recovery, which creates the loop familiar to anyone who has spent months afraid of falling.

Practically, this means the long tail is as much about the nervous system's threat level as about the inner ear:

  • Do not shrink your world. Avoiding buses, shops, and uneven ground teaches the brain that those places are dangerous. Graded exposure is the way out of the vigilance loop.
  • Work on the down-shift. Slow breathing with a longer exhale, a regular wind-down routine, and daylight early in the day all lower the baseline alarm level. See our guide to vagal tone for the mechanics.
  • Protect sleep and hydration honestly. They are the two variables most likely to tip you back over the margin.
  • If you are still wobbly after three months, ask specifically about vestibular rehabilitation and about an assessment for PPPD. Both are real, named, manageable problems with structured help available.

Acupressure Across the Recovery Phases

Safety first. If you are actively spinning, sit or lie down before you press anything. Never press while standing, walking, or driving. Skip any point that makes your symptoms worse — responses vary. If you are pregnant, note that LI-4 and SP-6 are traditionally avoided during pregnancy, so speak with your healthcare provider before using acupressure. Acupressure is a comfort and support practice, not a substitute for medical care.

Acupressure is the most portable TCM practice available to you during recovery: no equipment, no appointment, no side effects to weigh. Use pressure that feels like a firm, steady thumb — not pain — for one to two minutes per point, on both sides where relevant.

  • GB-20 (Fengchi) — in the two hollows at the base of the skull, between the neck muscles. Traditionally used for Wind-related dizziness and head symptoms; a good first point when the wobble flares.
  • Yintang — midway between the eyebrows. Traditionally used to settle the Shen; helpful for the anxious vigilance of Phase 4.
  • PC-6 (Neiguan) — two finger-widths above the wrist crease on the inner forearm, between the tendons. Traditionally used for nausea; the most studied acupressure point in this area.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width outside the shinbone. Traditionally used to strengthen digestion and build Qi — the Phase 3 rebuilding point.
  • ST-40 (Fenglong) — midway between the knee and ankle on the outer lower leg. Traditionally associated with transforming Phlegm — the classic Phase 2 point.
  • KI-3 (Taixi) — in the depression between the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon. Traditionally used to nourish Kidney reserves and support the ears.

A five-minute daily sequence

1. Sit with both feet flat on the floor. Thirty slow breaths, exhaling longer than you inhale.

2. GB-20, both sides, one minute. (Skip this one if you have recently had neck surgery or neck injury.)

3. Yintang, one minute, with light pressure.

4. ST-36 and ST-40 on each leg, one minute each.

5. KI-3 on each ankle, thirty seconds each, closing with three slow breaths.

Done once a day at a regular time, this becomes a useful daily check-in on how steady you feel — and an honest record of progress when recovery feels invisible.

When to See a Doctor

Vestibular neuritis is a clinical diagnosis, and the acute phase in particular needs proper assessment. Seek medical care promptly for any of the following:

  • Any first episode of sudden severe vertigo — this needs urgent evaluation to exclude a stroke or other central cause, especially with vascular risk factors, headache, double vision, or trouble speaking.
  • New or one-sided hearing loss — sudden hearing loss in one ear is a medical urgency and should be assessed within days, not weeks.
  • Inability to keep fluids down because of nausea — dehydration makes everything worse and may need intravenous support.
  • Any fall, near-fall, or injury related to your balance — especially if you live alone or have a raised fracture risk.
  • Balance that is worsening rather than improving after the first two weeks, or new numbness, weakness, or clumsiness.
  • Symptoms still interfering with work, driving, or daily life after three months — ask specifically about vestibular rehabilitation and about assessment for persistent postural-perceptual dizziness.
  • Any symptom that frightens you or that you cannot explain. That is a valid reason on its own.
Remember: TCM offers a complementary framework for the recovery period — how to pace, what to eat, how to settle the nervous system, and which points are traditionally used for nausea and dizziness. It does not replace prompt medical assessment, vestibular rehabilitation, or prescribed medication. If you work with a TCM practitioner, tell your doctor, and the other way round.

What Modern Research Suggests

  • Bedside eye tests in acute vertigo. Studies of the HINTS examination — head impulse testing, direction-changing nystagmus, and test of skew — suggest it can distinguish a peripheral cause from a central one with high sensitivity in the first days of an acute vestibular syndrome, sometimes outperforming early diffusion-weighted MRI. Kattah and colleagues published the widely cited series in Stroke in 2009. This is why same-day assessment matters.
  • Viral and post-viral associations. Vestibular neuritis frequently follows an upper respiratory infection, and some studies suggest reactivation of latent herpes simplex virus type 1 within the vestibular ganglion as one possible mechanism, based on post-mortem and virological work. The evidence is suggestive rather than settled — "probably viral, in many cases" remains the honest summary.
  • Corticosteroids. A randomized trial by Strupp and colleagues, published in Neurology in 2004, reported modest improvement in caloric recovery with corticosteroid therapy in vestibular neuritis. Subsequent commentary has questioned how clinically meaningful that benefit is, so practice varies. Any decision belongs with a clinician who has examined you.
  • Vestibular rehabilitation. Systematic reviews, including a Cochrane review by McDonnell and Hillier, generally support vestibular rehabilitation for unilateral peripheral vestibular dysfunction, with the strongest and most consistent gains in the early weeks after onset. This is the main evidence-based recovery tool, and the reason graded movement matters.
  • What compensation actually is. Basic and clinical research on vestibular compensation describes the brain actively re-calibrating after one-sided loss — with a "static" component that resolves relatively quickly and a "dynamic" component that takes longer and depends on movement experience. The neuroscience here aligns closely with the TCM emphasis on regular, gentle activity over prolonged rest.
  • Acupuncture and acupressure — early-stage evidence. Small randomized trials and case series have looked at acupuncture and acupressure for vertigo and for residual dizziness after vestibular disorders, and at PC-6 stimulation for nausea, with some positive signals. These studies are small, often unblinded, and far from definitive. They are the beginning of a research story, not the end of one — and they are not a reason to delay medical assessment.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:
  • Same-day medical assessment for a first episode of severe spinning. Acute vertigo can be a stroke presentation; HINTS eye testing is designed to sort that out. Do not self-diagnose.
  • Do not push through the storm. Rest, dim light, a fixed visual point, small sips, and fall-proofing your home. Acupressure is a comfort measure at most in this phase — never while standing.
  • In the fog phase, lighten up and start moving. Reduce fried, greasy, sweet, iced, and alcoholic food; favour broth, congee, ginger, and small warm meals; then begin short graded movement several times daily. Same principle as vestibular rehabilitation.
  • In the rebuilding phase, spend deliberately. Regular protein-rich meals, a fixed sleep window, and three short rehab sessions beat one exhausting one. Fatigue here is an energy-budget signal, not a character flaw.
  • Know the decompensation pattern and the margin concept. Alcohol, sleep debt, stress, and illness can briefly push a compensated system back over the edge. Watch the variables, not just the symptom.
  • Train the calm, not only the balance. Longer-exhale breathing, daylight early, and graded exposure to the environments you have started avoiding. Chronic vigilance is what keeps the long tail long.

Vestibular neuritis is a strange injury: a small nerve event that briefly takes over your entire world, and then a long, quiet recalibration that nobody else can see. Both halves are real. The first half needs a clinician; the second half needs patience, movement, sleep, food, and steady nerves. TCM's contribution sits in that second half — practical, unglamorous, and concerned with exactly the things that decide how fast the ground feels solid again.
