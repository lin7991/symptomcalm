Ménière's Support: A Practical TCM Self-Care Guide

Symptom Guide · 10 min read

You know the pattern. The ear fullness creeps in — that vague pressure that feels like your head is slowly filling with water. Then the tinnitus shifts pitch, or gets louder. And somewhere in the back of your awareness, a quiet question forms: Is an attack coming?

If you live with Ménière's disease, this waiting game is exhausting. Between episodes you're never quite sure what triggers the next one. The unpredictability can be as draining as the vertigo itself.

This guide takes a different approach. Instead of explaining what Ménière's is from a TCM perspective (for that, see our Ménière's Disease Through a TCM Lens), we're going straight to what you can do — a practical set of daily protocols, vertigo-first aid steps, and pattern-specific routines you can start using today.

How to use this guide: Start with the tracking system (Section 1) to identify your dominant pattern. Then go to the section that matches your pattern for specific dietary and acupressure protocols. Bookmark the Vertigo First Aid section for acute episodes. Build up gradually — don't try everything at once.

1. Build Your Ménière's Tracking System

Before you can use any protocol effectively, you need to know your pattern. Ménière's disease doesn't look the same from person to person, and the right daily care depends on whether your vertigo is driven by Liver Yang, Kidney Yin deficiency, or Spleen-related Dampness — or, most commonly, a combination.

Use a simple notebook or a notes app to track the following for two weeks. Keep it by your bedside so you can record episodes as they happen:

What to Track Why It Matters Pattern Clue
Time of day TCM organ clock links specific times to different systems 11pm–3am → Liver; 7am–9am → Spleen; 5pm–7pm → Kidney
Hours since last meal Spleen-related episodes often follow poor digestion or missed meals Episodes 2-4 hours after eating suggest Spleen-Dampness pattern
Stress level (1-10) Liver Yang/Wind episodes follow emotional triggers closely Vertigo within 1-3 hours of high stress → Liver pattern
Type of tinnitus High-pitched vs. muffled sounds point to different systems High-pitched ringing → Kidney/Yin pattern; muffled buzzing → Spleen/Dampness pattern; pulsatile → Liver pattern
Ear fullness sensation Pressure quantity and quality vary by pattern Sharp pressure with headache → Liver; vague fullness with fog → Spleen; dry pressure with fatigue → Kidney

After two weeks, review your journal. You'll likely see one or two patterns recurring — a specific time window, a consistent trigger, a type of tinnitus that dominates. This is your personal signal that tells you which system needs the most support.

2. Pattern-Specific Dietary Protocols

The existing Ménière's article outlines the general dietary framework for each pattern. This section gives you specific daily menus and meal templates you can follow directly.

If Your Pattern Is Liver Yang Rising

Goal: Cool, calm, downward-moving energy. Avoid anything that heats or stimulates.

Sample Daily Menu — Liver Pattern

Breakfast: Oatmeal or millet porridge with a handful of blueberries and sliced cucumber on the side. A cup of chrysanthemum tea.

Lunch: Steamed fish (cod or tilapia) with celery, bamboo shoots, and a small portion of brown rice. Season with a little mint and lemon.

Dinner: Mung bean and vegetable soup with seaweed, bok choy, and tofu. Lightly seasoned with ginger and coriander.

Avoid strictly: Caffeine, alcohol, spicy foods (chili, hot sauce, curry), fried foods, red meat in large quantities, and processed foods with MSG or excess salt.

Best snacks: Celery sticks, watermelon, cucumber slices, a small handful of almonds.

If Your Pattern Is Kidney Yin Deficiency

Goal: Nourish, moisturize, and build deep reserves. Avoid anything that dehydrates or drains.

Sample Daily Menu — Kidney Pattern

Breakfast: Congee (rice porridge) cooked with goji berries, a few black sesame seeds, and a splash of oat or almond milk. Serve warm.

Lunch: Slow-cooked bone broth with chunks of carrot, daikon radish, shiitake mushrooms, and quinoa. A side of steamed kale with a drizzle of sesame oil.

Dinner: Baked salmon with roasted asparagus and a small portion of black rice. A side of braised black fungus wood ear mushroom if available.

Avoid strictly: Caffeine (especially coffee), excessive alcohol, very spicy foods, and excessive sweating from intense exercise.

Best snacks: Goji berry tea, a small handful of walnuts, dried black dates, a poached egg on toast.

If Your Pattern Is Spleen Deficiency with Dampness

Goal: Dry out Dampness, warm the digestive fire, and keep meals light and regular.

Sample Daily Menu — Spleen Pattern

Breakfast: Warm millet porridge with a pinch of cinnamon and a teaspoon of honey. Or a bowl of congee with grated ginger and a splash of tamari.

Lunch: Steamed chicken breast with stir-fried bok choy, carrot, and a small portion of basmati rice. Lightly seasoned with ginger and garlic. No heavy sauces.

Dinner: A warming soup made with chicken, lotus root, astragalus root (if available), and barley. Served with a side of lightly steamed green beans.

Avoid strictly: Dairy (milk, cheese, yogurt, ice cream), excessive sugar and sweets, raw salads and cold foods, fried or greasy foods, excessive bread and wheat products, and cold drinks (especially iced water).

Best snacks: A small handful of roasted chestnuts, rice cakes with a light spread of miso, a piece of fresh ginger in hot water steeped as tea.

Important: If your pattern is mixed (the most common scenario), start by following the dietary protocol of your most symptomatic pattern. For example, if your vertigo is severe and triggered by stress, start with the Liver cooling protocol. If your digestion is consistently poor, start with the Spleen protocol. Add in the second protocol's best foods once you've established consistency with the first.

3. Acupressure: Daily Maintenance vs. Acute Care

Acupressure is one of the most practical self-care tools for Ménière's because it's available to you any time, anywhere — no equipment, no waiting. The key is to use different points for different situations.

Daily Maintenance Routine (3 minutes, twice a day)

Do this every morning and evening, even on good days. It builds resilience over time:

  1. KI3 (Taixi) — 1 minute each ankle: In the depression between the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon. Use gentle, sustained pressure. This nourishes Kidney Yin, the foundation of ear health.
  2. ST36 (Zusanli) — 1 minute each leg: Four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width outside the shinbone. This strengthens the Spleen and builds Qi — the body's resistance against vertigo episodes.
  3. LV3 (Taichong) — 30 seconds each foot: On the top of the foot, in the depression between the big toe and second toe. A gentle calming point for the Liver system.

Acute Episode Protocol (when vertigo starts)

At the first sign of an attack — ear fullness intensifying, tinnitus shifting, or the sensation of movement beginning:

  1. Sit or lie down immediately with your head supported. Do not try to "push through" a vertigo episode.
  2. Press GB20 (Fengchi) firmly: In the hollows at the base of your skull, between the two large neck muscles. Press upward (toward the skull) for 60-90 seconds with steady, moderate pressure. This point expels Wind — the TCM factor most associated with sudden, spinning vertigo.
  3. Press PC6 (Neiguan): Two finger-widths above the wrist crease, between the two tendons on the inner forearm. Hold for 60 seconds. This point is traditionally used for nausea and dizziness — it may help reduce the queasy component of a Ménière's attack.
  4. Press CV12 (Zhongwan): Midway between the navel and the bottom of the breastbone. Gentle circular pressure for 30-60 seconds. This settles the stomach and helps the body's energy descend rather than surging upward (which worsens vertigo in TCM theory).
Safety note: Do not press any point that makes your vertigo feel worse. Everyone's response is slightly different. If a point intensifies symptoms, skip it. Acupressure is a support tool — not something to force through discomfort.

4. Vertigo First Aid: A Step-by-Step Protocol

When a vertigo episode hits, your nervous system is in full emergency mode. The spinning sensation triggers a cascade of physiological responses — rapid heart rate, nausea, sweating, and panic. Having a written protocol you've practiced on good days can make the difference between a manageable episode and a complete derailment of your day.

  1. Stop moving. Sit or lie down immediately. Do not try to walk to a "better" spot unless you're unsafe where you are. Close your eyes to reduce visual stimulation that worsens the spinning.
  2. Fix your gaze on a single, stable point. If your eyes are open, pick one object in the room and keep looking at it. This gives your visual system a stationary reference that can reduce the intensity of the spinning sensation.
  3. Apply acupressure (GB20 + PC6) as described in the acute protocol above. Don't rush — steady, moderate pressure for 60-90 seconds per point.
  4. Breathe slowly and deeply. Inhale for 4 counts, hold for 4 counts, exhale for 6 counts. The extended exhale activates the vagus nerve and signals your nervous system to downshift from emergency mode. Repeat 5-10 cycles.
  5. Stay in position until the room stops spinning. Ménière's vertigo episodes typically last 20 minutes to several hours, but the most intense spinning usually subsides within 30 minutes. Do not rush to stand up. When you feel the spinning has stopped, sit up slowly — first to a seated position for 2 minutes, then stand with support.
  6. Hydrate with warm water or ginger tea once the acute phase has passed. A cup of ginger tea can help settle any lingering nausea and warm the digestive system.
  7. Rest for the remainder of the day if possible. Your body has expended significant energy during the episode, and your Kidney reserves (which govern the ears in TCM) need time to recover.
When to seek emergency care: If your vertigo is accompanied by any of the following, seek medical attention immediately: slurred speech, double vision, facial numbness or drooping, severe headache, difficulty walking, loss of consciousness, or fever. These may indicate a more serious condition such as a stroke or severe infection.

5. Nervous System Support for Ménière's Resilience

In both TCM and modern neuroscience, the nervous system plays a central role in Ménière's disease. The inner ear's fluid balance is directly influenced by stress hormones — cortisol and adrenaline can trigger or worsen episodes. TCM addresses this through the Liver (stress response) and Kidney (adrenal reserves) systems.

Here are three specific practices that may help reduce episode frequency by supporting your nervous system:

Practice 1: The 3-Minute Evening Wind-Down

A consistent evening routine signals your nervous system that it's safe to downshift. Do this in bed, just before sleep:

  • Lie on your back with a thin pillow or rolled towel under your knees.
  • Place one hand on your lower abdomen and one on your chest.
  • Breathe slowly — focus on making the lower hand rise more than the upper hand (diaphragmatic breathing).
  • After 2 minutes, gently press the GB20 points (base of skull) for 1 minute with your thumbs.

This combines vagus nerve activation (slow breathing) with Liver-calming acupressure. Over weeks, it can reduce the baseline reactivity of your nervous system.

Practice 2: Salt and Hydration Awareness

Sodium is a well-documented trigger for Ménière's episodes. Aim for under 2,000 mg per day. But equally important is fluid consistency — rapid shifts in fluid levels can trigger episodes. Drink small amounts of warm water throughout the day rather than large gulps. Avoid alternating between very salty and very hydrating foods, which creates the fluid fluctuations that the inner ear is sensitive to.

Practice 3: Head Position and Qi Flow

In TCM, the neck and upper shoulders are a common site of Qi stagnation, and this congestion can affect the ears. Gentle neck stretches (no sudden movements) and shoulder rolls throughout the day keep Qi flowing freely to the head. Avoid sleeping on your stomach, which twists the neck and can compress the cervical spine, potentially restricting Qi flow to the ears. A supportive pillow that keeps your head aligned with your spine is a small investment with potentially significant benefits.

6. When to See a Doctor

Ménière's disease requires proper medical diagnosis and management. While TCM self-care protocols can offer valuable support, some situations require professional medical attention:

  • Sudden, severe vertigo lasting more than a few hours or accompanied by fever, slurred speech, or double vision — these may indicate a stroke or other neurological emergency
  • Sudden hearing loss in one or both ears — this is a medical urgency; seek care within 24 hours
  • New or worsening symptoms despite consistent self-care — your treatment plan may need adjustment
  • Inability to keep down fluids due to nausea from vertigo — dehydration can worsen the condition and require IV support
  • Falls or injuries resulting from vertigo episodes — especially important if you live alone or are at higher risk of fracture
  • If you have not yet received a formal diagnosis — other conditions can mimic Ménière's disease and require different treatment
Remember: TCM self-care is a complementary approach. Work with both your healthcare provider and a qualified TCM practitioner for a comprehensive management plan. Never stop or change prescribed medications without consulting your doctor.

7. Practical Takeaways

  • Track before you treat — two weeks of daily journaling (time, triggers, tinnitus quality, stress level) will reveal your personal pattern and tell you which protocol to prioritize
  • Start with one protocol — choose the dietary or acupressure protocol that matches your dominant pattern. Practice it consistently for two weeks before adding more. Overloading yourself with changes creates stress, which works against the goal
  • Learn the vertigo first aid protocol — practice the GB20 + PC6 points on a good day so you can find them easily during an episode. Keep these instructions saved on your phone
  • Protect your sleep — consistent, quality sleep nourishes Kidney Yin and calms Liver Yang. If Ménière's-related anxiety keeps you awake, see our TCM Insomnia Guide for sleep-support protocols
  • Consider working with a TCM practitioner — a licensed acupuncturist can provide a proper pulse and tongue diagnosis to identify your specific pattern combination, and may recommend targeted acupuncture treatments and customized herbal formulas that go beyond self-care
  • Be patient with progress — Ménière's is a chronic condition. The goal of TCM self-care is to reduce episode frequency and severity over time, not to eliminate episodes overnight. Celebrate small wins: a week without vertigo, a trigger you successfully avoided, an episode that resolved faster than before
