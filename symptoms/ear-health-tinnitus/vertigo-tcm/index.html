Vertigo and Dizziness: TCM Insights into Balance

Symptom Guide · 9 min read

The world tilts without warning. You're sitting at your desk, and suddenly the room lurches — as if the floor just dropped a few inches. Or maybe it's a gentler sensation: a persistent lightheadedness, a feeling of being on a boat that never docks. Your eyes struggle to focus. Your balance feels unreliable. The simplest movements — turning your head, standing up too quickly — send the world spinning.

If you've experienced vertigo or dizziness, you know how disorienting it can be. And if your doctor has run tests that come back "normal," you also know how frustrating it is when the problem feels very real but defies easy explanation.

Western medicine typically categorizes dizziness into four subtypes: vertigo (a spinning sensation, often from the inner ear), presyncope (lightheadedness, feeling like you might faint), disequilibrium (a sense of unsteadiness or imbalance), and non-specific dizziness (a vague, hard-to-describe sensation). Common causes include benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), vestibular neuritis, Meniere's disease, migraines, low blood pressure, and anxiety.

Traditional Chinese Medicine offers a complementary framework. Rather than isolating dizziness to the inner ear or the brain, TCM sees it as a signal from your body's deeper systems — the Liver, Kidney, and Spleen — each of which contributes to your sense of balance in a different way. Understanding which system is involved may help you find more targeted, lasting relief.

A note on safety: Sudden, severe vertigo — especially when accompanied by slurred speech, facial drooping, double vision, chest pain, or loss of consciousness — requires immediate medical attention. Dizziness can sometimes signal a stroke, heart condition, or other serious medical event. TCM approaches are complementary and should not replace emergency care or a proper diagnosis.

How TCM Sees Balance: The Three-System Framework

Think of your body's balance system like a three-legged stool. Each leg represents a different organ system, and each contributes to stability in a distinct way. When all three are strong, you feel grounded and steady. When one leg weakens, the stool wobbles — and the wobble is dizziness.

System Role in Balance When Imbalanced
Liver Governs smooth Qi flow throughout the head. Keeps energy from surging upward. Sudden spinning vertigo, often triggered by stress or anger. Headaches and irritability accompany the dizziness.
Kidney Anchors Qi downward. Provides the structural foundation for the inner ear structures. Chronic, age-related unsteadiness. Dizziness accompanied by tinnitus, hearing loss, and low back weakness.
Spleen Manages fluid metabolism. Produces "clear Qi" that rises to the head to keep it bright and focused. Heavy, foggy dizziness with nausea. Brain fog, poor digestion, and a sensation of the head being "stuffed."

The analogy: your body's balance is like a ship on the water. The Liver is the wind in the sails (movement and direction), the Kidney is the anchor chain (stability and grounding), and the Spleen is the hull's internal ballast (fluid management and buoyancy). When any one of these is off, the ship rocks.

4 TCM Patterns of Vertigo and Dizziness

Pattern 1: Liver Yang Rising with Liver Wind (肝阳化风)

You might have this if: Your vertigo comes on suddenly, often after a period of stress, anger, or poor sleep. The spinning is intense — like a carnival ride that won't stop. You may also experience pulsating headaches at the temples, irritability, a red face, ringing in the ears, and tension in the neck and shoulders. The episodes may feel connected to emotional triggers: a frustrating meeting, an argument, or a buildup of daily pressure.

TCM explanation: When Liver Qi becomes stagnant under chronic stress, it builds pressure like steam in a boiler. Over time, this pressure transforms into "Liver Yang Rising" — energy that surges upward instead of flowing smoothly. When Yang becomes extreme, it generates "Liver Wind," which in TCM theory is associated with sudden, moving symptoms — vertigo, tremors, spasms, and migraines. The sudden-onset spinning maps directly to this concept of Wind disturbing the head.

What tends to trigger episodes: Emotional stress, sleep deprivation, high-sodium meals, alcohol, caffeine overload, and premenstrual hormonal shifts.

Pattern 2: Kidney Jing Deficiency (肾精亏虚)

You might have this if: Your dizziness is more of a chronic, low-grade unsteadiness than violent spinning. You feel "off-balance" rather than vertiginous. You may notice that your dizziness worsens when you're tired, and improves with rest. You likely also experience some degree of hearing loss or chronic tinnitus, lower back soreness, forgetfulness, and a general sense of your body having less resilience than it used to. This is common in people over 50, or in anyone who has been under chronic stress for years.

TCM explanation: Kidney Jing — often described as your body's "essence" or deepest vitality reserve — provides the structural foundation for hearing, balance, and the inner ear. Think of it as the anchor chain of a ship: when the chain is strong and deep, the vessel holds steady in rough water. When the chain is worn and corroded, even a moderate swell makes the entire ship lurch. Kidney Jing naturally declines with age, but it can also be prematurely depleted by chronic overwork, sleep deprivation, illness, or long-term stress.

What tends to trigger episodes: Physical exhaustion, illness, overwork, insufficient sleep, aging, and recovery from long-term stress.

Pattern 3: Spleen Deficiency with Phlegm-Dampness (脾虚痰湿)

You might have this if: Your dizziness feels less like spinning and more like a heavy, foggy sensation — as if your head is wrapped in cotton wool. You may feel nauseous during episodes, have poor digestion, loose stools, a thick or greasy tongue coating in the morning, and a general sensation of mental sluggishness. Your ears might feel full or congested. Rich, fatty, or sweet foods seem to make things worse.

TCM explanation: The Spleen is your body's digestive engine — it transforms food into Qi and Blood. When the Spleen is weak (often from irregular eating, excessive dairy, or overthinking), it produces "Dampness" instead of clean energy. This Dampness can accumulate and congeal into "Phlegm" — a thick, sticky substance that TCM says can obstruct the "orifices" of the head, including the ears and the sensory organs. The foggy, heavy quality of this type of dizziness reflects the nature of Dampness: like walking around with a waterlogged sponge inside your skull.

What tends to trigger episodes: Heavy meals, greasy or sweet foods, dairy, humid weather, irregular eating habits, and prolonged mental worry or overthinking.

Pattern 4: Blood Deficiency (血虚)

You might have this if: Your dizziness is most noticeable when you stand up quickly — a brief spinning or graying of vision that passes in a few seconds. You may also experience fatigue, pale complexion, dry skin or hair, brittle nails, poor memory, and a tendency toward palpitations or anxiety. This is particularly common after heavy menstrual bleeding, childbirth, gastrointestinal bleeding, or a period of very poor nutrition.

TCM explanation: Blood in TCM is both a physical substance and a nourishing force. It carries Qi and provides the material foundation for clear sensory function. When Blood is deficient, the brain and inner ear receive less nourishment, and the balance system becomes underpowered — like a light fixture with reduced wattage. The brain needs a steady supply of Blood to process position and movement signals; when supply dips, the system gets confused and produces dizziness, especially during position changes.

What tends to trigger episodes: Menstruation, postpartum recovery, poor diet, chronic illness, overwork, and insufficient sleep.

Note: Many cases of chronic dizziness involve a combination of patterns. The most common is Liver Yang Rising with underlying Kidney Yin Deficiency — where stress (Liver) burns through your reserves (Kidney), creating a cycle that worsens both. A TCM practitioner can help identify the specific pattern or combination at play in your case.

Acupressure Points for Dizziness and Vertigo

Acupressure is a self-administered technique that stimulates specific points along the body's energy channels. For dizziness, several points are traditionally used depending on the underlying pattern. Gentle, sustained pressure for 60-90 seconds per point, while breathing deeply, is the standard approach.

Point 1: LV-3 (Taichong, "Great Surge")

Location: On the top of the foot, in the depression between the first and second metatarsal bones — about 1-2 finger-widths from where the toes meet the foot.

Best for: Liver Yang Rising pattern — vertigo triggered by stress, with headaches and irritability.

How to use: Press firmly with your thumb into the hollow between the two bones. You should feel a distinct sensation — not sharp pain, but a deep ache or "electric" feeling. Hold for 60 seconds on each foot. This point is traditionally used to "subdue Liver Yang" and calm rising Qi.

Point 2: ST-36 (Zusanli, "Leg Three Li")

Location: Four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width toward the outside of the shin bone, in a depression in the muscle.

Best for: Spleen Deficiency and Blood Deficiency patterns — dizziness accompanied by fatigue, poor digestion, or pallor.

How to use: Apply firm, steady pressure with your thumb or knuckle. You may feel a dull, spreading sensation down the leg. Hold for 60 seconds on each leg. In TCM, ST-36 is a primary point for building Qi and Blood.

Point 3: KI-3 (Taixi, "Great Stream")

Location: In the depression between the inner ankle bone (medial malleolus) and the Achilles tendon, level with the tip of the ankle bone.

Best for: Kidney Jing Deficiency pattern — chronic unsteadiness with tinnitus, hearing loss, and low back soreness.

How to use: Use your thumb to press firmly into the hollow behind the ankle bone. Hold for 60 seconds on each ankle. This point is traditionally used to nourish Kidney Yin and anchor the body's Qi downward.

Point 4: GB-20 (Fengchi, "Wind Pool")

Location: In the hollows at the base of the skull, between the two large neck muscles, about two finger-widths apart.

Best for: All patterns — especially effective for acute vertigo and tension-type dizziness. Also useful for Meniere's-related episodes.

How to use: Use your thumbs to press upward into the hollows at the base of your skull. Apply steady, upward pressure for 60-90 seconds. You may feel a release of tension in your neck and a sensation of clarity in your head. This point is traditionally used to "expel Wind" and clear the sensory orifices of the head.

Dietary Approaches by Pattern

Food in TCM is not just fuel — it's medicine with specific energetic properties. Choosing the right foods for your pattern can support your body's natural balance and reduce the frequency or severity of dizzy episodes.

For Liver Yang Rising (sudden spinning, stress-related)

  • Emphasize: Cooling, calming foods — celery, cucumber, leafy greens, mung beans, watermelon, chrysanthemum tea, peppermint tea.
  • Reduce: Spicy foods, alcohol, caffeine, fried foods, and red meat — these fan the internal "fire" that drives Yang upward.
  • TCM tip: A simple cup of chrysanthemum tea (ju hua) is traditionally used to cool Liver Heat and calm rising Yang. Try it as an afternoon beverage instead of coffee.

For Kidney Jing Deficiency (chronic unsteadiness, age-related)

  • Emphasize: Nourishing, grounding foods — bone broth, walnuts, black sesame seeds, goji berries, black beans, seaweed, eggs, and slow-cooked soups.
  • Reduce: Overly stimulating foods (excessive coffee, energy drinks) that deplete reserves — and avoid skipping meals, which draws on Kidney Jing for emergency energy.
  • TCM tip: A small handful of walnuts and goji berries makes a Kidney-supportive daily snack. In TCM theory, foods that are black or dark in color are traditionally associated with Kidney nourishment.

For Spleen Deficiency / Phlegm-Dampness (foggy dizziness, nausea)

  • Emphasize: Light, warming, drying foods — ginger, garlic, turmeric, barley, oats, adzuki beans, pumpkin, and well-cooked vegetables. Small, frequent meals are easier for a weak Spleen to process.
  • Avoid: Dairy (especially cold dairy), excessive raw foods, sugar, refined carbohydrates, greasy foods, and large heavy meals — these produce Dampness overwhelming the Spleen.
  • TCM tip: A slice of fresh ginger steeped in hot water makes a simple digestive tonic. Adding a pinch of dried tangerine peel (chen pi) can help transform Phlegm and clear head congestion.

For Blood Deficiency (positional dizziness, pallor)

  • Emphasize: Blood-building foods — dark leafy greens (spinach, kale), red dates (jujube), beets, lean red meat, liver (in moderation), blackstrap molasses, and bone broth.
  • Avoid: Excessive raw foods (harder to digest when Blood is low), fasting, and extreme diets that restrict caloric intake.
  • TCM tip: A simple tea of red dates (jujube) and goji berries is traditionally used to nourish Blood. Simmer 3-5 red dates (sliced) with a tablespoon of goji berries in 2 cups of water for 15 minutes.

When to See a Doctor

Seek immediate medical attention if your dizziness or vertigo is accompanied by:
  • Slurred speech, facial drooping, or weakness on one side of the body
  • Double vision or vision loss
  • Chest pain or palpitations
  • Loss of consciousness or fainting
  • Sudden, severe headache (the worst headache of your life)
  • Difficulty walking or coordinating movements

Also consult a healthcare provider if:

  • Your dizziness is new, severe, or progressively worsening
  • It follows a head injury
  • You have a history of stroke, heart disease, or neurological conditions
  • It significantly interferes with your daily activities or quality of life
  • It occurs alongside new or changing hearing loss

What Modern Research Suggests

While research on TCM for vertigo and dizziness is still developing, several studies offer interesting insights:

  • Acupuncture for vertigo: A 2021 systematic review in Frontiers in Neurology examined acupuncture for various types of vertigo (including BPPV, Meniere's, and vestibular neuritis). The review found that acupuncture, particularly at GB-20 (Fengchi) and points along the Gallbladder and Bladder channels, showed promise in reducing vertigo severity and frequency, though the authors noted that larger, well-designed trials are needed.
  • Acupressure for motion sickness and dizziness: PC-6 (Neiguan, located on the inner forearm) has been studied extensively for nausea and motion sickness, with the Cochrane Review (2012) finding it effective for postoperative and chemotherapy-induced nausea. While not specific to vertigo, the anti-nausea effect may benefit those whose dizziness is accompanied by nausea.
  • TCM herbal formulas for cervical vertigo: A 2020 meta-analysis in Medicine found that Chinese herbal medicine combined with conventional treatment showed greater improvement in cervical vertigo (dizziness originating from neck issues) compared to conventional treatment alone, though the quality of included studies varied.
  • Chrysanthemum and Liver Yang: Laboratory studies have found that chrysanthemum (ju hua) extracts exhibit anti-inflammatory and vasodilatory effects, which may provide a scientific basis for its traditional use in calming Liver Yang and relieving tension-related dizziness.
Key takeaway: The research is preliminary but points in an interesting direction. The TCM patterns described in this article are based on centuries of clinical observation, and emerging science is beginning to explore the mechanisms behind them. TCM approaches are best used as a complement to — not a replacement for — proper medical evaluation and treatment.

Practical Takeaways

Your Vertigo Support Plan

1. Identify your pattern. Use the self-assessment table above to see which pattern most closely matches your experience. Remember that combinations are common.

2. Start with GB-20 acupressure. For acute vertigo, pressing GB-20 (Wind Pool) at the base of the skull is the most versatile first step. Use steady, upward pressure for 90 seconds.

3. Match your diet to your pattern. Stop eating foods that trigger your pattern (spicy food for Liver Yang, dairy for Phlegm, cold food for Spleen) and add pattern-supportive foods.

4. Prioritize sleep and rest. Every pattern of dizziness is worsened by fatigue. In TCM, sleep is the most powerful restorative — especially for Kidney Jing and Blood Deficiency patterns.

5. Track your triggers. Keep a simple log: when did the dizziness happen, what had you eaten, how was your stress level, what time of day was it? Over 2-3 weeks, patterns will emerge that help you and your practitioner identify the root.

Vertigo and dizziness can feel destabilizing in every sense of the word. But understanding the TCM framework gives you a new lens — not just to manage symptoms, but to understand what your body is signaling. Whether the root involves a Liver that needs calming, a Kidney that needs nourishing, a Spleen that needs supporting, or Blood that needs rebuilding, the path to steadier ground starts with listening to which system is asking for attention.
