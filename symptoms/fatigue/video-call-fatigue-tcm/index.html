Video Call Fatigue: Why Zoom Exhausts You — and How TCM Explains It

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

You close your laptop after a 90-minute video call and you're not just tired — you're flattened. Your eyes feel sandpapered. Your shoulders have crept up around your ears. The idea of one more meeting makes you want to disappear. And the strange part? You barely moved. You didn't lift anything, run anywhere, or fight any fires. You just sat in a chair and talked to faces on a screen.

So why does it feel like you ran a marathon?

If this sounds familiar, you're describing video call fatigue — sometimes called Zoom fatigue — and it is one of the most common energy complaints of remote work. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has a remarkably precise map of why screen-mediated conversation costs more than in-person conversation, which internal systems pay the bill, and how to structure your day so calls stop emptying your tank.

Note on scope: this guide is about the whole-body exhaustion that builds across call-heavy days — the drained, foggy, wired feeling. If your main struggle is dry, aching, blurry eyes at the screen, our guide to digital eye strain covers the eye-specific side in depth. And if you notice the same depletion after in-person gatherings, that's its own pattern — see social fatigue. Video call fatigue sits between the two: it's social, but filtered through a screen and a chair.

Why a Video Call Costs More Than the Same Conversation in Person

Before the TCM view, it helps to name what is actually different about a video call. In a real conversation, your body does a thousand small things that cost almost nothing: your gaze drifts to the window, back to the speaker, down to your notes. You blink freely. You lean back. Heads nod, people murmur "mm-hmm," side conversations happen — a constant, low-cost stream of feedback that quietly confirms we're still together in this.

Video removes most of that. The camera pins you in what feels like a sustained stare at conversational distance — something that almost never happens in person, where eye contact comes and goes in seconds. The slight delays and frozen frames break the natural rhythm of turn-taking. And because the usual feedback cues are missing, your mind has to work harder to guess whether you're being understood. Researchers who study this phenomenon call it "nonverbal overload," and they have named four contributing factors: excessive close-up gaze, higher cognitive load, the all-day mirror of seeing yourself, and reduced physical mobility.

Here's the elegant part: TCM described those same four costs centuries ago — as drains on three specific systems. Each video call quietly taxes your Liver Blood, your Heart-Shen, and your Spleen Qi. Not because the call is bad, but because of what the format demands: unbroken gazing, unrelenting attention, and unbroken stillness.

New to these terms? In TCM, Qi is the body's operating system — the energy that powers every function — and Blood is its material fuel, not just red cells. The Liver governs the smooth flow of Qi and nourishes the eyes, the Heart houses the Shen (awareness itself), and the Spleen transforms food into Qi and Blood. For full primers, see our TCM Basics guides on the Liver, the Heart-Shen, and the Spleen.

The TCM View: Three Taxes Hidden in Every Video Call

👁️ The Gaze Tax — Liver Blood

In TCM, the eyes are the "window" of the Liver, and they are among the most Blood-hungry tissues in the body — Liver Blood is what keeps them moist, focused, and rested. The oldest surviving TCM classic, the Huangdi Neijing, warns that prolonged gazing depletes Blood. Think of your eyes as high-beam headlights: they illuminate everything, but they draw serious power. A video call forces you to run the high beams for the entire meeting — staring at faces at close conversational range, often without blinking properly. A one-hour call can contain more sustained mutual gaze than an entire dinner with a friend, and every minute of it draws on Liver Blood. When the account runs low, the first notices arrive as eye strain, temple or behind-the-eye ache, and a short fuse by late afternoon.

🎭 The Attention Tax — Heart-Shen

The Heart houses the Shen — the aspect of mind that does the paying of attention. In person, conversation partly runs on autopilot: body language, shared glances, and ambient noise confirm understanding without any conscious effort. On a call, those cues are degraded or gone, so the Shen must continuously and consciously infer: Is that a pause or a lag? Did she follow that? Am I talking too long? It's the difference between flying by visual flight rules and flying on instruments alone — the same route, but the second demands constant, deliberate scanning. Add the mirror: seeing your own face all day turns the Shen back on itself, like a singer performing to a mirror, reviewing every micro-expression in real time. Awareness that should be flowing outward into connection is instead split between the conversation and a running self-review.

🪑 The Stillness Tax — Spleen Qi

The Spleen transforms food into Qi and Blood — think of it as the body's power plant — and it prefers to work while the body moves. The classics also warn that prolonged sitting harms the flesh and that excessive thinking strains the Spleen. A call-heavy day stacks both: hours of stillness plus continuous mental work, often with a slumped spine and shallow breathing. It's like leaving a power plant idling in a closed garage — fuel burns, but the energy has nowhere to circulate, so it pools. That's the heavy-limbed, foggy-headed, sighing, slightly nauseous "stale" feeling that lands mid-afternoon on meeting-heavy days. The Qi isn't gone; it's stuck.

Three Patterns of Video Call Fatigue — Which Sounds Like You?

Most people carry all three taxes to some degree, but usually one is doing the heaviest damage. Read each pattern and notice which one makes you say "that's me on a bad week."

👁️ Pattern 1: The Gaze-Drained (Liver Blood running thin)

Signs: eyes that feel gritty, heavy, or slow to refocus by afternoon; an ache behind or around the eyes, or a band of tension at the temples; a stiff neck from leaning into the screen; irritability that spikes out of proportion to the situation; vision that feels "tired" even if it isn't blurry. Worst on camera-on, back-to-back days.

First move: schedule genuine gaze breaks — look into the middle distance (out a window, down a hallway) for 20 seconds every 20 minutes, and palm your eyes for a full minute after your last call. Your Liver Blood replenishes during rest and deep sleep, so an earlier night pays off more than any supplement.

🎭 Pattern 2: The Wired-and-Drained (Heart-Shen overwork)

Signs: the call ends but your brain doesn't — you replay what you said, analyze a tone of voice, rehearse what you'll say next; feeling self-conscious or "on stage" whenever the camera is on; a fluttering or tight sensation in the chest during high-stakes calls; restless, light sleep after meeting-heavy days. The Shen stayed lit long after the screen went dark.

First move: hide your self-view before meetings start, and go camera-off (or audio-only) for low-stakes calls whenever the format allows. After the last call, do a deliberate 90-second settle: slow exhale breathing, palms over your eyes, and name three things you can hear — it returns the Shen to the room you're actually in.

🪑 Pattern 3: The Foggy-and-Heavy (Spleen Qi stagnation)

Signs: the mid-afternoon wall hits hardest on days with long blocks of sitting; heavy arms and legs, as if gravity doubled; brain fog that makes simple decisions feel effortful; bloating or a churning stomach by evening; frequent sighing; a posture that slowly collapses into the monitor by 3 p.m.

First move: move for two minutes every hour — walk to the kitchen, roll your shoulders, stand on your toes — and hold at least one call per day as a walking or standing call. A warm, cooked lunch (soup, rice bowl, leftovers) rather than a cold desk salad gives the Spleen fuel it can actually use.

A TCM-Inspired Protocol for Call-Heavy Days

Rather than fighting the format, you can structure around it. TCM's core logic is simple: expenditure must be matched by restoration, and Qi needs gaps in which to move. Here's what that looks like on a real workday.

Before your first call

  • Set the camera at eye level (stack a book under your laptop). When the camera is below you, you hunch down toward it for hours — a direct tax on the neck and shoulders that Liver Qi stagnation loves to collect.
  • Hide your self-view before the meeting starts. The mirror effect is strongest when you can see yourself react; removing it early prevents the Shen from turning inward.
  • Water within reach, and a 60-second exhale. One minute of slow breathing — in for four counts, out for six — before the first call sets a different baseline than diving in cold.

During calls

  • Use the 20-20-20 habit. Every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet away for 20 seconds. It gives the gaze muscles (and Liver Blood) a genuine rest, and it's the one eye-care practice with broad research support.
  • Blink on purpose. Studies suggest people blink dramatically less during screen work, which is why eyes feel raw by evening. A conscious blink every few minutes keeps the surface of the eye moist.
  • Stand for at least one call a day. Even a standing stretch mid-meeting (camera off) lets Spleen Qi circulate instead of pooling. If you can take a low-stakes call as audio-only on your feet, so much the better.

Between calls

  • Protect a 3–5 minute buffer. Back-to-back meetings give Qi no gap in which to move — the energetic equivalent of rush hour with no green lights. Even a short buffer, used to walk and drink water, changes the arc of the whole afternoon.
  • One movement, one breath. In the buffer, do one physical reset (walk, stretch, roll your neck) and one breathing reset (three slow exhales). That combination clears both the Spleen's stagnation and the Shen's leftover activation.

After your last call

  • Build a shutdown ritual, not a shutdown thought. Ten minutes off screens before switching to evening mode: a walk, cooking, gentle stretching. In TCM terms, this lets the Qi that was "up" in the head and chest descend back into the body.
  • Palm your eyes. Rub your palms together until warm, cup them over your closed eyes, and breathe slowly for one to two minutes. It's a traditional rest for the eyes that also signals the Shen that the workday is over.
  • Skip the "one more scroll." Reopening the work chat or inbox after your ritual re-engages the Shen at the exact moment it's trying to settle — the fastest route from "done" back to "wired."

Acupressure Points for the Post-Call Reset

Acupressure is a traditional self-care tool: firm but gentle pressure in small circles on specific points, one to two minutes per side. Three points are especially useful after call-heavy days:

  • GB-20 (Wind Pool) — in the hollows at the base of your skull, just under the ridge of bone. This is the classic release point for the neck-and-temple tension that accumulates from leaning into a screen, and it's traditionally used to calm the head and eyes. Press both hollows simultaneously with your fingertips.
  • HT-7 (Shenmen) — on the inner wrist crease, in line with the little finger. The "Spirit Gate" is the classic point for settling a Shen that won't switch off. If you finish calls feeling wired, replaying conversations, or restless, spend two minutes here on each wrist.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — about four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width toward the outside of the shin. This is the Spleen-Stomach workhorse point, traditionally used to support energy and digestion. If your pattern is the heavy, foggy, post-lunch slump, this is your point.

Press gently — you're looking for a dull, pleasant ache, not pain. If you're pregnant or have a health condition, check with your healthcare provider before practicing acupressure regularly.

What Research Suggests

Video call fatigue is young enough that research is still catching up, but several threads align with the three-tax picture:

  • Four causes of Zoom fatigue. In 2021, communication researcher Jeremy Bailenson proposed that Zoom fatigue stems from nonverbal overload: excessive close-up eye gaze, increased cognitive load, the all-day mirror of self-view, and reduced physical mobility. Those four map almost one-to-one onto the gaze tax, the attention tax, and the stillness tax above.
  • The mirror matters. Research using the Zoom Exhaustion and Fatigue Scale found that the self-view mirror was a significant predictor of fatigue — and that women reported higher fatigue scores, consistent with findings that self-focused attention tends to be higher when one's own image is displayed.
  • Blink rate drops at screens. Studies suggest that people blink far less often during screen work than in normal conversation, which contributes to the dry, gritty eye sensation at the end of a screen-heavy day — the surface-level signature of the gaze tax.
  • Breaks from stillness help. Research on prolonged sitting consistently finds that short, frequent breaks improve perceived energy and reduce discomfort, and some studies suggest walking meetings can support both focus and well-being — practical confirmations of the Spleen's preference for movement.

These threads are suggestive, not proof of TCM's framework — but they do confirm that video call fatigue is real, measurable, and worth designing around rather than pushing through.

When to See a Doctor

When to See a Doctor: Feeling drained after a day of calls is common, but see a healthcare provider if the exhaustion stops tracking your schedule — if you're fatigued every day regardless of meetings, for two weeks or more, or if it comes with unexplained weight change, fever, or severe weakness. Seek help if you feel persistently low, lose interest in things you used to enjoy, or find yourself withdrawing from all contact — these can be signs of depression that deserves proper evaluation. Also see a doctor promptly for new or worsening headaches with vision changes, chest pain or palpitations with shortness of breath, or eye pain and persistent visual disturbances. TCM is a complementary perspective — it does not replace medical diagnosis or care.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  • Video call fatigue is a real energy cost, not weakness or laziness — and TCM explains it as three specific taxes: the gaze tax (Liver Blood), the attention tax (Heart-Shen), and the stillness tax (Spleen Qi).
  • Name your dominant pattern — Gaze-Drained, Wired-and-Drained, or Foggy-and-Heavy — and start with that pattern's first move rather than trying everything at once.
  • The cheapest fixes are structural: camera at eye level, hide self-view, camera-off for low-stakes calls, and protect a few minutes of buffer between meetings.
  • Move for two minutes every hour and take at least one walking or standing call a day — movement is what lets Spleen Qi circulate.
  • Use the 20-20-20 gaze break and blink on purpose during calls; palm your eyes after your last meeting.
  • Build a screen-free shutdown ritual after work — even ten minutes — so the Shen can settle and the day's Qi can descend.
