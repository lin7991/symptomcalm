POTS (Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia): A TCM View on Dizziness and Fatigue

Symptom Guide · 8 min read

You're fine lying down. Then you stand up — to grab water, answer the door, or just rise from the couch — and the world tilts. Your vision dims for a beat. Your heart starts slamming against your ribs like it's trying to make up for something. You grip the counter until it passes, then spend the next hour feeling like you've run a sprint you never signed up for.

If this is your daily pattern, you may have heard the term POTS — postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome. It's a condition where standing up triggers an exaggerated heart-rate response and a cascade of symptoms: lightheadedness, brain fog, fatigue that lands hours later, sometimes nausea, sometimes near-fainting. Your blood work looks "normal." Your heart scans look "normal." And yet your body behaves as if gravity itself is the enemy.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has no word for POTS. But it has spent two thousand years studying exactly what POTS puts on display: what happens when the body struggles to keep things up — blood, energy, and clarity — against the constant pull of gravity. This article explores that perspective, and what it may offer as a complementary way to understand and work with your symptoms.

A note before we begin: POTS is a medical diagnosis, usually confirmed with tests like a tilt-table evaluation and cardiac monitoring. If you suspect you have it, please work with a doctor first — POTS can be driven by underlying conditions (anemia, thyroid issues, autoimmune activity, medication side effects) that need proper medical attention. This article is educational: it explains how TCM conceptualizes orthostatic symptoms, and how traditional approaches may support your day-to-day comfort alongside — never instead of — medical care.

What POTS Is — and Why Position Matters So Much

In conventional medicine, POTS is a form of orthostatic intolerance: the body's circulation doesn't adapt smoothly when you move from lying or sitting to standing. Gravity pulls blood toward your lower body, and in a healthy system, reflexes constrict blood vessels and adjust heart rate to keep blood flowing to your brain. In POTS, that adjustment goes haywire. The heart rate jumps sharply — by diagnostic convention, 30 or more beats per minute within ten minutes of standing in adults — while symptoms like dizziness, palpitations, and fatigue follow.

Why does this matter for understanding your fatigue? Because POTS fatigue is positional. It's not the flat, steady tiredness of a long day. It's a fatigue that spikes after being upright, improves when you lie down, and often lands hardest in the morning or after meals — the moments when blood is most likely to pool away from where it's needed.

For a broader map of how TCM approaches chronic fatigue in general, our Fatigue guide covers the main patterns. This article focuses specifically on the orthostatic experience — the dizziness, the racing heart, and the gravity-sensitive exhaustion that defines POTS.

The TCM View: A Gravity Problem, Not a Laziness Problem

Think of your body as a high-rise building with a water system. Water reaches the top floors only if the pumps generate enough pressure to push it against gravity. When the pressure drops, the top floors lose supply first: the taps sputter, the lights flicker, the elevator stutters.

Your head is the top floor. Standing up is the moment gravity tests your internal pump pressure — and in TCM theory, that pressure has a name: Qi, and more specifically the holding and lifting functions of Qi that keep Blood and fluids circulating upward. When Qi is strong, standing feels effortless: blood rises, your mind stays clear, your legs feel steady. When Qi is weak, the moment you stand, the "top floor" runs short — lightheadedness, dimming vision, fog — and the Heart, the body's pump, starts racing to compensate for the pressure it can't generate on its own.

Three TCM concepts come together in this picture, and they map neatly onto the POTS experience:

  • Qi holds things up. In TCM theory, Qi has a lifting and holding function — it keeps Blood in its vessels, organs in their places, and clarity in the head. When this function weakens, the body literally "sags": symptoms worsen with standing and improve with lying down. Our TCM Basics article on Sinking Qi explores this concept in depth.
  • Blood must rise to nourish the head. The brain needs Blood (and the Qi that carries it) to function. When Blood doesn't rise, you get dizziness, brain fog, and a feeling of emptiness in the head. The relationship between Qi and Blood — one pushes, the other carries — is explained in our guide to Qi and Blood.
  • The Heart regulates the pulse. When the body senses low pressure at the top, the Heart responds by speeding up. In TCM, a racing pulse on standing is read as the Heart trying to compensate — a signal worth listening to, not a malfunction to fear.

Four TCM Patterns That Can Look Like POTS

TCM doesn't diagnose "POTS." It looks at how your orthostatic symptoms present — the flavor of your dizziness, your temperature, your digestion, your energy curve — and groups them into patterns. Most people with POTS-like symptoms recognize themselves in one or two of the following.

Pattern 1: Spleen Qi Sinking — The Sagging Water Column

You might have this if: Lightheadedness hits within seconds of standing, worse in the morning and after long upright periods. You feel "low-pressure" — heavy legs, a sagging, dragging sensation, brain fog that clears the moment you lie down. Digestion is often weak: bloating, loose stools, fatigue after meals, a tendency to crave sugar for a quick lift that doesn't last.

The TCM view: The Spleen is the organ system that transforms food into Qi and Blood, and in TCM theory it also holds things up. When Spleen Qi is deficient, the upward force weakens — like a water column that can't hold its height. The first place to show the failure is the top: your head.

What helps: Warm, cooked, easy-to-digest meals (congee, soups, steamed vegetables); small frequent meals rather than large ones; avoiding raw food and cold drinks that add to the Spleen's workload; gentle pressure on ST-36 (below the knee, outer edge) and DU-20 (top of the head) — two points traditionally used to support the lifting function.

Pattern 2: Heart Blood and Qi Deficiency — The Racing Alarm

You might have this if: The dominant symptom is palpitations — your heart pounds or flutters when you stand, sometimes with anxiety, shortness of breath, or a sense of dread. You may also have pale skin, poor memory, restless or light sleep, and a tired, washed-out feeling by mid-afternoon.

The TCM view: The Heart houses the Shen (the mind-spirit), and it needs sufficient Blood to stay calm. When Heart Blood is deficient, the Heart has less cushion — it overreacts to every shift in demand, like a fish in a shallow pond splashing at the slightest movement. The racing heartbeat is the splash. For the deeper framework, see our TCM Basics guide to the Heart and Shen.

What helps: Calming acupressure — PC-6 (inner forearm, two finger-widths above the wrist crease) and HT-7 (inner wrist crease, little-finger side) — held for 1-2 minutes each, especially during a dizzy spell; warm drinks rather than iced; foods traditionally used to nourish Blood: cooked dark leafy greens, red dates, oats, eggs, and bone broths; consistent early sleep.

Pattern 3: Kidney Yang Deficiency — The Reserve Tank on Empty

You might have this if: Your fatigue is deep and chronic, with cold hands and feet, a lower back that aches or feels unsupported, frequent urination (especially at night), legs that feel heavy or "full" after standing, and a sense that your energy never really comes back — even after good sleep. Cold weather makes everything worse.

The TCM view: The Kidneys store Jing, your body's deepest reserve — the battery bank that powers the whole system. Kidney Yang is the warming, activating side of that reserve. When it runs low, the body lacks the warmth and drive to push Blood and fluids upward against gravity; blood settles in the lower body the way water settles in a pond when the pump slows. The heavy legs, the cold, and the unshakeable fatigue are the reserve tank running on empty.

What helps: Warming the lower back and belly with a heat pack or warm compress at night; soaking feet in warm water; grounding movement (slow walking, tai chi) rather than intense exercise; warming foods like cooked root vegetables, black beans, walnuts, and small amounts of ginger; KI-3 (inner ankle, the depression between the ankle bone and Achilles tendon) and CV-4 (below the navel) as points traditionally used to support Kidney energy.

Pattern 4: Phlegm-Damp Obstructing the Clear Orifices — The Cotton-Head Fog

You might have this if: Your dizziness is more of a heavy fog than a tilt — a stuffed, cotton-wool sensation in the head, sometimes with nausea on standing, a muzzy feeling that lingers, worse in humid weather or after heavy, greasy, or sweet meals. You may feel physically heavy overall and notice a thick coating on your tongue in the morning.

The TCM view: When the body's fluid metabolism is sluggish, fluids turn into Dampness — a heavy, turbid "humidity" inside the body. In TCM theory, Dampness can cloud the head like fog over a windshield, making clear perception and stable balance difficult. The classic formula is "Dampness obstructs the clear orifices," and it produces exactly the kind of muddled, heavy-headed dizziness that some people with POTS describe. The concept of Dampness is explained in our TCM Basics guide to Dampness.

What helps: Lighter meals with less fried food, dairy, and refined sugar; draining foods like barley, adzuki beans, celery, and cucumber; regular movement to keep fluids circulating; ST-40 (outer lower leg, midway between knee and ankle) — the point traditionally called the "Phlegm-transforming master" — and SP-9 (inner leg, below the knee) for fluid drainage.

The Standing Test: A Self-Assessment

One of the most useful things about the orthostatic lens is that it gives you a simple experiment — not a diagnosis, but a noticing exercise. If you're exploring what's happening in your body, try this gentle version, ideally with a doctor's awareness and only if you feel safe doing so:

  1. Lie down for five minutes and notice your baseline: heart rate (if you have a monitor), dizziness, fog, energy.
  2. Sit up slowly and wait two minutes. Note what shifts.
  3. Stand (with a wall or chair nearby) and stay for three minutes if you can. Note what arrives: lightheadedness, pounding pulse, heavy legs, fog, nausea — and when it peaks.
  4. Lie down again and watch the symptoms fade. How quickly do they settle?

If you feel faint at any point, sit or lie down immediately — the test is a noticing tool, not a fitness challenge. What you're looking for is the pattern: which symptoms appear on standing, how fast, and how completely they clear when you're horizontal. That pattern is exactly the information a TCM practitioner would use to choose a pattern and a supportive approach.

Daily Support Strategies

Whether or not you pursue TCM treatment formally, several traditional and lifestyle strategies may support the body's upright management system. Think of these as ways to reduce the load on a system that's working overtime against gravity.

Rise Like a Submarine, Not a Jack-in-the-Box

Sudden standing is a pressure shock to a system that's already struggling. Transition in stages: lie → sit for a count of ten → stand with support → pause before walking. In TCM terms, this gives Qi and Blood time to catch up — you're not asking the pump to instantly overcome gravity from a dead stop.

Use Your Legs as a Manual Pump

Your calf muscles are a second pump for venous return — physiologists call it the skeletal muscle pump, and it's the reason leg movement helps move blood upward. Simple counter-maneuvers — crossing your legs and squeezing, clenching your calf and thigh muscles, gently rising onto your toes a few times — can support circulation when you feel a dizzy spell coming on. In TCM language, you're actively helping the Qi move Blood upward instead of waiting for the system to do it alone.

Warm Fluids, Steady Volume

Conventional guidance for POTS often includes increased fluid and salt intake to support blood volume. TCM adds a layer of nuance: it's not just how much you drink, but how warm. In TCM theory, the Spleen transforms fluids into usable Jin-Ye (body fluids) with warmth; cold drinks are seen as adding to the Spleen's workload. Warm water, ginger tea, and broths hydrate while supporting the transformation. If you're managing salt, electrolytes, or fluid restrictions for a medical condition, check with your doctor before changing anything.

Compression as External Support

Thigh-high compression garments are sometimes recommended in conventional POTS care to reduce blood pooling in the legs — essentially external "guy wires" that keep the blood column from sagging. From a TCM perspective, this is a physical expression of the Qi-holding function: supporting the structure from the outside while you work on rebuilding the force from within. Talk to your healthcare team about whether compression is appropriate for you.

A Calming Acupressure Sequence

For a daily reset, try this 5-minute sequence, pressing each point gently in small circles for about a minute on each side:

  • DU-20 (top of the head) — traditionally used to raise sinking Qi and clear the head
  • ST-36 (outer lower leg, below the kneecap) — supports the Spleen's energy production
  • PC-6 (inner forearm) — calms palpitations and nausea
  • KI-3 (inner ankle) — supports Kidney reserve

Skip SP-6 (inner lower leg, above the ankle) if you are pregnant — it is traditionally avoided during pregnancy.

Protect the Recharge Window

In TCM theory, the hours before midnight are when the Kidney system — your reserve tank — does its deepest recharging. For someone whose reserve is already low, protecting early sleep is one of the highest-leverage habits available. Aim for lights out by 10:30-11 PM, and view evening screens as a drain on the recharge cycle.

What Research Suggests

POTS is a young field in Western medicine, and the research on TCM specifically for POTS is still emerging — but several threads are worth noting:

  • The diagnostic frame is well established. Expert consensus (e.g., the 2015 Heart Rhythm Society position statement) defines POTS as a heart-rate increase of 30+ beats per minute within 10 minutes of standing, without orthostatic hypotension, alongside symptoms of orthostatic intolerance. This makes the "gravity lens" a recognized, measurable feature of the condition — not a metaphor imposed on it.
  • Post-viral onset is documented. Some studies suggest a notable share of POTS cases begin after a viral illness, and reports during the COVID-19 pandemic described new-onset POTS-like symptoms in a subset of patients. This aligns with the TCM observation that severe illness can drain Qi and Blood, leaving the body's lifting capacity weakened afterward.
  • Counter-maneuvers have a physiological basis. Research on venous return shows that lower-limb muscle activation (leg crossing, calf clenching) measurably supports blood flow back to the heart — the mechanism behind the "manual pump" strategies recommended in POTS care.
  • Acupressure and acupuncture are being studied for related symptoms. Small studies suggest acupressure at PC-6 may help with nausea, and preliminary research has explored acupuncture for orthostatic dizziness and related autonomic symptoms — promising but far from conclusive, so frame these as comfort and support measures rather than stand-alone interventions.
Practical Takeaways:
  • Get a proper medical evaluation first — POTS has a diagnostic pathway (tilt-table, cardiac workup), and underlying causes like anemia or thyroid issues must be ruled out before any complementary approach makes sense.
  • Use the standing test to learn your pattern: which symptoms appear on standing, how fast, and how fully they clear when you lie down.
  • Identify your TCM pattern — Spleen Qi sinking (lightheaded, low-pressure feel), Heart Blood deficiency (palpitations, anxiety), Kidney Yang deficiency (cold, deep fatigue, heavy legs), or Dampness (cotton-head fog, nausea) — and match your support to it.
  • Build the daily habits: staged rising, calf-pump counter-maneuvers, warm fluids, warming meals, compression if your care team approves, and early sleep.
  • Work with a licensed TCM practitioner if you explore acupuncture or herbs — pattern diagnosis is a skilled art, and herbs are powerful substances that deserve professional guidance.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ Seek medical attention promptly if you faint or lose consciousness; experience chest pain, severe shortness of breath, or a heartbeat that is irregular, very fast, or racing while at rest; notice blood in your stool or vomit; or develop sudden, unexplained weight loss or fever. If your dizziness began after starting a new medication, contact your prescriber. And if you suspect POTS, request a proper evaluation — a tilt-table test and cardiac workup — rather than self-diagnosing. TCM is a complementary perspective: it can help you understand and support your body, but it does not replace medical diagnosis or care. Also see a doctor if standing symptoms begin suddenly, worsen significantly, or interfere with daily function.
