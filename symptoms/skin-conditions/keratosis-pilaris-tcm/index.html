Melasma and Skin Pigmentation: A TCM View of Liver and Spleen

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

You do a double take in the bathroom mirror. That patch on your cheek — the one you have been hoping was just a shadow or a smudge — has decided to stay. It is not painful. It does not itch. It just sits there, darker than the skin around it, refusing to fade no matter what you wash it with or how much concealer you layer on.

If you have melasma, you know this scene intimately. The brown or gray-brown patches that spread across the cheeks, forehead, upper lip, or chin. The way they deepen after sun exposure, during pregnancy, or in stressful seasons of life. The quiet frustration of a condition that is not dangerous, not contagious, but deeply visible — and stubbornly resistant to the usual skincare routine.

Melasma is common — it affects millions of people, most of them women, and some estimates suggest up to half of pregnant women notice some darkening (it is often called the "mask of pregnancy"). Dermatology has effective ways to lighten it, yet the patches frequently return, especially after sun exposure. That pattern — it comes back — is exactly what Traditional Chinese Medicine has a distinctive way of explaining.

TCM does not approach melasma as a surface problem to be bleached away. It reads the pigment as a signal — like silt settling in a slow-moving river — and asks what, deeper in the body, is slowing the flow.

The Western View: What Dermatology Knows About Melasma

In conventional dermatology, melasma is understood as a disorder of pigment production. The melanocytes — the skin cells that make melanin, the pigment that gives skin its color — become hyperactive in certain areas and deposit excess pigment in the upper layers of the skin.

Three factors are consistently implicated:

  • UV exposure: Sunlight directly stimulates melanocytes to produce more pigment. This is why melasma almost always worsens in summer and in sunny climates.
  • Hormones: Melasma is strongly linked to estrogen and progesterone. It can be triggered or worsened by pregnancy, birth control pills, hormone replacement therapy, and thyroid changes. Some studies suggest melanocytes themselves carry receptors for these hormones — meaning the pigment cells are directly listening to the hormonal signal.
  • Genetics and skin type: Melasma runs in families and is more common in people with medium to dark skin tones, who naturally have more active melanocytes.

Conventional options include lightening agents, prescription creams, chemical peels, and laser or light therapies. These can be effective, but the pigment often reappears — especially when sun exposure continues — which leads many people to feel like they are fighting the same battle every year.

A Different Lens: Pigment as Sediment in a Slowing River

Here is where TCM offers a genuinely different frame. In TCM theory, the face is not just skin — it is a visible surface where the state of your Qi and Blood shows through. And changes in facial color are read the way a riverbank reads the current: where the water moves well, the riverbed stays clean; where the current slows, silt settles and stains.

In that picture, melanin pigment behaves like silt. The Liver, in TCM, is the organ that governs the smooth, even flow of Qi and Blood through the whole system — the current itself. When that flow is smooth, pigment is carried evenly and the complexion looks even. When the flow stagnates — from stress, frustration, hormonal shifts, or sluggish digestion — pigment settles in patches, like sediment dropping out of slow water. This is why, from a TCM perspective, the most useful question is not "what can I put on this patch?" but "what is slowing the river?" — and why the answer so often involves the Liver, Spleen, and Kidneys rather than the skin itself. The Face Map: Where Your Patches Sit, and What It May Suggest

TCM face reading — one branch of traditional diagnosis — treats different facial regions as windows onto different organ systems. While interpretations vary between lineages, a common map looks like this:

  • Forehead: often associated with the Heart
  • Cheeks: frequently read as the Liver (and Lung) zones
  • Nose and around the mouth: the Spleen and Stomach (digestive) zone
  • Upper lip and chin: traditionally linked to the Kidneys and the reproductive system

Dermatology groups melasma into three distribution patterns — centrofacial (forehead, cheeks, nose, upper lip), malar (cheeks), and mandibular (jawline). Notice the overlap: the areas where melasma most often appears — cheeks, upper lip, chin — are precisely the zones that TCM face reading associates with the Liver and the Kidney-reproductive axis. TCM practitioners find this convergence meaningful: the two systems most involved in stress response and hormonal rhythm are the two systems whose facial "zones" show the pigment.

Pattern 1: Liver Qi Stagnation with Blood Stasis — The Logjam

What it feels like: patches that arrived or deepened during a demanding season of life — a stressful job, a difficult relationship, a period of suppressed frustration. You might notice you sigh a lot, carry tension in your shoulders or jaw, feel irritable or "wound up," and have PMS-style breast tenderness or an irregular cycle.

Why it matters for melasma: in TCM theory, the Liver's core job is smooth flow — of Qi, of Blood, of emotion. Chronic stress creates stagnation: the current slows, and pigment settles like silt behind a logjam. This is the most commonly described TCM pattern for melasma, often named Liver depression with Blood stasis — the stress story written on the cheeks.

Traditionally supportive habits: daily movement that releases tension (brisk walks, qigong, yoga), breathing practices that shift the nervous system, and teas traditionally used to ease Qi flow — mint, rose bud, or chrysanthemum. Acupressure on the Liver point LV-3 (in the web between the big toe and second toe) is a classic for supporting smooth flow.

Pattern 2: Spleen Qi Deficiency with Dampness — The Muddy Complexion

What it feels like: a diffuse, dingy brown or yellowish tone rather than sharp, well-defined patches. Often paired with fatigue after meals, heavy limbs, loose stools, brain fog, and a tendency to feel "waterlogged" — puffiness, bloating, or weight that hangs on.

Why it matters for melasma: the Spleen, in TCM, transforms food into Qi and Blood and transports fluids — it is the body's processing plant. When Spleen Qi is weak, fluids do not move cleanly; they accumulate as Dampness, and the complexion takes on a muddy, stirred-water quality. The pigment here is not a sharp stain — it is more like clay suspended in slow water, dulling the whole face.

Traditionally supportive habits: warm, cooked, easily digested meals; regular eating times; gentle movement after eating; and foods like pumpkin, sweet potato, and congee that are traditionally considered gentle on the Spleen. Reducing raw salads, cold drinks, excess sugar, and dairy is the classic advice for a damp constitution. The Stomach-36 point (ST-36, just below the kneecap) is the go-to for supporting digestion.

Pattern 3: Kidney Yin Deficiency — The Hormonal Shift

What it feels like: patches that appeared alongside a hormonal milestone — pregnancy (the "mask of pregnancy"), starting or stopping birth control, perimenopause, or menopause. Often accompanied by classic Yin-deficiency signs: night sweats, hot flashes, dry skin or eyes, low back ache, feeling "wired but tired," and restless sleep.

Why it matters for melasma: in TCM, the Kidneys store Jing — the body's deepest reserve — and govern the reproductive-hormonal rhythm. When Kidney Yin runs low, the system runs hot and dry, and the pigment-regulating side of the hormonal axis shifts. TCM describes this as Empty Heat: a low-grade, drying warmth that darkens rather than inflames. This pattern maps neatly onto melasma's strong hormonal trigger — including its tendency to appear on the upper lip and chin, the face-reading zones linked to the reproductive system.

Traditionally supportive habits: foods traditionally used to nourish Yin — black sesame, black beans, goji berries, mulberries, and bone broths; consistent, early sleep; and gentle, restorative movement rather than exhausting workouts. The Kidney point KI-3 (in the hollow behind the inner ankle bone) is a classic for supporting Yin reserves.

Pattern 4: Liver Blood Deficiency — The Faded Canvas

What it feels like: uneven pigmentation alongside skin that generally looks tired — dry, dull, missing the even flush it once had. Often paired with dry eyes, brittle nails, a scanty cycle, fatigue, and poor sleep.

Why it matters for melasma: in TCM, Blood nourishes the skin the way water nourishes soil. When Blood is thin or depleted — after long periods of stress, heavy cycles, poor sleep, or inadequate nourishment — the skin loses its even, watered quality and shows patchy wear. The pigment here is not an explosion of color; it is a canvas where the paint has faded unevenly, leaving some areas darker than others. For more on how TCM understands this state, see our TCM Basics guide to Blood Deficiency.

Traditionally supportive habits: nourishing foods in their traditional TCM forms — dark leafy greens, red dates, goji berries, eggs, and organ meats if you eat them; prioritizing sleep (the Liver replenishes Blood during rest); and gentle acupressure on SP-6 (above the inner ankle) and SP-10 (inner side, above the kneecap) — points traditionally used to build and move Blood.

Which Pattern Sounds Like You? A Quick Self-Check

Most people are a blend — the patterns feed each other. Use this quick scan to find where your emphasis sits:

  • Stress-driven patches + sighing, tension, irritability → Liver Qi stagnation emphasis. Prioritize movement, breathwork, and the LV-3 point.
  • Diffuse muddy tone + post-meal fatigue + loose stools → Spleen Dampness emphasis. Prioritize warm cooked meals and ST-36.
  • Patches tied to pregnancy or hormones + night heat + dry skin → Kidney Yin emphasis. Prioritize Yin-nourishing foods, early sleep, and KI-3.
  • Uneven dull tone + dry eyes or nails + fatigue → Blood deficiency emphasis. Prioritize nourishing food, early nights, and SP-6/SP-10.

What May Support Clearer Skin Tone: A TCM-Informed Daily Approach

None of this is about replacing your dermatologist's advice — it is a complementary layer. Here is what a TCM-informed approach to melasma looks like in practice:

1. Sun protection, without negotiation

This is the one step every framework agrees on. In TCM terms, UV is a form of external Heat that stirs the pigment; in dermatology terms, it directly stimulates melanocytes. Broad-spectrum sunscreen, a hat, and shade-seeking behavior are the foundation — without them, every other effort is working against the strongest trigger. Some research suggests melasma is driven by visible light as well as UV, so a physical (mineral) sunscreen or a tinted product may offer broader shielding.

2. Gentle facial circulation

If pigment is silt, helping the micro-circulation of the face is like keeping the tributaries moving. Traditional tools like gua sha and facial massage are gentle, low-risk ways to support facial circulation — see our companion guide to facial massage and gua sha for skin health for step-by-step technique. The key word is gentle: melasma-prone skin is often sensitive, and harsh scrubbing or aggressive tools may worsen irritation.

3. Acupressure: a 3-minute daily reset

Acupressure is the self-care version of acupuncture — firm, comfortable pressure held for 30 to 60 seconds per point. A simple daily sequence for melasma support:

Point Location Traditional use
LV-3 (Taichong) Web between big toe and second toe Liver Qi flow — for the stress pattern
SP-6 (Sanyinjiao) Four finger-widths above the inner ankle Blood and hormonal balance
SP-10 (Xuehai) Inner side, just above the kneecap "Sea of Blood" — circulation
ST-36 (Zusanli) Four finger-widths below the kneecap, outer side Spleen Qi and digestion
LI-4 (Hegu) Web between thumb and index finger Face and head channel — facial concerns
If you are pregnant: melasma is common in pregnancy, but two of these points — SP-6 and LI-4 — are traditionally considered off-limits during pregnancy, and many practitioners advise avoiding acupressure below the waist altogether. If pregnant, skip the acupressure points and lean on sun protection, food, sleep, and your healthcare provider's guidance.

4. Eat for your pattern

The diet advice lives in each pattern card above. The through-line: warm, cooked, whole foods; enough protein and dark leafy greens to feed the Blood; and cutting way back on the fuels of stagnation — excess sugar, alcohol, fried food, and cold drinks. For the full food framework, see our guide to TCM dietary principles for skin health.

5. Sleep and stress — the quiet half of the picture

In TCM, the Liver stores Blood during rest; a late, restless night is like keeping the river running shallow. And the stress-pigment link is not only metaphor — see the research below. Prioritizing consistent sleep and building in even ten minutes a day of breath-focused calm is, in this framework, skin care.

What Research Says

Science and TCM use different languages, but on melasma they converge in interesting places:

  • The hormonal mechanism is real: some studies suggest melanocytes carry receptors for estrogen and progesterone, giving a biological basis for melasma's strong link to pregnancy and hormonal medications — the same terrain TCM maps to the Kidney and Liver systems.
  • Stress and pigment share a pathway: a single precursor molecule in the body is cleaved to produce both the stress hormone ACTH and alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (alpha-MSH), which directly stimulates pigment production. Some research suggests chronic stress may influence pigmentation through this shared pathway — a striking echo of the TCM link between Liver Qi stagnation and facial darkening.
  • Sun protection is the best-evidenced step: broad-spectrum photoprotection, including against visible light, is consistently supported as the foundation of melasma care in dermatology research.
  • Conventional options work but recur: lightening agents, prescription creams, and lasers show results in studies, yet recurrence after sun exposure is well documented — matching the TCM view that if you only lighten the surface without addressing the flow, the silt returns.
  • TCM research is early but real: a Cochrane review of Chinese herbal medicine for melasma found the evidence base too limited for firm conclusions, while smaller studies of pattern-based herbal formulas — especially those aimed at Liver depression and Blood stasis — report preliminary promise. The honest summary is: a promising framework with emerging evidence.

When to See a Doctor

Melasma itself is harmless — but any pigmented spot deserves a professional look. See a dermatologist if you notice any spot that:
  • is asymmetric or has irregular borders
  • contains multiple colors — especially black, blue, or red within a brown patch
  • is growing, changing, itching, or bleeding
  • is larger than a pencil eraser (about 6 mm)
  • is new after age 40, or simply looks different from your other spots

These are the classic warning signs dermatologists use to screen for skin cancer, and they should always be checked by a professional — no cream, herbal or otherwise, is a substitute for that evaluation. If you are pregnant, talk to your obstetrician before starting any new supplement, tea, or skincare routine. And if melasma appears suddenly without any clear trigger, or comes with other symptoms, get it assessed.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  • Sun protection is the foundation. Broad-spectrum sunscreen, hats, and shade — every day, not just sunny ones. Without it, little else moves the needle.
  • Read your face, not just your patch. Stress-driven cheek patches, diffuse muddy tone, hormone-tied lip-and-chin patches, and tired dull skin point to different patterns — Liver, Spleen, Kidney, Blood — and different daily habits.
  • Support the flow. Daily movement, breathwork, and gentle facial gua sha or massage align with the TCM view that pigment is silt in a slow river. Gentle is the operative word.
  • Eat for your pattern. Warm cooked meals for the Spleen, Yin-nourishing foods (black sesame, goji, black beans) for hormonal shifts, and enough protein and leafy greens to feed the Blood.
  • Sleep is skin care. The Liver replenishes Blood during rest; consistent, early sleep supports every pattern above.
  • Get the patch checked. Melasma is benign, but only a professional can confirm that. See a dermatologist for any changing, multi-colored, or irregular spot.
