Varicose Veins and Blood Stasis: A TCM View on Heavy, Aching Legs

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

By late afternoon, your legs feel like they're filled with sand. You stand up from your desk and the first few steps are stiff and heavy — a dull, full ache that only eases when you put your feet up. Maybe you've also noticed the veins: a web of blue and purple lines creeping across your calves, or a knot behind your knee that wasn't there a few years ago.

If you've mentioned it to a doctor, you've probably heard the same two words: cosmetic and genetic. And both are partly true. But neither explains the actual feeling — the heaviness, the aching, the way your legs seem to "fill up" over the course of a day. That gap between the medical explanation and the lived experience is exactly where Traditional Chinese Medicine has something useful to say.

Varicose veins are extremely common. Studies suggest roughly one in four adults has them, and the numbers climb with age, standing-heavy jobs, pregnancy, and family history. In Western medicine they're understood as veins whose one-way valves have weakened. What TCM adds is a different question: why does the same amount of standing, sitting, or aging leave one person's legs clear and another person's veins bulging? The answer, in TCM theory, comes down to one word: flow.

The Western View: What Varicose Veins Actually Are

To understand the TCM view, it helps to start with the mechanics. Every vein in your leg is a one-way street. Tiny flap-like valves inside the vein open to let blood move upward — back toward your heart — and close to stop it from sliding back down. Your calf muscles are the engine: every step, every ankle flex, squeezes the veins and pushes blood uphill. This is why the calf is sometimes called the "second heart."

When the valves weaken — through genetics, pregnancy, aging, or years of standing — blood begins to pool in the lower legs. The vein walls stretch under the extra pressure, the valves stop sealing properly, and the vein bulges. Western medicine calls the whole picture chronic venous insufficiency (CVI): the leg's return pipeline is underperforming. Symptoms include that familiar heaviness, aching, swelling around the ankles by evening, and sometimes leg cramps at night.

Because varicose veins rarely cause dangerous problems by themselves, they're often treated as a cosmetic issue — something to be removed or ignored. But the heaviness and aching are real, and they're signals from a system under strain. That's where the TCM lens earns its keep.

A Different Lens: Legs as a Gravity Problem

TCM looks at the body the way an engineer looks at a building: everything depends on things moving through pipes — and the pipes are only as good as the pressure behind them and the clearance inside them. In TCM, that moving substance is Blood, and the force that keeps it moving is Qi — the body's operating system, the invisible infrastructure that runs every process.

Now picture the legs as the basement of that building. Blood has to travel up from the basement against gravity, through one-way doors, powered by the pump in your calves. When everything works, the return trip is effortless. When flow slows, the blood pools — and a pooled, stagnant river is exactly what a bulging, twisted vein looks like. In TCM terms, this is Blood Stasis: blood that is present but not moving well, like water that has stopped circulating and started settling.

For the broader picture of what Blood Stasis is, how to recognize it, and how it shows up across the whole body, see our TCM Basics guide to Blood Stasis. What follows is the leg-specific version: the patterns that create the heavy, aching feeling, and what may support better flow.

Pattern 1: Blood Stasis — The Stuck River

What it feels like: a dull, deep ache that builds through the day, legs that feel "full" or heavy, visible bulging or twisted veins that look dark blue or purple, cramps at night, and clear relief when you elevate your legs. The ache tends to settle in fixed spots — often the inner calf or behind the knee — like sediment that has collected in one bend of a river.

Why it matters for varicose veins: in TCM, this is the classic varicose pattern. Blood has pooled in the lower legs because the return trip is struggling: the pump (calf muscles) may be underused, the channels may be congested, or the walls themselves may lack tone. The visible vein is the river channel swollen with water that isn't moving on. This is also why the legs feel worse at the end of the day — gravity has been winning all day, and nothing has pushed the water back uphill.

Traditionally supportive habits: regular movement — the single most direct way to support circulation; hourly breaks from sitting and standing; ankle pumps (point and flex the feet 20 times) to switch on the calf pump; elevating the legs for 15 minutes after work; and foods traditionally used to move Blood — dark berries, ginger, turmeric, and warm soups. Gentle acupressure on BL-57 (in the belly of the calf) and SP-6 (above the inner ankle) is a classic pairing for supporting lower-leg flow.

Pattern 2: Spleen Qi Deficiency with Dampness — The Heavy-Legged Slump

What it feels like: legs that feel heavy and leaden rather than sharp or achy — as if they belong to someone else. Mild puffiness around the ankles by late afternoon, socks leaving deeper marks than they used to. The heaviness is worse in humid weather and after rich, sugary, or heavy meals. Digestion may be sluggish too: bloating, soft stools, an afternoon energy crash.

Why it matters for varicose veins: in TCM, the Spleen is the body's power plant — it converts food into Qi and Blood, and it's also the system in charge of holding things up and keeping fluids moving. When Spleen Qi runs low, two things happen: muscle tone drops (the calf pump gets weaker), and fluids start pooling in the lower body like humidity in a basement. The result is a heavy, waterlogged feeling that sits on top of any vein congestion. If your main complaint is pitting swelling rather than visible veins, our guide to fluid retention (edema) in TCM goes deeper into that territory.

Traditionally supportive habits: warm, cooked meals — the Spleen's favorite fuel; ginger, millet, sweet potato, pumpkin, and broth-based soups; fewer raw salads, cold drinks, and refined sugar; small frequent meals instead of one heavy one; brisk walks after eating; and gentle acupressure on ST-36 (below the kneecap) and SP-9 (on the inner shin, below the knee) — points traditionally used to support the Spleen and drain lower-body Dampness.

Pattern 3: Liver Qi Stagnation — The Kinked Hose

What it feels like: legs that feel tight, achy, and restless rather than simply heavy — often worse during stressful seasons or after long, tense days at a desk. You might notice you sit with your legs crossed, your jaw and shoulders are frequently tight, you sigh a lot, and the ache shifts around from day to day instead of settling in one spot.

Why it matters for varicose veins: in TCM, the Liver governs the smooth flow of Qi through the entire body — it's the traffic controller of the internal highway system. Chronic stress keeps the controller gridlocked: Qi moves sluggishly, tissues stay tense, and the legs — the farthest exits on the highway — show the congestion first. Think of a garden hose with a kink: the water is there, but it can't move. Releasing the tension in the system is part of restoring flow in the legs. For more on this dynamic, see our TCM Basics guide to Qi Stagnation.

Traditionally supportive habits: daily movement that releases tension — brisk walks, qigong, stretching, anything that gets the breath moving; short movement breaks every hour; breathwork with a long exhale; and gentle acupressure on LV-3 (in the web between the big toe and second toe), a point traditionally used to smooth Liver Qi. Warm, aromatic foods like ginger, scallions, and mild spices support flow without adding heaviness.

Which Pattern Sounds Like You? A Quick Self-Check

Most people are a blend — the patterns feed each other. Use this quick scan to find where your emphasis sits:

  • Dull ache that builds all day + full heavy legs + visible bulging veins + relief when you elevate → Blood Stasis emphasis. Prioritize movement, ankle pumps, elevation, and BL-57/SP-6.
  • Legs feel leaden + ankle puffiness + worse after meals and in humidity + sluggish digestion → Spleen Qi / Dampness emphasis. Prioritize warm cooked meals, ginger, and ST-36/SP-9.
  • Tight, restless ache + stress flare-ups + tense jaw and shoulders + shifting discomfort → Liver Qi Stagnation emphasis. Prioritize daily movement, breathwork, and LV-3.

What May Support Leg Comfort: A TCM-Informed Daily Approach

None of this is a replacement for medical care, and varicose veins that cause significant discomfort deserve a conversation with a doctor. But if you're looking for daily habits that work with your body's own circulation, the TCM-informed approach is practical and low-tech:

1. Switch on the second heart, every hour

Your calf muscles are the pump that pushes blood back uphill — and they only work when you move. Set a timer to stand, walk, or do 20 ankle pumps (point, flex, point, flex) every hour. If you have a standing job, do the reverse: sit or walk for a few minutes each hour, and shift your weight constantly. The goal is the same — keep the pump cycling instead of letting blood settle.

2. Elevate at the end of the day

Fifteen minutes with your legs propped above heart level — on a stack of pillows, an ottoman, or up a wall — gives gravity a day off. In TCM terms, it lets pooled Blood and Dampness drain back toward the center of the body. Many people notice the heaviness lifts noticeably after a week of consistent elevation breaks.

3. A gentle acupressure mini-routine

Use 1–2 minutes per point, small circles with comfortable (not deep) pressure, on both legs:

  • BL-57 (Chengshan) — in the belly of the calf, the "support the mountain" point for lower-leg flow.
  • SP-6 (Sanyinjiao) — above the inner ankle, where three Yin channels meet; traditionally used to support Blood and fluids. Skip this point during pregnancy.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — below the kneecap, the classic point for building Qi and Blood.
  • LV-3 (Taichong) — between the big toe and second toe, for smooth flow when stress is part of the picture.

Safety first: never press directly on a varicose vein — work gently on the tissue beside it, and avoid the back of the knee and the groin. If you take blood thinners or have a history of blood clots, check with your doctor before any massage-style self-care. For a tool-based approach to leg circulation — done safely around veins — see our Gua Sha for Legs guide.

4. Warmth and water

Warmth moves blood; cold and stillness let it settle. A warm (not hot) foot or leg soak in the evening can be deeply relieving after a standing-heavy day. Stay well hydrated — thicker blood moves less easily — and favor warm drinks over iced ones. Foods that traditionally support flow include ginger, turmeric, dark berries, and warm soups; foods that tend to create heaviness include fried food, excess sugar, and heavy dairy.

5. Compression: where the two frameworks agree

Graduated compression stockings — the Western standard for symptomatic varicose veins — work on exactly the same logic as the TCM approach: they help blood move back up instead of pooling. Many people find them genuinely helpful for the heavy, aching feeling. They're not a substitute for movement, but they're a complement both traditions would endorse. Ask a pharmacist or doctor about the right compression level for you.

Research: What Modern Science Says

Scientific research on varicose veins maps neatly onto the TCM framework — most of it comes down to supporting the return trip:

  • The valve-and-pooling mechanism is well documented. Ultrasound studies of chronic venous insufficiency show that valve failure produces blood reflux and pooling in the lower legs — the "one-way street with a broken door" picture, and the physical basis of the TCM concept of Blood Stasis.
  • The calf pump really matters. Small studies of ankle range-of-motion exercises and heel raises have found measurable improvements in venous return — direct support for the "second heart" framing, and for the TCM emphasis on movement as the first-line circulation tool.
  • Compression helps symptoms. Systematic reviews of graduated compression stockings suggest they may reduce leg pain and swelling in chronic venous insufficiency, with the caveat that results vary and comfort matters for adherence.
  • Some plant extracts show promise. A Cochrane review of horse chestnut seed extract (a traditional European remedy, not a TCM herb) found it may reduce leg pain and swelling in CVI compared with placebo — an interesting parallel to the TCM tradition of using moving, warming botanicals for leg heaviness. It can interact with blood thinners, so it belongs behind a conversation with your doctor, not in your shopping cart.
  • Acupuncture research is early-stage. Some preliminary studies have explored acupuncture for chronic venous insufficiency, with early results suggesting possible symptom improvements; larger, higher-quality trials are still needed.
When to See a Doctor: Varicose veins are usually harmless, but some symptoms are not. Seek medical attention promptly if you notice: sudden swelling, pain, warmth, or redness in one leg (possible deep vein thrombosis — a medical emergency); chest pain or shortness of breath; a varicose vein that bleeds; a hard, painful, red cord along a vein; a sore or ulcer near your ankle that isn't improving; or skin around the ankle becoming hard, dark, and discolored. Leg pain that is new, severe, or accompanied by fever also warrants a medical check. The self-care approaches in this article are for everyday heaviness and discomfort — not for these signals.
Practical Takeaways:
  • Think of your calf muscles as your second heart — switch the pump on every hour with a walk or 20 ankle pumps.
  • Learn your pattern: stuck river (Blood Stasis), heavy slump (Spleen Qi + Dampness), or kinked hose (Liver Qi Stagnation) — and target your habits accordingly.
  • Elevate for 15 minutes after standing-heavy days; warmth, hydration, and movement all support flow.
  • Acupressure gently on BL-57, SP-6, ST-36, and LV-3 — never directly on the vein, and skip SP-6 during pregnancy.
  • Compression stockings and movement work together — both support the return trip that varicose veins struggle with.
