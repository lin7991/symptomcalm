Hidradenitis Suppurativa: A TCM View of Recurrent Boil-Like Lumps

Symptom Guide · 8 min read

You know the pattern. A tender lump appears under your arm, or in your groin, or beneath a breast. You assume it is an ingrown hair or an infected follicle. You wait. It swells, throbs, and finally drains — leaving a small scar you barely notice. And then, weeks or months later, another one arrives. Often in the same region. Sometimes right next to the old scar. The doctor may have mentioned hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) — or you may have never heard the name at all.

HS is one of the most misunderstood skin conditions in dermatology. It is not caused by poor hygiene, and it is not contagious. It is a chronic inflammatory condition of the skin folds, and for many people it arrives with shame, frustration, and a long list of "have you tried antibacterial soap?" questions. This article looks at HS through a different lens — the lens of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) — and explains why this condition is so stubbornly recurrent, and what a TCM perspective may add to your understanding and daily routine.

What Hidradenitis Suppurativa Actually Is

From a Western medical perspective, HS is a chronic inflammatory skin disease that affects areas of the body where skin rubs against skin — most commonly the armpits, groin, buttocks, and under the breasts. It typically begins after puberty and is more common in women. Estimates suggest it affects roughly 1–4% of the population, though many cases go unrecognized for years.

The condition starts when hair follicles become blocked and inflamed deep beneath the surface. Instead of clearing quickly like a typical pimple, the inflammation builds into painful, boil-like nodules and abscesses that may drain pus or blood. Over time, repeated flares in the same location can create tunnel-like tracts under the skin and permanent scarring.

Here is the part that matters most: HS is not a hygiene problem. Washing more aggressively, scrubbing harder, or switching to stronger soaps will not resolve it — and harsh cleansing can actually worsen inflammation. Conventional care focuses on reducing inflammation (topical or systemic anti-inflammatories, sometimes antibiotics for secondary infection), and in more severe cases, surgical or laser options. A dermatologist is the right person to guide any of these choices.

The TCM Lens: Why the Same Spot Keeps Coming Back

In TCM theory, the skin is the body's outermost surface — the place where internal patterns become visible. When a condition keeps returning to the same territory, TCM asks a question that recurrence-focused medicine rarely does: what in the internal environment keeps re-creating it?

For HS, three TCM concepts come together: Damp-Heat (the swamp), Heat Toxin (the rooted lump), and the Spleen–Liver terrain (the fuel that keeps feeding both). If you have read our guides on Damp-Heat in skin or Blood Heat and acne, you already know the fundamentals — this article focuses specifically on why HS behaves like a deep, recurring boil rather than a surface rash, and what that distinction means in practice.

Pattern 1: Damp-Heat in the Skin Folds (The Swamp)

Think of the body's skin folds — armpits, groin, under-breast creases — as low-lying valleys where air moves slowly and moisture collects. In TCM terms, these are classic gathering points for Dampness: fluid that the body has failed to process and move along. Add Heat — from rich food, alcohol, stress, or friction — and the valleys become warm, stagnant swamps. This is the Damp-Heat pattern, and it is the most common lens through which TCM views HS.

Signs of the Damp-Heat pattern

  • Flares that feel moist, weepy, or slow to dry — the "swamp" quality
  • Lumps that appear or worsen after hot, humid weather, sweating, or tight clothing
  • Flare-ups after greasy, fried, or sugary foods, dairy, or alcohol
  • Digestive signals alongside the skin: bloating, heaviness after meals, sticky stools, a thick or greasy tongue coating in the morning
  • A tendency to feel heavy, sluggish, or foggy — the classic Dampness body feeling

For this pattern, the traditional logic is to drain the swamp rather than just swat the mosquitoes. Cooling, draining foods — cucumber, celery, leafy greens, mung beans, barley, and adzuki bean broths — are the classic allies, while greasy, sweet, and spicy inputs are the fuel that keeps the swamp warm. Keeping skin folds dry and breathing (loose fabrics, gentle drying after showers) supports the same logic from the outside.

Pattern 2: Heat Toxin Deep in the Channels (The Rooted Lump)

A typical pimple is shallow. HS lumps are deep — they feel like something rooted under the skin, and they keep returning to the same spot. In TCM language, this depth and stubbornness point to Heat Toxin: a more intense, more entrenched form of Heat that has burrowed into the channels rather than lingering at the surface.

The best analogy is a dandelion. You can break off the leaves and the flower — the visible part — and the plant looks gone. But if the root remains intact, the dandelion regrows. Cutting the visible lump off the surface does not address the root; that is why HS lesions have a habit of returning to familiar ground.

Signs of the Heat Toxin pattern

  • Deep, hard, painful nodules that feel "rooted" rather than superficial
  • Red or dark-purple lumps that burn or throb
  • Recurrence in the exact same location, often near a previous scar
  • Lumps that take a long time to surface or drain — and sometimes never fully settle
  • A history of repeated courses of antibiotics with partial or temporary results

Heat Toxin responds to clearing rather than warming. Cooling foods — bitter greens, watercress, mung bean soup, cucumber, and watermelon — are traditionally used to help clear Heat from the body's deeper layers. Many herbal formulas for "toxic heat" skin conditions center on cooling, clearing herbs; at food level, think of the bitter and cooling end of the spectrum. There is one firm rule in TCM practice that Western advice happens to agree with: never squeeze or pop HS lesions. Popping forces inflammation deeper and can convert a nodule into a draining tract.

Pattern 3: The Spleen–Liver Terrain (The Fuel That Re-Feeds the Fire)

Patterns 1 and 2 describe the fire. Pattern 3 describes the fuel. In TCM, the Spleen is the organ system responsible for transforming food into usable energy and moving fluid through the body — think of it as the body's water-treatment plant. When the Spleen is overtaxed by irregular eating, excess sugar, alcohol, or heavy meals, its fluid-processing slows down, and the "unprocessed" residue is exactly what TCM calls Dampness. Dampness is the raw material for the swamp.

The Liver, meanwhile, is the system that keeps Qi — the body's vital energy — moving smoothly. Stress, frustration, and hormonal shifts all create Liver Qi stagnation, which over time generates internal Heat. This is why many people notice HS flares around stressful periods or, for women, around their menstrual cycle. The terrain (Spleen) produces the dampness; the traffic jam (Liver) produces the heat. Together they re-stoke the fire.

Signs this pattern is in the mix

  • Flare-ups track your stress levels, deadlines, or cycle — not just what you ate
  • Digestive fatigue: you feel tired or heavy after eating, especially after bread, dairy, or sugar
  • Bloating, loose or sticky stools, sugar cravings, afternoon energy crashes
  • Irritability, sighing, jaw clenching, or shoulder tension alongside skin flares
  • Flares that improve when you sleep well and get moving — and worsen when you do neither

Supporting this terrain looks less like a skincare routine and more like daily rhythm: warm, cooked meals eaten at regular times, regular movement (a walk moves Liver Qi better than any supplement), and sleep that actually restores. In TCM logic, you are not chasing the lumps — you are changing the soil they grow in.

Daily Support: What May Help (and What to Avoid)

TCM is not a substitute for dermatological care in HS — but it offers a coherent framework for the things you can do every day, most of which are gentle, low-cost, and hard to do wrong.

Foods to favor

  • Cooling, draining vegetables: cucumber, celery, leafy greens, zucchini, winter melon
  • Clearing staples: mung bean soup, barley, adzuki beans, bitter greens (arugula, radicchio, dandelion greens)
  • Warm, cooked, Spleen-friendly meals: porridge, soups, steamed vegetables, well-cooked whole grains — the "settled" way of eating
  • Plain water and light teas: chrysanthemum or mint tea are traditional cooling choices; hydration supports fluid processing

Foods to cut back on (temporarily, as an experiment)

  • Fried, greasy, and heavy foods — the classic Dampness fuel
  • Excess sugar and refined carbs — they feed the swamp and the fire
  • Alcohol — traditionally viewed as damp-heat generating; also a common reported flare trigger
  • Dairy and very spicy food — some people with HS report these as triggers; worth a 3–4 week trial to test your own response

Skin care and friction

  • Gentle cleansing only. Mild soap, warm water, no scrubbing. Pat dry — especially in skin folds — and let the area fully air out
  • Loose, breathable clothing. Tight seams and synthetic fabrics trap heat and moisture; cotton and loose layers let the folds breathe
  • Never squeeze, pop, or lance lumps yourself. This is the single most important self-care rule — it deepens inflammation and encourages tunneling
  • Warm (not hot) compresses may be soothing for a painful nodule; stop if the area becomes more painful or spreads

Acupressure (on healthy skin only)

Acupressure points are traditionally used to support the patterns behind HS — but never press directly on, near, or over an active lesion. Press only on healthy skin, 1–2 minutes per point, gentle small circles, both sides:

  • LI-11 (Quchi) — outer end of the elbow crease; the classic point for clearing Heat from the skin
  • SP-10 (Xuehai) — above the inner knee; cools Blood, traditionally used for skin eruptions
  • ST-40 (Fenglong) — outer lower leg; the "resolve Dampness and Phlegm" point
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — below the kneecap; supports Spleen Qi and digestion
  • LV-3 (Taichong) — between the first and second toes; moves Liver Qi for stress-linked flares

Rhythm and stress

Because stress and hormonal shifts are common flare triggers, the "terrain" support matters: regular movement (a daily walk is a Liver Qi practice), consistent sleep, and a few minutes of slow, extended-exhale breathing on high-stress days. None of this is a quick fix — it is the opposite of one, and that is the point.

What Research Says

Western research on HS is young but growing, and several findings line up remarkably well with the TCM framework:

  • Diagnosis is often delayed for years. Research suggests many people with HS wait an average of around 7 years between their first symptoms and a formal diagnosis. Knowing the name of the condition — and asking a dermatologist directly about it — may shorten that delay.
  • Smoking is strongly associated with HS. Epidemiological studies consistently find higher rates of HS (and more severe disease) among smokers. In TCM terms, tobacco smoke is a classic source of Heat and toxicity — the same categories HS falls into.
  • Metabolic factors matter. HS is associated with obesity, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome. Some studies suggest that weight loss and better glycemic control may be associated with fewer or milder flares — consistent with the TCM view that diet and terrain shape the condition.
  • Dietary triggers are commonly reported. Patient surveys frequently cite dairy, high-glycemic foods, and alcohol as flare triggers, though the evidence base is preliminary and not everyone responds the same way. A personal elimination trial is a reasonable, low-risk experiment.
  • Stress is a reported trigger. Studies and patient reports note stress as a common precipitant of flares — paralleling the TCM Liver-Qi-stagnation-generates-Heat model.
  • Cooling herbs show laboratory promise. Laboratory studies of herbs traditionally used for Heat-toxin skin conditions — such as honeysuckle (jin yin hua) and berberine-containing plants (huang lian) — report antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activity. Human studies specific to HS do not yet exist, so these remain interesting laboratory findings, not recommendations.

What the research does not show is any single step that erases HS entirely — Western or TCM. What it does show is that HS is a real, chronic, inflammatory condition with identifiable contributors: smoking, metabolic health, diet, and stress. Those are exactly the levers the TCM framework reaches for.

Practical Takeaways:

  • Name it. If you suspect HS, ask a dermatologist directly — research suggests people wait years for a diagnosis that earlier recognition could shorten.
  • Think "drain the swamp," not "scrub the surface." Cooling, draining foods (cucumber, mung bean, barley, leafy greens) and cutting alcohol, fried food, and excess sugar for 3–4 weeks is the single most TCM-aligned experiment.
  • Never squeeze or pop lesions. Popping drives inflammation deeper and encourages scarring and tunneling — the one rule every framework agrees on.
  • Change the terrain, not just the lump. Regular meals, daily walks, consistent sleep, and a stress practice address the Spleen–Liver fuel behind recurrence.
  • Use acupressure on healthy skin only: LI-11, SP-10, ST-40, ST-36, and LV-3, 1–2 minutes per point — never over an active lesion.
  • Cut smoking. The association between smoking and HS severity is one of the most consistent findings in the literature; smoking-cessation support is worth raising with your doctor.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ HS is a medical condition, and some situations need prompt professional care. See a doctor (a dermatologist ideally) if you experience:

  • Signs of spreading infection: fever, chills, or redness that is spreading beyond the lump, warm to the touch, or rapidly enlarging
  • Very painful, tense lumps that interfere with movement or daily life — an abscess may need drainage by a professional
  • Tunnel-like tracts under the skin or extensive scarring — early specialist care is associated with better long-term outcomes
  • HS during pregnancy, or alongside diabetes, immunosuppression, or other chronic conditions
  • Lumps that never settle, keep multiplying, or appear in unusual places

If you notice symptoms of sepsis — high fever, rapid heartbeat, confusion, severe pain — seek emergency care immediately. TCM is a complementary lens, not a replacement for dermatological diagnosis and care.
