Age Spots and Liver Health: A TCM View of Pigmentation

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

You catch sight of the back of your hand in good light, and there they are: flat brown spots, perfectly content to stay — marks that weren't there a decade ago. Someone once called them "liver spots." You've had your liver checked; it's fine. The dermatologist calls them solar lentigines and says they're sun damage — harmless, nothing to worry about. Both statements are true. And neither tells the whole story.

TCM has been looking at spots like these for centuries — and the English name "liver spot" may be closer to the truth than anyone suspected, just not in the way you'd think. The Liver in TCM is not the anatomical organ that processes alcohol and filters blood. It's a functional system that governs the smooth flow of Qi and the storage and movement of Blood — and in TCM theory, the quality of that flow writes itself directly onto the skin.

Before we begin: age spots are extremely common, overwhelmingly benign, and most people will develop at least a few over a lifetime. Dermatology understands them as cumulative sun damage. What TCM adds is a different question: why does the same amount of sun produce prominent spots in one person and barely any in another? The answer, in TCM theory, has a lot to do with what's flowing — or not flowing — beneath the surface.

The Western View: What Dermatology Knows About Age Spots

In conventional dermatology, age spots are called solar lentigines — flat, well-defined, light-to-dark-brown patches that appear on sun-exposed skin: the backs of the hands, the forearms, the face, the shoulders, and the décolletage. They are caused by decades of cumulative ultraviolet exposure. UV light stimulates the skin's pigment-producing cells (melanocytes) to make extra melanin, and over time that pigment accumulates in the skin's basal layer — the same layer where new skin cells are born.

Because they track sun exposure rather than age itself, they can appear as early as your 30s if you've spent years outdoors without protection, and they tend to multiply and darken with each passing decade. They are benign — they are not precancerous, and they do not turn into melanoma on their own. The main clinical task for a dermatologist is distinguishing them from actual worrying lesions, which is why the "watch your spots" rules matter (we'll get to those in the When to See a Doctor section).

And the name? "Liver spots" is a historical misnomer. Centuries ago, brown patches were loosely attributed to liver complaints — a guess with no anatomical basis. Modern dermatology is unambiguous: these spots have nothing to do with the function of your liver, and the name persists only because it's stuck in the culture.

A Different Lens: The 'Liver Spot' Name, and the Liver That Isn't an Organ

Here's where TCM adds a layer the Western name accidentally points at. In TCM, the Liver (Gan) is a functional system, not the anatomical organ. Among its jobs: it stores Blood during rest and governs the smooth, even movement of Qi and Blood throughout the body — circulation, in the broadest sense of the word. When that flow is smooth, tissues are nourished, renewed, and cleared of metabolic byproducts. When it slows, things start to settle.

Think of pigment as sediment in a river. The river is your microcirculation — the fine network of vessels that feeds the skin and carries waste away. Sun exposure adds silt: melanin deposits from years of UV. In a fast, well-maintained current, sediment gets carried off, diluted, and turned over with normal skin renewal. In a slow current — circulation that has gradually lost its momentum over decades — the same silt settles. It settles in exactly the places the river runs slowest and the sun shines hardest: the backs of hands, the forearms, the face.

That's the TCM reading of age spots: not a liver disease, and not simply "old age" — but a visible record of circulation quality, written in pigment. It's why two people with identical sun exposure age so differently, and why TCM looks not just at the spot, but at the whole river it sits in: skin texture, energy, digestion, sleep, stress. Pattern 1: Liver Blood Deficiency with Blood Stasis — The Slowing River

What it feels like: spots that arrived gradually and keep multiplying, usually starting on the hands and forearms before appearing on the face. The surrounding skin often looks dull, dry, or thin. You bruise easily, your eyes feel dry or tired by evening, your nails are brittle, and you notice fatigue that sleep doesn't fully clear. Sleep may be light or broken.

Why it matters for age spots: in TCM theory, Blood nourishes the skin the way water nourishes soil — and the Liver is the system that keeps Blood moving smoothly and stores it at rest. When Liver Blood is depleted (after long periods of stress, inadequate nourishment, poor sleep, or heavy cycles in women), the skin is built on thinner material. When flow also stagnates — Blood Stasis — pigment that should be carried away settles instead, like silt dropping out of a slow current. This is the classic "liver spot" pattern: the name happens to be right about the system, just not about the organ. For the underlying concepts, see our TCM Basics guide to Blood Deficiency and Blood Stasis.

Traditionally supportive habits: foods used to build Blood — dark leafy greens, red dates, goji berries, eggs, beets, and bone broths; enough protein at every meal; gentle daily movement to keep the current moving (walking, swimming, stretching); prioritizing early, consistent sleep, when the body rebuilds Blood; and acupressure points that support Blood flow (see the daily routine below).

Pattern 2: Kidney Yin and Jing Decline — The Aging Reserve

What it feels like: spots that appear as part of a broader picture of aging — skin that has grown drier and thinner overall, hair that is thinning or graying, a lower back that tires easily, knees that feel less sturdy, occasional ringing in the ears, and a sense that you're "aging faster than your years." You may feel more sensitive to heat or cold extremes than you used to, and sleep may feel shallower.

Why it matters for age spots: in TCM theory, the Kidney stores Jing — the body's deepest reserve of vitality and the "battery" that governs the pace of aging itself. The skin is one of the first places the state of that reserve shows. As Jing naturally declines with the decades, the body's ability to renew tissue — including clearing pigment from the skin — slows down. Age spots, in this reading, are one visible marker on an odometer that's also ticking off lower-back ache, drier skin, and shorter sleep. For the broader framework, see our TCM Basics guide to the Kidney, and for the skin-specific aging picture, our guide to Aging Skin and the Kidney Clock.

Traditionally supportive habits: foods associated with nourishing Jing — black beans, black sesame, walnuts, goji berries, and bone broths; cooked root vegetables; protecting sleep as a non-negotiable (this is when the reserve is rebuilt); and avoiding the chronic overwork-and-caffeine treadmill that draws down the battery faster than the decades alone would.

Pattern 3: Spleen Qi Deficiency — The Under-Fueled Renewal

What it feels like: spots with a dull, slightly gray or muddy tone rather than a crisp brown — and skin that overall looks sallow, as if the light is missing from it. You feel tired after meals, especially heavier ones; digestion is sluggish (bloating, soft stools, heavy limbs); and your energy runs on a short fuse. Cold drinks or raw salads tend to sit heavy.

Why it matters for age spots: in TCM theory, the Spleen is the system that transforms food into Qi and Blood — the raw material for every tissue, including the skin's renewal cycle. When Spleen Qi is deficient, the factory underproduces: new skin cells arrive more slowly, and old pigmented cells linger longer than they should. The spot you have isn't being replaced — it's being kept. This pattern is worth knowing because its fix is different: it's less about pigment directly and more about re-firing the engine that renews skin in the first place. For the concept, see our TCM Basics guide to the Spleen.

Traditionally supportive habits: warm, cooked, easily digested meals — oats, millet, pumpkin, sweet potato, gentle soups; ginger in cooking; small frequent meals instead of large ones; minimizing cold drinks and raw foods; and gentle movement after meals rather than collapsing on the couch.

What May Support Even Skin Tone: A TCM-Informed Daily Approach

Whatever your dominant pattern, the daily routine below stacks the odds in your skin's favor. It's not about erasing spots overnight — it's about restoring the conditions under which the skin's renewal cycle can do its job.

1. Sun protection, without negotiation

This is the one step where Western dermatology and TCM fully agree. UV exposure is the external driver of pigment accumulation — the silt the river has to deal with. Daily broad-spectrum SPF on exposed skin, a hat in strong sun, and shade at peak hours don't just slow new spots; they also protect against the far more serious lesions that age spots are sometimes confused with. If you do nothing else, do this.

2. Move the current: acupressure for Blood flow

Acupressure is a gentle, self-administered way to support circulation — the river in our analogy. Four points, 1–2 minutes each, both sides, once or twice daily:

  • LV-3 (Taichong) — on the top of the foot, in the hollow between the big toe and second toe. Supports smooth Liver flow; the classic point for the "slowing river."
  • SP-10 (Xuehai) — "Sea of Blood," on the inner thigh, about two finger-widths above the kneecap. A traditional point for moving Blood and supporting even skin tone.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — four finger-widths below the kneecap, just outside the shinbone. Builds Qi and Blood; the Spleen pattern's workhorse.
  • KI-3 (Taixi) — in the depression between the inner ankle bone and Achilles tendon. Supports Kidney Yin and the aging reserve.

Press with comfortable firmness and make small circles. Avoid pressing on broken, bruised, or irritated skin. During pregnancy, traditional guidance is to skip SP-6 and LI-4; the points above are gentle, but check with your healthcare provider before acupressure during pregnancy. For a face-specific routine, see our TCM Facial Massage guide.

3. Eat for your pattern

  • Liver Blood + Stasis: dark leafy greens, red dates, goji berries, eggs, beets, bone broth, black sesame — plus gentle daily movement to keep flow moving.
  • Kidney Yin/Jing: black beans, walnuts, black sesame, goji, cooked root vegetables, bone broths; early nights.
  • Spleen Qi: warm cooked meals, oats, millet, pumpkin, sweet potato, ginger; small frequent meals; minimize cold drinks and raw salads.

4. Sleep — the renewal shift

Skin cell renewal peaks during deep sleep, when the body directs resources to repair rather than activity. In TCM terms, this is when Blood returns to the Liver and the reserve is rebuilt. Protecting 7–8 hours of consistent, early sleep is arguably the most powerful "topical treatment" nobody sells.

5. Watch the spots

Track your spots the way you'd track a garden: note size, shape, and color, and take a yearly photo so you can compare. Most age spots are stable for years. Any spot that changes — or any new spot that looks different from your others — deserves a dermatologist's eyes. See the section below for the specific signs.

What Research Says

Science and TCM speak different languages, but on age spots they converge in useful places:

  • Cumulative UV is the dominant modifiable driver: solar lentigines are strongly associated with lifetime sun exposure, and longitudinal studies associate consistent sunscreen use with fewer and lighter lentigines in later life. This is the Western evidence for the TCM view that the external input (the silt) matters — and that it can be managed.
  • Topical niacinamide has trial evidence: controlled studies suggest topical niacinamide (a form of vitamin B3) can reduce hyperpigmentation, including solar lentigines, over roughly 12 weeks — one of the better-studied ingredients for spot fading.
  • Vitamin C is a research-supported helper: topical ascorbic acid has evidence for lightening hyperpigmentation through inhibition of melanin synthesis — a modest but real effect, and an easy ingredient to add to a daily routine.
  • Licorice (Gan Cao) offers a traditional bridge: laboratory studies find that glabridin, a compound in licorice root — a staple of TCM herbal formulas — inhibits tyrosinase, the rate-limiting enzyme in melanin production. Human evidence is limited, but it's a striking parallel between a traditional herb and a modern mechanism.

These findings support gentle, evidence-informed skincare alongside a TCM-informed lifestyle approach — not as a substitute for dermatological care, and not as a promise of spot removal.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ Most age spots are harmless — but because they sit on sun-damaged skin, they deserve respectful monitoring. See a dermatologist promptly if any spot:

  • Is asymmetric (one half doesn't match the other)
  • Has an irregular, notched, or scalloped border
  • Contains multiple colors — brown, black, tan, red, white, or blue in one lesion
  • Is larger than 6mm (about the size of a pencil eraser)
  • Is evolving — changing in size, shape, or color over weeks or months

Also see a doctor about any new spot that appears suddenly, any spot that bleeds, itches, or becomes tender, or any spot that looks different from your others. This is the "ABCDE" rule dermatologists use — and it's the most important safety habit for anyone with sun-damaged skin. TCM is a complementary approach; it does not replace dermatological examination, especially of changing lesions.

Practical Takeaways:
  • Sun protection is non-negotiable — the one intervention both Western dermatology and TCM fully endorse; it's about new spots and far more serious lesions alike.
  • Know your pattern, eat for it — use the self-check table; Liver Blood/Stasis, Kidney Jing, and Spleen Qi each have different supportive foods and habits.
  • Move the current daily — a 10-minute walk plus 5 minutes of acupressure (LV-3, SP-10, ST-36, KI-3) supports the circulation that clears pigment.
  • Protect sleep as the renewal shift — skin repair peaks at night; consistent early sleep supports the reserve that slows the whole aging picture.
  • Watch your spots with the ABCDE rule — asymmetry, border, color, diameter, evolution; yearly photos and a dermatologist check for anything changing.
