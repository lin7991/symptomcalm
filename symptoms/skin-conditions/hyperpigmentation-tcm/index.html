Hyperpigmentation and TCM: Dark Spots, Blood Stasis, and Skin Tone

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

You step out of the shower, wipe the mirror, and there it is again: the small brown spot where a pimple used to live — months ago. You have tried the serums. The masks. The promise that it would fade "in a few weeks." It hasn't. Maybe it's the mark a scratch left on your forearm that never quite disappeared, or the way your whole face has taken on a dull, uneven tone that no cleanser seems to touch.

If this sounds familiar, you are in good company. Dark spots are among the most common skin complaints in the world — and among the most frustrating, because the usual products either underdeliver or come with a long list of caveats about irritation, rebound darkening, and endless reapplying. Traditional Chinese Medicine offers a genuinely different frame: it reads those marks not as a cosmetic accident on the surface, but as a signal from deeper in the body about how your Blood is moving, how your reserves are holding up, and how your skin's renewal cycle is running.

First, a useful distinction. "Hyperpigmentation" is an umbrella term — it covers several different kinds of dark marks, from post-acne spots to hormonal patches to sun-related freckling. The right approach depends on which kind you have. This guide maps the whole territory, then zooms in on the patterns TCM uses to make sense of them. For the hormonal facial patches known as melasma, and for age-related liver spots, we have dedicated guides linked below.

What Hyperpigmentation Actually Covers

In conventional dermatology, hyperpigmentation simply means areas of skin that are darker than the surrounding tone. The color comes from melanin — the pigment produced by skin cells called melanocytes, the same cells that tan you in the sun. When melanocytes get overstimulated in one area, they deposit extra pigment, and the result is a visible dark spot.

Under that single word, dermatology groups several distinct conditions with different triggers:

  • Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH): dark marks left behind after acne, eczema, a scratch, a burn, or a cosmetic procedure. The spot appears exactly where the inflammation was. This is the most common form and the one people usually mean when they say "my acne left marks."
  • Melasma: larger, symmetrical brown or gray-brown patches on the cheeks, forehead, upper lip, or chin, strongly linked to hormones and sun exposure.
  • Solar lentigines ("age spots"): flat, well-defined brown spots on sun-exposed areas — face, hands, shoulders — that accumulate with decades of UV exposure.
  • Freckles: small, genetic, light-sensitive spots that typically appear in childhood and darken with sun exposure.

These four share a mechanism — active melanocytes — but their triggers, timelines, and best responses differ. That's why "one cream for all dark spots" so often disappoints.

A Different Lens: The Pigment Printing Press

Here is where TCM reframes the picture. In TCM theory, the skin is not a passive wrapper — it is the visible outer surface where the state of your Qi and Blood shows through, the way a printout shows the state of a printer. Melanocytes, in this picture, are like the ink cartridges: they are supposed to deposit an even, appropriate amount of pigment. What TCM pays attention to is the press itself — the flow of Blood that runs the machine and clears the finished pages.

Two things can go wrong, and TCM distinguishes them sharply:

  • The ink isn't clearing (Blood Stasis). When microcirculation slows — the current of Blood that normally carries away old pigment and dead skin cells — marks dry in place and linger for months or years. For a deeper look at this state, see our TCM Basics guide to Blood Stasis.
  • The press is under-supplied (Blood Deficiency). Skin renewal depends on Blood nourishment the way a print job depends on supply. When Blood is thin or depleted, the whole cycle slows — new cells arrive late, old pigment sits longer, and the complexion goes dull and patchy. Our TCM Basics article on Blood Deficiency explains this state in detail.

That's why the most useful TCM question is not "what can I bleach this spot with?" but "why is the ink not clearing?" — a question that leads to the Liver, the Spleen, and your overall reserves, not just the skin itself.

First: What Kind of Dark Spot Is Yours?

Before diving into patterns, a quick triage — because the type of mark changes everything:

  • Appeared after a pimple, scratch, rash, or procedure → likely PIH. It sits exactly where the inflammation was, and it fades slowly. This guide focuses most closely on this type.
  • Symmetrical patches on cheeks, forehead, or upper lip → likely melasma. Deepened by hormones, sun, and heat. For the full hormonal story, see our guide to Melasma and Skin Pigmentation.
  • Flat brown spots on sun-exposed skin, appearing in your 40s and beyond → likely age spots. These are the record of cumulative sun exposure. Our guide to Age Spots and Liver Health covers them in depth.
  • Small, present since childhood, darken in summer → freckles. Genetic and harmless in themselves; they simply respond to sun avoidance.
  • A spot that is changing, irregular, or unusual-looking — see a dermatologist before any self-care. Details in the When to See a Doctor section below.

Pattern 1: Blood Stasis — The Clogged Conveyor Belt

What it feels like: marks that arrived after some form of skin trauma — an acne breakout, a scratched mosquito bite, a burn, even a facial treatment — and then simply stayed. The spots are usually well-defined and darker than the skin around them. You might also notice you bruise easily, run cold hands and feet, or carry tension in your shoulders and jaw.

Why it matters for dark spots: in TCM theory, Blood should move continuously, delivering nourishment and clearing waste. When the flow slows — from cold, from long periods of sitting, from emotional tension that locks the body into a held pattern — the microcirculation that normally clears pigment stalls. The mark is not new pigment being printed; it's old pigment stuck on the belt. This is the most commonly described TCM pattern behind stubborn, long-lasting PIH.

Traditionally supportive habits: daily movement — brisk walks, qigong, yoga — that keeps Blood circulating; warming, aromatic foods like ginger, turmeric, and black pepper; and acupressure on LV-3 (in the web between the big toe and second toe), a classic point for getting things moving again.

Pattern 2: Blood Deficiency — The Under-Supplied Renewal Crew

What it feels like: spots that fade painfully slowly — or not at all — alongside skin that generally looks tired: dry, dull, and missing the even flush it once had. Often paired with fatigue, brittle nails, dry eyes, a scanty cycle (if you menstruate), and sleep that doesn't feel restorative.

Why it matters for dark spots: in TCM, Blood nourishes the skin the way water nourishes soil — and skin renewal is a Blood-dependent process. When Blood is depleted — after long periods of stress, heavy cycles, poor sleep, or simply eating less than the body needs — the renewal crew is understaffed. New skin cells arrive late, old pigmented cells linger past their retirement date, and the whole surface goes dull and uneven. For more on this state, see our TCM Basics guide to Blood Deficiency.

Traditionally supportive habits: Blood-nourishing foods — cooked red dates, goji berries, dark leafy greens, beets, eggs, and bone broth; consistent, early sleep (Blood is restored most deeply at night); and gentle, restorative movement rather than exhausting workouts. Acupressure on ST-36 (just below the kneecap) and SP-6 (inner calf, above the ankle) are classic points for building Blood — skip SP-6 if you are pregnant.

Pattern 3: Liver Qi Stagnation with Heat — The Overheated Press

What it feels like: darkening that shows up or worsens in stressful seasons of life — and sits on skin that is also reactive: redness, breakouts, or eczema flares alongside the marks. You might notice irritability, sighing, jaw clenching, or PMS-type tension. The spots themselves may have a slightly reddish or inflamed edge rather than a flat, cold brown.

Why it matters for dark spots: in TCM theory, the Liver governs the smooth flow of Qi and emotion. Chronic stress creates stagnation — and stagnation, left long enough, generates Heat. Heat is inflammation in the language of an older era, and inflammation is exactly what stimulates melanocytes: the press runs hot and prints extra ink. This pattern maps neatly onto PIH, where the original inflammatory event (the pimple, the rash) is the spark, and ongoing stress keeps the fire smoldering.

Traditionally supportive habits: teas traditionally used to cool and move — mint, chrysanthemum, rose bud; cooling foods like cucumber, celery, and mung bean; and daily movement that releases held tension. Acupressure on LV-3, plus slow, extended-exhale breathing, are classic supports for settling the system.

Pattern 4: Spleen Qi Deficiency — The Under-Fueled Factory

What it feels like: not sharp, well-defined spots, but a diffuse, patchy, slightly yellowish or muddy unevenness across the face — the complexion that looks "off" even when the lighting is good. Often paired with fatigue after meals, bloating, loose stools, brain fog, and a tendency to feel heavy or sluggish.

Why it matters for dark spots: the Spleen, in TCM, transforms food into Qi and Blood — it is the factory that produces the raw materials the skin needs for renewal. When Spleen Qi is weak, production drops: Blood runs thinner, fluids move sluggishly, and the complexion loses its even, watered quality. The pigment here is less a stain than a symptom of an under-fueled system. Our TCM Basics article on the Spleen covers this system in depth.

Traditionally supportive habits: warm, cooked, easily digested meals at regular times; foods like congee, pumpkin, sweet potato, and oats; and a break from raw salads, cold drinks, and excess sugar — the classic advice for a tired Spleen. ST-36 is the go-to point for this pattern.

Sun, Heat, and the External Fire: The One Factor That Touches Every Pattern

Here is the honest baseline: sun protection is not optional. Broad-spectrum SPF 30+ worn daily is the single most evidence-supported step for every form of hyperpigmentation — PIH, melasma, and age spots alike. UV exposure directly stimulates melanocytes, deepens existing marks, and slows their fading. TCM does not replace this; it works around it. If a TCM-informed routine sounds appealing but you skip the sunscreen, you are pouring water into a leaking bucket.

In TCM terms, UV is best understood as external fire — a Heat from outside that lands on the skin's surface and drives the pigment response. This explains why marks darken in summer, worsen on sunny holidays, and fade (slowly) in winter. It also explains the classic TCM advice for the season of sun: eat cooling foods — cucumber, watermelon, mung bean, green tea, mint — and avoid anything that leaves the skin irritated and inflamed, because irritation is a fresh stamp on the press.

This is also why aggressive bleaching products can backfire from a TCM perspective: if a product irritates the skin, it creates inflammation — and inflammation is precisely the signal that prints more ink. Gentle, consistent care beats harsh, intermittent assault.

What May Support Even Skin Tone: A TCM-Informed Daily Approach

Putting the patterns to work, a daily approach has three lanes — food, movement, and skin-side care:

Food by pattern

  • To move Blood (stasis pattern): turmeric, ginger, black pepper, citrus peel, berries, and dark leafy greens. Think of these as helping the conveyor belt run again.
  • To build Blood (deficiency pattern): cooked red dates, goji berries, beets, eggs, dark leafy greens, black beans, and bone broth — eaten warm, not raw.
  • To cool Heat (overheated pattern): cucumber, celery, mung bean, green tea, mint, and chrysanthemum tea.
  • To fuel the Spleen: regular warm meals built on congee, oats, pumpkin, sweet potato, and millet — and less raw food, cold drinks, and sugar.

Acupressure (1–2 minutes per point, both sides, most days)

  • ST-36 (below the kneecap, outer edge of the shin): the digestion and Blood-building workhorse.
  • SP-6 (inner calf, above the ankle): nourishes Blood and fluids — skip in pregnancy.
  • LV-3 (between the big toe and second toe): moves the Qi that stagnation and stress lock up.
  • LI-11 (outer end of the elbow crease): traditionally used for skin Heat and reactivity.

Skin-side rules

  • Stop creating new stamps. Picking a pimple, scratching a bite, or scrubbing hard is trauma — and each trauma can print a fresh mark that lasts months. This is the single most underrated step.
  • Keep products gentle. If something stings or reddens your skin, it is adding Heat. Moisturize; skip the burn.
  • Protect daily. SPF 30+ every morning, reapply if you're outdoors, and a hat when the sun is strong.
  • Sleep. Blood and Yin are restored most deeply at night; chronic sleep debt slows the whole renewal cycle.
  • Be patient. Skin cells turn over on a cycle of weeks, and pigment sits deep. Expect months, not days — the goal is a calmer, more even baseline, not an overnight eraser.

What Research Says

Science and TCM speak different languages, but on dark spots they converge in useful places:

  • PIH is common and stubborn — especially in deeper skin tones: dermatology reviews note that post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is one of the most frequent reasons people with skin of color seek care, and that marks can persist for months to years. This matches the TCM observation that once pigment is "stuck," clearing it takes time and requires addressing the underlying flow.
  • Inflammation is the biological trigger: when skin is inflamed — by acne, eczema, or injury — it releases signaling molecules (including prostaglandins and endothelin-1) that directly stimulate melanocytes to produce pigment. This is the molecular version of the TCM "Heat leaves a stamp" logic: the inflammatory event itself is what prints the mark.
  • Control the inflammation early: because PIH follows inflammation, the best-evidenced way to limit it is to manage the underlying condition (acne control, eczema care) and avoid picking and scratching. In TCM terms: stop creating new stamps, and cool the fire before it prints.
  • Turmeric offers a traditional-modern bridge: laboratory and some small human studies suggest curcumin — the active compound in turmeric, a staple of traditional Asian medicine — has anti-inflammatory activity and may modulate pigment pathways. Human evidence for spot-fading specifically is still early.
  • TCM research is emerging: small studies of pattern-based herbal formulas and acupuncture for facial pigmentation report preliminary promise, though the evidence base is too limited for firm conclusions. The honest summary: an intriguing framework with early-stage research.

None of this replaces dermatological care — especially the professional evaluation of any unusual or changing spot.

When to See a Doctor

Most dark spots are harmless — but some require immediate professional attention. See a dermatologist if you notice any spot that:
  • Is new, changing, or looks different from your other spots — remember the ABCDEs: Asymmetry, irregular Border, multiple Colors, large Diameter, or Evolving.
  • Bleeds, itches, or becomes tender without a known cause.
  • Has appeared alongside generalized skin darkening with fatigue, weight loss, or dizziness — this can signal a systemic condition that needs medical evaluation.
  • Shows velvety, darker patches in skin folds (neck, armpits, groin) — worth checking for metabolic concerns.
  • Appeared after starting a new medication.
TCM is a complementary approach and does not replace dermatological evaluation, especially for anything that changes over time.

Practical Takeaways

Your five-step map to calmer, more even skin tone:
  1. Name your type first. Post-acne marks, hormonal patches, and age spots need different care — use the triage list above before buying anything.
  2. Sun protection daily. SPF 30+ is non-negotiable for every type of dark spot.
  3. Find your pattern. Lingering marks → Blood Stasis; slow fading with fatigue → Blood Deficiency; stress-linked with redness → Liver Qi Stagnation with Heat; diffuse dullness with digestion issues → Spleen Qi Deficiency. Eat for your pattern.
  4. Stop creating new stamps. No picking, no scratching, no stinging products — each irritation is a fresh mark waiting to happen.
  5. Move, press, sleep, and wait. Daily movement, four acupressure points, early nights, and genuine patience — the renewal cycle runs in months, not days.
