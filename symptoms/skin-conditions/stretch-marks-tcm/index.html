Stretch Marks and TCM: Skin Resilience Through the Lens of Qi and Blood

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

You notice them in the mirror one day — pale, silvery lines running across your belly, hips, thighs, or chest. Maybe they arrived with pregnancy. Maybe with a growth spurt, a body recomposition, or a season of weight change. They don't hurt. They don't itch. They just stay — a permanent-looking record of a time your skin was asked to stretch faster than it could handle.

If you've ever wondered why some people go through the same changes without a single line, you've stumbled onto the exact question Traditional Chinese Medicine has spent centuries exploring. Stretch marks aren't random. In the TCM framework, they are a visible report on your skin's resilience — and resilience, in this tradition, is built from the inside.

Stretch marks are incredibly common and completely harmless. Studies suggest the majority of pregnant women develop them, and they also appear during adolescent growth spurts, rapid weight or muscle changes, and with certain hormonal shifts. Dermatology understands them as tears in the skin's deeper layers. What TCM adds is a different question: why does the same stretch tear one person's skin and not another's?

The Western View: What Stretch Marks Actually Are

In conventional dermatology, stretch marks (striae distensae) are a form of scarring in the dermis — the skin's middle layer. When skin is stretched faster than it can rebuild, the collagen and elastin fibers that give it structure and spring get pulled past their limit and tear. The mark you see at the surface is the scar tissue of that deeper tearing.

A few things dermatology consistently finds:

  • They have a strong genetic component. If your mother had stretch marks in pregnancy, your risk is noticeably higher — the architecture of your skin, including how much elastin it produces, is partly inherited.
  • They're tied to hormones. Pregnancy, puberty, and corticosteroid exposure all change how skin behaves. Corticosteroids, in particular, can suppress the production of collagen — which is why people on long-term steroid therapy, or with conditions that raise cortisol, often develop marks more easily.
  • They evolve in color. Fresh marks are pink, red, or purple (striae rubra) because blood vessels show through the thinned dermis. Over months to years they fade to white or silver (striae alba) as the blood supply withdraws — which is why old marks are so hard to shift.
  • Conventional options are limited. Topical retinoids, hyaluronic acid, laser therapy, and microneedling have shown some evidence for improving the appearance of established marks — but nothing reliably erases them, and most prevention-focused trials have been disappointing.

A Different Lens: Skin as Fabric Stretched Past Its Weave

Here's where TCM offers a genuinely different picture. Imagine skin as a piece of fabric. Two things determine whether it survives being stretched: the quality of the material, and the speed of the stretch. You can yank cheap, thin fabric and it tears. You can also yank high-quality fabric so fast that even it gives way. But give good fabric a slow, supported stretch and it holds — even under considerable load.

In TCM theory, the "quality of the material" is largely a story about Qi and Blood. Blood is what nourishes the skin, the sinews, and the connective tissue — it is the raw material the body uses to keep skin supple, moist, and elastic. Qi is the force that moves that Blood, delivers it where it's needed, and drives the constant rebuilding that keeps skin resilient. For a deeper look at how TCM understands this system, see our TCM Basics guides to Blood in TCM and the relationship between Qi and Blood.

From this angle, a stretch mark is what happens when the demand for skin resilience outstrips the supply. Two people can go through the same pregnancy, the same growth spurt, the same weight shift — but if one has deeper Blood reserves and smoother Qi flow, their skin has more give. The other person's skin tears, and the mark is the record. That's not a judgment — it's a map. It tells you which internal systems may have been running low.

Pattern 1: Blood Deficiency — The Under-Funded Supply Line

What it feels like: marks that appeared alongside skin that generally looks and feels under-nourished — dry, dull, thin, slow to bounce back. Often paired with a pale complexion, fatigue, brittle nails, a scanty or irregular cycle, and poor sleep. You might notice that when you gain or lose weight, your skin seems to "give" quickly.

Why it matters for stretch marks: in TCM, Blood nourishes the skin the way water nourishes soil — it keeps the tissue moist, pliant, and elastic. When Blood is depleted — after long periods of stress, heavy cycles, inadequate nourishment, or poor sleep — the skin is built on thinner material. It may look fine at rest and fail exactly when it's asked to stretch. For more on this state, see our TCM Basics guide to Blood Deficiency.

Traditionally supportive habits: foods traditionally used to build Blood — dark leafy greens, red dates, goji berries, eggs, beets, and bone broths; enough protein at every meal; prioritizing early, consistent sleep (the body rebuilds Blood during rest); and gentle acupressure on SP-6 (above the inner ankle) and ST-36 (below the kneecap) — points traditionally used to support Blood and digestion.

Pattern 2: Liver Blood and the Connective Web — The Tightening Threads

What it feels like: marks that appeared during a demanding or stressful season — a hard pregnancy, an intense training block, a period of chronic tension. Often accompanied by tight shoulders and jaw, dry eyes, irritability, sighing, and a feeling of being "wound up." Your skin may feel tight rather than dry.

Why it matters for stretch marks: in TCM, the Liver governs the sinews — the connective web of tendons, ligaments, and fascia that runs through the whole body — and Liver Blood is what keeps that web supple. When Liver Qi stagnates under chronic stress, it tightens the web; when Liver Blood runs low, the web loses its moisture and spring. The result is a connective system with less give, more likely to tear under stretch. This is also the pattern behind the classic link between stress and skin that "doesn't recover like it used to." For the broader picture, see our TCM Basics article on the Liver.

Traditionally supportive habits: daily movement that releases tension (brisk walks, qigong, yoga — anything that gets the breath and body moving), breathwork, and gentle acupressure on LV-3 (in the web between the big toe and second toe). Warm, moistening foods like soups and stews support both Liver Blood and the connective tissues.

Pattern 3: Blood Stasis — The Mark That Stays Purple

What it feels like: marks that stay pink, red, or purple for months — sometimes well over a year — instead of fading toward silver. They may be slightly raised or feel firm, sit in a fixed spot, and occasionally itch. You might also notice cold hands or feet, or a tendency to bruise easily.

Why it matters for stretch marks: in TCM, the fresh red-purple color of a new mark reflects Blood that is congested and slow — Stagnant Blood pooled at the site of the tear. Where flow is good, the body gradually clears the congestion and the mark fades to a pale, quiet line. Where flow is sluggish, the mark lingers in its purple phase. In this framework, the color of your marks is a live readout of your circulation. For more, see our TCM Basics guide to Blood Stasis.

Traditionally supportive habits: regular movement — the single most direct way to support circulation; dry brushing, gentle massage, and oil application over the marks themselves; warm baths; and foods traditionally used to move Blood like dark berries, turmeric, and ginger. Acupressure on SP-10 (inner side, just above the kneecap — the "Sea of Blood") and BL-17 (between the shoulder blades, the influential point of Blood) is a classic pairing for supporting circulation.

Pattern 4: Kidney Jing and the High-Demand Window — Growing Faster Than Reserves Allow

What it feels like: marks that appeared during a major growth or transformation window — pregnancy, an adolescent growth spurt, a rapid weight or muscle gain, or a period of intense physical demand. Often accompanied by lower-back ache, fatigue, hair changes, or a sense of feeling "drained" — as if the body is spending faster than it's earning.

Why it matters for stretch marks: in TCM, the Kidneys store Jing — the body's deepest structural reserve, the resource that fuels growth, reproduction, and repair. Windows of rapid change draw directly on that reserve: the body builds new tissue at speed, and if the reserve is shallow, the quality of everything built — including skin — reflects it. This is why stretch marks cluster in exactly the moments when the body is growing or transforming fastest. For more on this concept, see our TCM Basics guide to Jing (Essence).

Traditionally supportive habits: nourishing, slow-cooked foods — bone broths, black beans, walnuts, sesame, root vegetables; steady protein rather than crash dieting (rapid weight loss can stress the reserve as much as rapid gain); consistent, early sleep; and gentleness with yourself during high-demand windows. The Kidney point KI-3 (in the hollow behind the inner ankle) is traditionally used to support reserves.

Which Pattern Sounds Like You? A Quick Self-Check

Most people are a blend — the patterns feed each other. Use this quick scan to find where your emphasis sits:

  • Dry, dull skin + fatigue + brittle nails + pale complexion → Blood deficiency emphasis. Prioritize nourishing food, early nights, and SP-6/ST-36.
  • Marks during a stressful season + tension + dry eyes + irritability → Liver Blood / Qi stagnation emphasis. Prioritize movement, breathwork, and LV-3.
  • Marks that stay purple for months + cold extremities + easy bruising → Blood stasis emphasis. Prioritize circulation, massage, and SP-10/BL-17.
  • Marks tied to pregnancy, growth spurts, or rapid change + lower-back ache + deep fatigue → Kidney Jing emphasis. Prioritize broths, warm foods, early sleep, and KI-3.

What May Support Skin Resilience: A TCM-Informed Daily Approach

None of this promises to erase marks that have already formed — nothing reliably does. What a TCM-informed approach offers is a way to support your skin's resilience from the inside out, so the next stretch window is met with more give.

1. Nourish the material

If Blood is the fabric of resilient skin, then the kitchen is the loom. Warm, cooked, whole foods; enough protein and dark leafy greens; bone broths and soups; and the Blood-building foods in Pattern 1. For the full food framework, see our guide to TCM dietary principles for skin health.

2. Keep the flow moving

Qi moves Blood — and movement moves Qi. Daily walks, stretching, qigong, or yoga keep circulation flowing to the skin. During a stretch window (pregnancy, growth, weight change), gentle consistent movement is more supportive than sporadic intensity. Regular massage or dry brushing over existing marks supports the local circulation that helps them fade on their natural timeline.

3. Moisture and topical care

Well-moisturized skin has more mechanical give, and massage itself supports circulation — two reasons gentle daily oil massage is a staple of traditional skin care. Simple, well-tolerated oils (jojoba, sweet almond, shea-based balms) are the classic choice. Evidence for any single topical preventing stretch marks is limited, but the ritual — daily touch, moisture, and circulation — is harmless and pleasant, and it maps neatly onto the TCM emphasis on supporting the skin from its surface outward.

4. Acupressure: a 3-minute daily sequence

Acupressure is the self-care version of acupuncture — firm, comfortable pressure held for 30 to 60 seconds per point. A simple sequence for skin resilience support:

Point Location Traditional use
ST-36 (Zusanli) Four finger-widths below the kneecap, outer side Digestion and Blood production — the foundation
SP-6 (Sanyinjiao) Four finger-widths above the inner ankle Blood and Yin nourishment
SP-10 (Xuehai) Inner side, just above the kneecap "Sea of Blood" — circulation to the skin
LV-3 (Taichong) Web between big toe and second toe Liver Qi flow and Liver Blood support
KI-3 (Taixi) Hollow behind the inner ankle bone Kidney reserves and deep nourishment
If you are pregnant: stretch marks most often appear in pregnancy — and two of these points, SP-6 and LI-4, are traditionally considered off-limits during pregnancy, with many practitioners advising against acupressure below the waist entirely. If pregnant, skip the acupressure points and lean on nourishment, gentle movement, topical oils, and your healthcare provider's guidance.

5. Sleep and stress — the quiet half of the picture

In TCM, the Liver stores Blood during rest, and the deep repair work of skin happens while you sleep. A late, restless night is like leaving the supply line running thin. Protecting sleep — especially during high-demand windows like pregnancy or growth — is, in this framework, skin care. Stress management matters for the same reason: chronic tension tightens the connective web (Pattern 2) and drains the reserves (Pattern 4).

Research: What Modern Science Says

Science and TCM use different languages, but on stretch marks they keep arriving at the same neighborhoods:

  • The genetic case is strong. Family history is one of the most consistently reported risk factors for stretch marks — some studies suggest heritability plays a major role. This aligns neatly with the TCM view that skin resilience is partly a matter of your constitutional reserve, not just what you do in the moment.
  • Hormones change skin behavior. Corticosteroid exposure suppresses collagen production and is a well-documented trigger for stretch marks — the same hormonal axis TCM maps to the Kidney system's role in growth and transformation.
  • Prevention trials have been humbling. Randomized studies of cocoa butter and other popular oils have found little evidence they stop marks from forming — while massage itself, regardless of product, remains a low-cost way to support circulation and skin pliability.
  • Topical actives show modest promise for established marks. Hyaluronic acid and retinoid-based products have some evidence for improving the appearance of marks that have already formed — though retinoids are not recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding, so the TCM emphasis on internal nourishment becomes the safer complementary route.
  • Emerging therapies target the deeper layer. Microneedling and certain laser therapies aim to stimulate collagen remodeling in the dermis — conceptually not far from the TCM goal of supporting the skin's own rebuilding capacity. Evidence is early and results vary.

When to See a Doctor

Stretch marks themselves are harmless — but they can occasionally signal something worth checking. See a doctor if you notice:
  • Many marks appearing rapidly along with thinning skin, easy bruising, weight gain, muscle weakness, or a rounded face — a combination that can suggest elevated cortisol levels and deserves evaluation.
  • Marks that become painful, itchy, swollen, or break down into open sores.
  • Marks appearing in unusual places — like the back, arms, or armpits — especially without an obvious cause such as pregnancy, growth, or weight change.
  • If you are pregnant: talk to your obstetrician before starting any new supplement, tea, or topical routine — including retinoid products, which are not recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding.

If the appearance of marks is causing you real distress, a dermatologist can discuss the evidence for cosmetic options honestly. Nothing here replaces professional evaluation — this framework is a complementary lens, not a substitute.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  • Stretch marks are a resilience story, not a failure. They form when skin is asked to stretch faster than it can rebuild — and TCM reads that as a signal about internal nourishment, not a character flaw.
  • Nourish the material. Warm cooked meals, protein, dark leafy greens, bone broths, and early sleep build the Blood that keeps skin supple and elastic.
  • Read your marks. Dry thin skin points to Blood deficiency; stress-linked marks to Liver Blood and Qi stagnation; purple marks that linger to Blood stasis; marks during growth windows to Kidney Jing — and each has different supportive habits.
  • Keep the flow moving. Daily movement, gentle massage, and dry brushing support the circulation that helps marks fade on their natural timeline.
  • During pregnancy, keep it simple. Skip acupressure (especially SP-6 and LI-4), avoid retinoids, and lean on food, gentle movement, sleep, and topical oils — with your obstetrician's input.
  • Get sudden or extensive marks checked. Rapid onset with thinning skin, bruising, or weakness can signal elevated cortisol and deserves professional evaluation.
