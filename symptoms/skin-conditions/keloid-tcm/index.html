Keloid Scars and TCM: Blood Stasis and Scar Tissue

Symptom Guide · 6 min read

Your surgery healed months ago. The wound closed, the stitches came out — but something kept growing. A raised, firm ridge of scar tissue that now sits above the original wound, sometimes bigger than it. It may itch. It may ache. And it has a stubborn opinion about going away.

Keloids are one of the most frustrating things the skin does: instead of repairing a wound and stopping, the repair process keeps going — and going — laying down extra tissue long after the job is done. Western dermatology describes this clearly but often treats it as a cosmetic nuisance. TCM asks a different question: why is the repair crew still pouring concrete?

The Western View: Why Some Scars Overbuild

In Western medicine, a keloid is a benign overgrowth of scar tissue that extends beyond the original wound boundary. Unlike a normal scar — or a hypertrophic scar, which stays within the wound's edges — a keloid keeps growing outward, sometimes for years.

A few facts worth knowing:

  • It's a collagen problem: the cells that produce collagen (fibroblasts) keep producing it at high levels, and the enzymes that normally break excess collagen down don't keep pace. The result is a dense, raised, firm mass.
  • Genetics play a large role: keloids run in families and are significantly more common in people with darker skin tones. The same injury can produce a flat scar in one person and a keloid in their relative.
  • Certain body sites are prone: the chest, shoulders, earlobes, and upper back are classic keloid zones — areas where skin is under constant tension or where cartilage sits close to the surface.
  • They can return after removal: surgical excision alone has a well-documented recurrence rate, which is why dermatologists often combine it with pressure, silicone, steroid injections, or other approaches.

A keloid isn't dangerous in itself — it won't become cancer, and it isn't contagious. But it can itch, ache, restrict movement over a joint, and feel like a permanent, unwanted souvenir of an injury you'd rather forget.

A Different Lens: The Construction Crew That Won't Stop Pouring

Here's the analogy that makes keloids click: imagine a road crew fixing a pothole. A normal scar is a crew that fills the hole, smooths the asphalt, packs up the cones, and leaves. A keloid is a crew that keeps pouring concrete — widening the patch past the pothole, across the lane, ignoring the "job complete" sign.

In TCM, that runaway construction is understood as Blood Stasis — not "stale blood" in the literal sense, but a pattern where Blood (the body's nourishing, moving, repair-delivering substance) has settled into a sluggish, congested state at a specific site. The tissue around the scar isn't remodeling; it's stuck in a holding pattern, and the local environment keeps signaling "keep building."

Two scars, two problems. If your scar's problem is that it won't fade — flat marks, dark spots, pitted texture — see our guide to acne scars from a TCM perspective. That's the under-supplied repair crew. This article is about the opposite failure: the repair crew that won't stop.

Within the keloid picture, TCM distinguishes three common patterns — and which one applies to you changes what's traditionally considered supportive.

Pattern 1: Blood Stasis — The Hardened Slab

What it feels like: a keloid that is firm and fixed, darker than the surrounding skin (purple-red or brown), and stable — it isn't growing much anymore, but it also isn't going anywhere. It may ache in one spot or itch intermittently. Cold weather or prolonged pressure can make it feel tighter or more tender.

The image: concrete that has set — hard, immobile, dense. The repair crew finished, but the material they left is a slab, not a smooth patch. The blood at the site has congealed into a traffic jam that won't clear on its own.

What is traditionally considered supportive:

  • Movement and circulation: regular gentle exercise helps the body's Blood move systemically — the opposite of the stuck, static state of stasis.
  • Circulation-friendly foods: dark leafy greens, beetroot, berries, and warm cooked meals are traditionally used to support healthy Blood movement. Cold, raw, and iced foods are often minimized in this pattern.
  • Acupressure: points that move Blood — SP-10 (Xuehai, "Sea of Blood," above the inner knee) and LV-3 (Taichong, on the top of the foot) — pressed in small circles for 1–2 minutes each may support circulation. BL-17 (Geshu, between the shoulder blades) is known in TCM as the "meeting point of Blood."

Pattern 2: Blood Stasis with Heat — The Overactive Pour

What it feels like: a keloid that is actively growing — red, warm to the touch, itchy or even burning, and expanding beyond the original wound edges. This is the "active phase" of a keloid, often the first 6–18 months after injury. It may flare up with heat, alcohol, spicy food, or stress.

The image: concrete that's still curing and generating heat — the crew is pouring faster than the site can absorb. The Heat is the visible signature of that overdrive.

What is traditionally considered supportive:

  • Cooling the local environment: keeping the scar out of direct sun, avoiding tight friction, and using cooling foods — cucumber, mint, mung bean, watermelon — are traditional choices for Heat patterns.
  • Reducing flare triggers: alcohol and spicy food are classically viewed as fanning Heat; many people notice their keloid itches more after both.
  • Acupressure: LI-11 (Quchi, at the outer elbow crease) is the classic point for clearing Heat, paired with SP-10 and LV-3 for the stasis underneath.

Pattern 3: Blood Deficiency with Stasis — The Weak Cement

What it feels like: a keloid that is pale or pink rather than red — the scar forms, but the tissue around it looks thin, fragile, or slow to mature. You may notice generally poor wound healing, fatigue, dizziness, or pale lips and nails. The keloid is present, but the raw materials for healthy tissue are running low.

The image: a crew pouring concrete mixed with too little cement — it sets, but badly. The scar exists because the build happened; it looks weak because the body's Blood (the raw material of repair) is undersupplied.

What is traditionally considered supportive:

  • Nourishing foods: cooked red dates, goji berries, dark leafy greens, eggs, and bone broth are traditional Blood-nourishing choices. Warm, cooked meals over raw salads help the Spleen turn food into usable Qi and Blood.
  • Rest as medicine: this pattern is fueled by chronic depletion — adequate sleep is not optional support, it's the main support.
  • Acupressure: ST-36 (Zusanli, below the knee) is the workhorse point for building Qi and Blood, paired with SP-10 and BL-17.

Daily Habits That May Support Scar Comfort

These habits are complementary — they support comfort and resilience alongside whatever your dermatologist recommends (pressure therapy, silicone sheets, steroid injections, or other options).

  • Gentle scar massage: once the wound is fully closed (check with your clinician first), some people find that gentle circular massage around — not on — the scar with a plain moisturizer supports tissue flexibility. Never massage an open, infected, or actively painful site.
  • Sun protection: keloids can darken with UV exposure. Covering the scar or using sunscreen helps keep the tissue from becoming more pigmented.
  • Reduce friction: tight collars, bra straps, or jewelry rubbing a chest or earlobe keloid can keep the area irritated — a mechanical version of "keeping the site inflamed."
  • Manage stress: in TCM, chronic stress stagnates Liver Qi, which in turn worsens Blood Stasis. Whatever helps you unwind — walking, breathing practice, time in nature — is also scar support.
  • Daily acupressure reset: 1–2 minutes each of SP-10, LV-3, and ST-36 (or LI-11 if your scar feels hot) is a practical five-minute routine you can do while watching TV.

What Research Says

The scientific picture on keloids is still developing, but a few threads are worth knowing:

  • Pressure and silicone are the mainstream mainstays: pressure garments and silicone sheets/gels have been studied for decades; evidence is mixed but they remain the most common first-line adjuncts, particularly after surgery. TCM views these as reasonable "mechanical" support — they reduce tension and occlusion on a site that is prone to over-building.
  • Scar massage has some support: small studies suggest massage may improve scar pliability and reduce discomfort, though protocols vary widely. This aligns with the TCM emphasis on keeping the local tissue from "setting" like concrete.
  • Laboratory work on TCM herbs is promising but early: compounds from Salvia miltiorrhiza (dan shen), a herb traditionally used to invigorate Blood, have been studied in keloid fibroblast models for their effects on collagen production. This is laboratory research — not a clinical claim — but it mirrors the TCM logic that moving Blood matters at the tissue level.
  • Acupuncture around scars: preliminary and small studies have explored needling around scar tissue to support local circulation and sensation; the evidence base is limited and designs vary.

None of this replaces your dermatologist's care plan — it's context for why TCM looks at keloids the way it does.

When to See a Doctor

See a dermatologist or your regular clinician if you notice any of the following:

  • A scar that keeps growing beyond the original wound, especially rapidly
  • Pain, bleeding, ulceration, or discharge from the scar
  • A scar over a joint that restricts movement
  • New keloids forming after minor injuries or procedures
  • Any scar that changes color, texture, or sensation suddenly

Dermatologists have a range of options — steroid injections, cryotherapy, surgical removal combined with pressure or radiation therapy, laser, and others — often used in combination because keloids are famously persistent. A licensed TCM practitioner can complement that care with a pattern-based approach; the two systems can work alongside each other, with your doctor's knowledge.

Practical Takeaways

What you can do starting this week:

  • Read your scar's signals: red + growing + burning = Heat phase; dark + fixed = Stasis slab; pale + tired + slow = Deficiency. Your self-care follows the pattern.
  • Move your Blood daily: walking, stretching, and the 5-minute acupressure reset (SP-10, LV-3, ST-36) keep the site from "setting."
  • Keep the site cool and protected: sunscreen, loose clothing, no friction — for the Heat pattern especially.
  • Feed the repair crew properly: warm cooked meals, dark leafy greens, and Blood-nourishing foods if you lean toward the Deficiency pattern.
  • Stay in your dermatologist's loop: TCM supports the journey; your medical team guides it.
