When Meditation Feels Wrong: A TCM View on Meditation Side Effects

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

The Meditation That Made Things Worse

You did everything right. The app, the timer, the cushion, the twenty minutes every morning. Everyone said meditation would calm you down — instead, something strange happened. The more you sat, the more your mind raced. Your heart pounded. Old frustrations surfaced with a force that surprised you. Or you felt weirdly detached, like you were watching yourself from across the room, floating somewhere above your own body.

Maybe you stopped meditating and felt guilty about it. Or you pushed through, because everyone says the discomfort is "part of the process." But here is the thing nobody tells you: meditation genuinely does not feel good for everyone — and research increasingly agrees. Studies have documented meditation-related experiences ranging from temporary anxiety to panic, dissociation, and the resurfacing of difficult emotions.

If this is you, you are not broken, and you are not "bad at meditating." From a TCM perspective, the problem is not the practice itself — it is the mismatch between the practice and what your body is doing underneath. This article looks at why meditation can trigger anxiety, panic, or dissociation — and how to practice in a way that works with your patterns instead of against them.

Why a "Calming" Practice Can Feel Anything But

In TCM, meditation is not a neutral activity. It is a practice that directs Qi — the body's energy and information flow — and asks the Shen to settle. The Shen is the aspect of mind the Heart houses: the quality of awareness that keeps you present, clear, and grounded. For a broader overview of what the Shen is and why it matters, see our TCM Basics guide to the Shen (Spirit) in TCM.

The key insight: meditation asks the Shen to come home and sit down. If the home is in disorder, sitting down is not relaxing — it is confronting.

Think of it this way. During a normal day, your attention is constantly occupied: work, screens, conversations, traffic, meals. That constant activity acts like background noise that keeps certain internal signals at a distance. When you sit in stillness, you remove the noise — and whatever was moving underneath becomes audible. Meditation does not create that distress. It reveals what was already there, the way stopping the shaking of a jar lets the sediment settle and become visible.

Western analogy: Imagine a browser with thirty tabs open, all playing video at once. Someone tells you the fix is to close the browser completely. But when you try, the system does not calmly shut down — it freezes, the fans spin up, and the computer lags harder than before. A stressed nervous system often reacts the same way to forced stillness: the demand to "stop everything at once" can spike arousal instead of lowering it. Some researchers call this paradoxical reaction relaxation-induced anxiety — the body responding to relaxation itself as if it were a threat.

In TCM terms, the way meditation affects you depends on which system is struggling. Three patterns explain most meditation side effects — and each calls for a different approach.

Pattern 1: Heart-Shen Disturbance — When the Mind Won't Land

If this sounds like you: Sitting still makes your thoughts race harder. Your heart pounds, your chest feels tight, and the quiet room becomes a stage for every worry at once. Sleep may already be shallow, and your mind often feels like it is "on" even when your body is exhausted.

In TCM, the Heart houses the Shen — and the Shen needs a foundation to rest on. That foundation is Heart Blood and Heart Yin: the nourishing, cooling resources that keep the mind calm and anchored. When those resources are sufficient, the Shen settles like a fish swimming calmly in deep water. When they run low, the fish rises toward the surface — reactive, exposed, unable to rest. Our guide to heart palpitations from a TCM perspective explores this pattern in more depth.

Meditation asks the Shen to land. But if there is no calm surface to land on, the mind just circles faster — like a pilot trying to land a plane with the runway lights off. The racing thoughts are not a failure of technique. They are a sign that the Shen lacks the resources to settle.

What may help:

  • Don't close the tabs — close one. Instead of "empty your mind," use a single-point focus that gives the Shen one thing to hold: the feeling of breath at the nostrils, the weight of your hands, or a soft gaze on a candle or a spot on the wall.
  • Keep sits short. Two to five minutes of anchored attention is a meaningful practice. The goal is a Shen that learns to land, not a marathon of stillness.
  • Press HT-7 (Shenmen). On the inner wrist crease, in line with the little finger, HT-7 is the classic "calm the Shen" point. One to two minutes of gentle circular pressure on each wrist, before or after sitting, may help the mind settle. Some studies suggest HT-7 acupressure is associated with reduced anxiety and better sleep.

Pattern 2: Liver Qi Stagnation — The Lid Comes Off

If this sounds like you: Meditation surfaces irritability, frustration, or sudden tears. You feel agitated and restless in the sit — legs twitch, jaw clenches, and you cannot wait for the timer. You may notice tight shoulders, frequent sighing, or a knot in your throat that meditation somehow makes more noticeable, not less.

The Liver in TCM governs the smooth flow of Qi — movement, both physical and emotional. When stress accumulates, Qi can stagnate: the emotional river hits a dam, and pressure builds upstream. During a normal day, that pressure is partially vented by constant activity — moving, working, scrolling, talking. Meditation removes the vent. The dammed-up energy has nowhere to go, and the lid comes off.

This is why the tears or irritability feel so sudden and confusing. They are not a sign that meditation is "unlocking trauma" in some mystical sense — in TCM terms, they are a release of stored-up Qi that daily life has been holding at bay. The pressure was there before you sat down.

Western analogy: A shaken soda bottle. During the day, the pressure stays contained because the cap — your activity and distraction — is on tight. When you sit in stillness, you effectively loosen the cap, and the release can be sudden, fizzy, and messier than expected. The soda was not created by the act of opening the bottle. It was already under pressure.

What may help:

  • Move first, sit second. A brisk walk, some shaking, or gentle qigong before sitting gives stagnant Qi a channel to move through. For this pattern, movement is not the opposite of meditation — it is the first half of it.
  • Let the release be physical. If tears or frustration surface, allow them rather than fighting them. In TCM thinking, this is the stagnation finally moving. If it keeps happening and feels overwhelming, that is a signal to shift approach — and to check in with a professional.
  • Press LV-3 (Taichong). On the top of the foot between the first and second toes, LV-3 is the classic point for moving Liver Qi. Firm circular pressure for one to two minutes per foot may help the energy find its way through.
  • Choose movement-based practices. Walking meditation or mindful movement can deliver many of the same benefits without requiring the body to be still — often a better fit when stillness itself is the trigger.

Pattern 3: Kidney Qi Deficiency — When Stillness Drains You

If this sounds like you: Meditation leaves you feeling spaced out, lightheaded, or oddly detached — like watching yourself from a distance. You might feel exhausted after a sit, or have trouble "coming back" into your body. This is the pattern behind depersonalization-type experiences.

The Kidneys in TCM are the body's deepest reserve — the battery bank that stores your constitutional energy. They also have a specific job in the mind-body system: holding Qi down. In TCM theory, the Lungs send Qi downward and the Kidneys "grasp" it — like an anchor holding a boat in place. When Kidney Qi is strong, your awareness stays rooted in your body. When the reserve runs low, the anchor gets lighter — and instead of settling, awareness floats.

This is why sitting still can feel draining when reserves are low: meditation asks Qi to settle downward, but there is not enough anchoring force to hold it there. The mind lifts off instead of landing.

What may help:

  • Ground before you go inward. Feet flat on the floor, both hands on your thighs, eyes open with a soft downward gaze. Five slow breaths, counting the exhale — the longer the exhale, the more the body is signaled that it is safe to settle.
  • Press KI-1 (Yongquan, "Bubbling Spring"). On the sole of the foot, about a third of the way from the toes toward the heel. This point is traditionally used to root awareness in the body. Press it firmly for a minute per foot before sitting.
  • Short sits, long recovery. Three to five minutes maximum — and stop while you still feel present, not after you have floated away.
  • Rebuild the reserve. Deep sleep is the single biggest factor in restoring Kidney resources. Warm cooked meals, black beans, walnuts, and bone broths are traditional food supports. If you are in a depleted season of life, this is not the time for silent retreats or extended practice.

Self-Check: Which Reaction Sounds Like You?

Most people recognize a little of all three — these systems interact. But one pattern is usually primary, and it points to the most useful adjustment.

What Research Says About Meditation Side Effects

Meditation side effects are real and increasingly documented. A few findings worth knowing:

  • Negative effects are more common than often assumed. A 2020 systematic review of meditation research, published in Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica, found that adverse events were reported across different meditation practices — with anxiety and depression the most frequently documented — and noted that in one randomized trial, roughly 8% of participants reported negative effects.
  • Difficult experiences have their own landscape. Researchers at Brown University interviewed experienced meditators and catalogued the kinds of challenging experiences that can arise — including fear, grief, and shifts in the sense of self (Lindahl et al., 2017, PLOS ONE). Their work suggests that difficulty in meditation is not rare or "wrong" — it is a documented phenomenon.
  • Some people react to relaxation itself. Early research on "relaxation-induced anxiety" (Heide & Borkovec, 1983) found that some individuals experience increased anxiety during relaxation exercises — an observation that resonates with the TCM view that stillness itself can disturb an already unsettled system.
  • Trauma considerations. Clinicians working with trauma have noted that stillness-based practices can surface distressing material, and many recommend trauma-informed adaptations — shorter sessions, eyes open, movement-based practice, and professional support. This parallels the TCM view that the practice should match the person's current state, not the other way around.

None of this means meditation is bad. It means meditation is not one-size-fits-all — which is exactly the TCM starting point.

Practical Takeaways: Practicing With Your Patterns

Practical Takeaways:

  • Match the practice to the pattern. Racing mind → anchored single-point focus and short sits. Restless irritability → movement first, sitting second. Spaced out and drained → grounding, feet on the floor, eyes open, very short sits.
  • Time-box everything. Two to five minutes is a real practice. If a sit leaves you worse than it found you, shorten it or change the format — the goal is a practice you can sustain, not one you endure.
  • Use acupressure as a bridge. HT-7 for the racing mind, LV-3 for the lid-off pressure, KI-1 for grounding. One to two minutes of gentle pressure per point, before or after sitting.
  • Remember that meditation does not require sitting. Walking meditation, mindful movement, qigong, and breath-based practices can offer similar benefits with less stillness — often a better fit for Liver and Kidney patterns. See our guide to breathwork for anxiety for a body-first alternative.
  • Adjust or pause without shame. If a practice consistently leaves you anxious, detached, or exhausted, it is not something to push through. Adjust it, or pause it — and check in with a healthcare provider if symptoms persist.

When to See a Doctor

When to See a Doctor:

Seek professional help if meditation (or any practice) is accompanied by: panic attacks that continue after the session ends; persistent dissociation or feeling detached from reality; worsening depression, hopelessness, or thoughts of self-harm; new or intensifying physical symptoms such as chest pain, fainting, or severe dizziness; or any experience that feels out of your control. These are not "normal meditation discomfort" — they deserve prompt attention from a mental health professional or physician. Pause the practice in the meantime; it will still be there when you are ready.
