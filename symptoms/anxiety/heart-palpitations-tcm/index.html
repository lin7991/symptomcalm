Heart Palpitations and Anxiety: A TCM Guide to Calming the Shen

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

The Symptom That Feels Terrifying (but Usually Isn't)

You're lying in bed, almost asleep — and then it happens. Without warning, your heart lurches. A single thump so strong it makes your whole body flinch. Or maybe it's a fluttering sensation in your chest, like a bird trapped behind your ribs. Or your heart simply starts racing — not from exercise or excitement, but from nothing at all.

You pause and pay attention. Thump-thump. Thump-thump. Is it too fast? Too irregular? You check your pulse at your wrist. Your breathing turns shallow. The more you focus on your heartbeat, the more it seems to misbehave — and the more anxious you become.

If this scenario feels familiar, you're experiencing what doctors call palpitations: the awareness of your heartbeat as unpleasant, forceful, rapid, or irregular. Palpitations are one of the most common reasons people visit cardiology clinics. The vast majority are benign — your heart is structurally normal, but something in your body's signaling system is creating a sensation of alarm.

Traditional Chinese Medicine has a particularly valuable perspective on this experience. In TCM theory, the Heart is not just a pump — it houses the Shen, the aspect of consciousness responsible for emotional stability, mental clarity, and a sense of grounded calm. Palpitations are understood as a signal that the Shen is unsettled — a message from your body that something in the system needs attention.

This article explores the TCM patterns most commonly associated with anxiety-related palpitations, and what you can do — safely and practically — when your heart starts to speak too loudly.

Why Your Heart Is Beating Like This — A TCM Framework

In conventional medicine, palpitations related to anxiety are linked to the autonomic nervous system. Stress activates the sympathetic "fight or flight" branch, which increases heart rate and contractility. The brain notices the change, interprets it as a threat (or a sign of illness), and the cycle intensifies. It's a feedback loop: palpitations create anxiety, and anxiety amplifies palpitations.

TCM approaches the same phenomenon from a different angle. Instead of asking "what's wrong with the heart's electrical system?" it asks: "Why has the Shen lost its home?"

The Shen is like a pilot flying a plane. When the pilot is well-rested, well-fed, and calm, the plane flies smoothly — even in turbulence. When the pilot is exhausted, malnourished, or overwhelmed by noise from the control tower, the plane lurches with every small disturbance. The aircraft (your heart) is fine. The pilot (your Shen) needs support.

Multiple factors can unsettle the Shen in TCM theory — and each produces a slightly different flavor of palpitations. Understanding which pattern fits your experience helps you choose the right approach.

Pattern 1: Heart Blood Deficiency — The "Empty Thump"

Blood in TCM is not just the red fluid in your veins — it's a nourishing, grounding substance that anchors the Shen. When Heart Blood is sufficient, the Shen is stable and the mind feels steady. When Heart Blood is deficient, the Shen has no place to rest — it floats, and the heart responds with palpitations.

How it feels: Palpitations that are more of a strong, empty thump than a racing flutter. You feel your heartbeat as a heavy, forceful beat that almost rocks your body. The sensation is often worse at night when you're lying still and quiet. You may also experience dizziness, pale complexion, poor memory, difficulty concentrating, and dreams that feel vivid or unsettling.

Who it tends to affect: People who have experienced prolonged stress, insufficient sleep, a heavy menstrual flow, or a diet low in iron and B vitamins. Chronic worriers and those who burn the candle at both ends are especially prone — the lifestyle that depletes Blood resources also unsettles the Shen.

The analogy: Think of the Shen as a fish in a pond. When the water (Heart Blood) is deep and clear, the fish swims calmly near the bottom. When the water level drops (Blood deficiency), the fish is closer to the surface — more exposed, more reactive, and visible with every movement. The palpitations are the fish's splash.

What this pattern may respond to: In TCM theory, this pattern is traditionally supported by nourishing the Blood and settling the Shen. Acupressure at HT7 (Shen Men) — located at the ulnar end of the wrist crease, in the hollow on the pinky-finger side — is a classic point for calming the Shen. Taking 2–3 minutes on each wrist to press gently can be surprisingly settling. Foods traditionally considered blood-building include dark leafy greens, red meat, black sesame seeds, goji berries, and beets. Consistent, early bedtimes are considered foundational — the Shen needs sleep to replenish.

Pattern 2: Heart Yin Deficiency With Empty Fire — The Nighttime Racer

Yin is the cooling, moistening, anchoring substance in TCM. Heart Yin provides a calm, cool environment for the Heart to function. When Heart Yin is depleted — often through chronic stress, overwork, or age-related change — the balance tips. Relative "empty heat" (Fire) rises, and the Heart responds by racing.

How it feels: Palpitations that arrive specifically at night or in quiet moments — your heart starts racing as soon as you lie down. There's often a sensation of heat in the chest or palms, a dry mouth or throat, mild night sweats, and a feeling of being "wired but tired." You may find yourself restless in bed, tossing and turning, with your mind buzzing despite physical exhaustion.

Who it tends to affect: People in high-stress careers, those approaching menopause (when Yin resources naturally shift), night-shift workers, and individuals who push through fatigue regularly. It's common in people whose lives demand constant output with insufficient restoration.

The analogy: Heart Yin Deficiency is like a car radiator running low on coolant. The engine (your heart) works fine — but without enough coolant, it runs hot. You feel the heat, and the system becomes more sensitive. The palpitations are the temperature warning light flickering on.

What this pattern may respond to: Cooling, nourishing approaches may be supportive. In TCM, herbs traditionally used for Heart Yin patterns include Suan Zao Ren (sour jujube seed) and Bai Zi Ren (arborvitae seed), though these should be discussed with a qualified practitioner. Acupressure at HT7 (Shen Men) and PC6 (Nei Guan) — located about two finger-widths above the wrist crease between the two visible tendons on the inner forearm — are traditionally used together for calming palpitations with heat sensation. Reducing caffeine, alcohol, and spicy foods may help lower the "empty fire." Cooling evening practices — slow, full-body stretching, a lukewarm bath, or lying with legs up the wall — can help the Yin settle before bed.

Pattern 3: Liver Qi Stagnation With Heart Involvement — The Tension Palpitation

The Liver in TCM governs the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When stress, frustration, or suppressed emotions block this flow, Liver Qi becomes stagnant. Because the Liver channel runs through the chest and connects energetically with the Heart, Liver Qi stagnation easily "invades" the Heart — creating palpitations that feel bound up with emotional tension.

How it feels: Palpitations that come on during or after stressful events — an argument, a tense work meeting, rush hour traffic. The heart feels like it's pounding against a tight chest. There's often a sensation of a lump in the throat, a need to sigh, and tension across the shoulders and jaw. The palpitations may be accompanied by irritability, a feeling of being "about to snap," and digestive issues like bloating or acid reflux.

Who it tends to affect: People in high-pressure environments, those who suppress their feelings ("I'm fine" when they clearly aren't), individuals with sedentary desk jobs where stress accumulates without physical release, and anyone whose schedule leaves no room for decompression.

The analogy: Liver Qi stagnation is like a traffic jam on the highway that backs up so far that the side streets (the Heart channel) get clogged too. The palpitations are the sound of horns honking — the system signaling that congestion has reached a critical point.

What this pattern may respond to: Physical movement that releases Qi is traditionally recommended — brisk walking, side-bending stretches, qigong, or gentle yoga that opens the side body (triangle pose, gate pose, cat-cow). Acupressure at LV3 (Tai Chong) — on the top of the foot between the first and second toes, in the hollow before the bones meet — is a primary point for moving Liver Qi. Progressive muscle relaxation (systematically tensing and releasing each muscle group) for 5–10 minutes can help release the stored tension that fuels this pattern.

Pattern 4: Phlegm-Heat Harassing the Heart — The Unsettled Flutter

Phlegm in TCM refers to a turbid, sticky accumulation that results from poor fluid metabolism — most commonly linked to Spleen Qi Deficiency and a diet rich in greasy, sweet, or processed foods. When Phlegm combines with Heat (from stress, diet, or Liver stagnation), the resulting Phlegm-Heat can "harass" the Heart and disturb the Shen.

How it feels: Palpitations that feel more like a flutter, skip, or irregular thumping — less a steady race and more a chaotic, unsettled rhythm. There's often a sensation of heaviness or oppression in the chest, mental fog or cloudiness, a bitter taste in the mouth, and a tendency toward restless sleep with disturbing dreams. You may feel both anxious and sluggish, which is a confusing combination.

Who it tends to affect: People whose diets include frequent fried foods, dairy, sugar, or alcohol combined with a sedentary lifestyle. Those who experience both anxiety and digestive issues (bloating, acid reflux, thick white coating on the tongue) are more likely to fall into this pattern.

The analogy: Phlegm-Heat is like a room that's both humid and overheated — the air feels thick, hard to breathe, and somehow "wrong." You can turn on a fan (calming techniques), but the underlying humidity (Phlegm) and temperature (Heat) will keep the air uncomfortable until both are addressed.

What this pattern may respond to: Dietary adjustments are considered central — reducing greasy, sweet, and dairy-heavy foods while increasing light, bitter, and cooling options like leafy greens, mung beans, celery, and cucumber. Acupressure at ST40 (Fenglong) — located on the outer lower leg, about midway between the knee and ankle, two finger-widths from the shin bone — is traditionally known as the "Phlegm-transforming" point. Gentle daily movement (even a 20-minute walk) helps clear the stagnation that generates both Phlegm and Heat.

When Palpitations Are (and Aren't) a Medical Emergency

Before exploring any complementary approach, it's essential to know when palpitations require immediate medical evaluation. TCM patterns are designed for functional imbalances — not structural heart conditions. Palpitations that arise from anxiety or stress-related patterns are generally benign, but some presentations can signal underlying cardiac issues.

Seek emergency medical care if your palpitations come with:

  • Severe chest pain, pressure, or tightness (especially radiating to the arm, jaw, or back)
  • Shortness of breath that doesn't resolve within minutes
  • Fainting or near-fainting (syncope or pre-syncope)
  • A sustained heart rate over 150 bpm at rest (not from exercise or fever)
  • A family history of sudden cardiac death or arrhythmias

Schedule a medical evaluation (non-emergency) if:

  • Palpitations are new, frequent, or getting worse over time
  • You have a known heart condition (arrhythmia, valve disease, cardiomyopathy)
  • You're over 65 and experiencing new-onset palpitations
  • Palpitations happen during exercise rather than at rest

For the majority of people whose palpitations are linked to anxiety, stress, or sleep deprivation — and whose medical workup has come back clear — TCM offers a useful framework for understanding why the heart is reacting this way and what may help settle it.

What You Can Do When Your Heart Starts Racing

When palpitations arrive, the first instinct is often to panic — which feeds the cycle. Here are three things you can try in the moment:

1. The Wrist-Pressure Pause (PC6 + HT7)

Sit or lie down. Turn one palm up. Find PC6 (Nei Guan) — about two finger-widths below the base of your palm, between the two prominent tendons. Apply gentle, steady pressure with your thumb for 30 seconds. Then slide your thumb toward the pinky side of the wrist to HT7 (Shen Men) — the hollow at the wrist crease, in line with the pinky finger. Press steadily for another 30 seconds. Switch wrists and repeat. This combined sequence is traditionally used for calming palpitations and settling the Shen.

2. Extended Exhalation Breathing

Palpitations tend to make your breathing shallow and rapid, which further activates the sympathetic nervous system. Counter it by extending your exhale: breathe in for a count of 4, then out for a count of 6–8. Focus on making the exhale slow, smooth, and complete. Repeat for 2–3 minutes. This activates the vagus nerve and supports the parasympathetic "rest and digest" state — directly counteracting the stress response that drives palpitations.

3. The Tongue Drop (mental reset)

When palpitations grab your attention, your awareness locks onto your chest. Gently let your tongue fall to the bottom of your mouth — relax the jaw, soften the throat, and let the tongue rest heavy. This simple release often helps the neck and chest muscles follow suit. Complete three slow breaths with a soft jaw, then check in with your heart again.

What Research Says About Palpitations, Anxiety, and TCM Approaches

While research specifically on TCM and anxiety-related palpitations is still emerging, several lines of evidence are relevant:

  • Acupuncture for palpitations and anxiety: A 2020 systematic review in Medicine found that acupuncture — particularly at points including HT7 (Shen Men) — was associated with improvements in palpitation frequency and anxiety scores, though the authors noted that larger, higher-quality trials are needed. The autonomic regulatory effects of acupuncture are reasonably well-documented: 2018 review in Acupuncture in Medicine found PC6 stimulation was associated with modulation of autonomic nervous system activity, including reduced nausea, postoperative pain, and potentially cardiac-related anxiety.
  • HT7 (Shen Men) for anxiety-related cardiac symptoms: Laboratory studies in animal models have found that electroacupuncture at HT7 may influence heart rate variability (HRV) and reduce stress-induced cardiac responses — findings that align with its traditional use for calming the Shen and settling palpitations (Frontiers in Neuroscience, 2021).
  • Vagus nerve and extended exhalation: A 2018 study in Frontiers in Psychology confirmed that slow breathing — particularly with prolonged exhalation — increases vagal tone and HRV, providing a clear physiological mechanism for an approach TCM has recommended for centuries: when the Shen is disturbed, regulate the breath.
  • The gut-heart connection: A 2022 study in Nature Communications found that certain gut bacteria were associated with lower anxiety scores and better stress resilience — supporting the TCM concept that digestive health (Spleen function) influences the Shen's stability, and that addressing gut health may have downstream benefits for palpitations related to anxiety.

Lifestyle Foundations for a Calm Shen

Beyond in-the-moment techniques, TCM emphasizes daily habits that build a stable environment for the Heart and Shen:

Sleep as the First Medicine

The Shen rests during sleep. In TCM theory, the hours of 11 PM to 3 AM are particularly important for the Heart and Liver systems. Consistent early bedtimes — ideally asleep by 10:30–11 PM — are considered one of the most powerful ways to support Heart Blood and Yin. Even 30 minutes of earlier sleep can make a noticeable difference over two weeks.

Caffeine and Stimulant Awareness

Caffeine directly activates the sympathetic nervous system and unsettles the Shen. For people prone to palpitations, even one cup of coffee — especially on an empty stomach — can trigger a cascade. Consider switching to lower-caffeine alternatives (green tea, which contains L-theanine as a calming counterbalance) or caffeine-free herbal teas. If you do drink coffee, have it with food, not before.

Gentle Movement Over Intense Exercise

While exercise is generally beneficial, intense cardiovascular training can temporarily spike the same stress hormones that drive palpitations. During periods when palpitations are frequent, prioritize gentle movement: walking, qigong, slow flow yoga, or tai chi. These forms of exercise move Qi without over-activating the sympathetic branch, which aligns with the TCM principle of "nourishing before clearing."

Practical Takeaways

Calming the Shen: Your Quick Reference

  • Know your pattern: Is your palpitation an empty thump (Blood deficiency), a nighttime racer (Yin deficiency), a stress-triggered pounding (Liver Qi stagnation), or an unsettled flutter (Phlegm-Heat)? Match your approach to your pattern.
  • Master two acupoints: HT7 (Shen Men) at the wrist crease on the pinky side, and PC6 (Nei Guan) two finger-widths below the wrist crease between the tendons. Use these when palpitations start or before bed.
  • Extend your exhale: When the heart races, slow the breath — especially the exhale. Inhale 4 counts, exhale 6–8 counts. Repeat for 2–3 minutes.
  • Prioritize early sleep: The hours before midnight carry particular weight for Heart Blood and Yin restoration. Aim for lights out by 10:30–11 PM.
  • Reduce stimulants: Caffeine, alcohol, and spicy foods can all unsettle the Shen. Test a 2-week reduction and notice how your heart responds.

When to See a Doctor

While this article focuses on the TCM understanding of anxiety-related palpitations, it is essential to work with a healthcare provider to ensure nothing more serious is going on. TCM is a complementary perspective — it offers insight into why your heart may be reacting this way and what traditionally used approaches may support it, but it does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment.

Consult a healthcare provider if any of the following apply to you:

  • You experience chest pain, shortness of breath, or fainting alongside palpitations
  • Your palpitations are new, increasingly frequent, or different in character from before
  • You have a known heart condition or a family history of heart disease or arrhythmias
  • You're taking medication that affects heart rate or rhythm
  • Your palpitations interfere with daily activities or cause significant anxiety

For most people with anxiety-related palpitations that have been medically cleared, the TCM patterns described in this article offer a practical framework for understanding what's happening in your body and what may help. Start with the pattern that sounds most like you — and give the simplest approach (acupressure, extended exhalation, or earlier sleep) a few days to work. Often, the most effective remedy is the one that addresses the root cause, not the one that silences the symptom fastest.
