Airplane Headaches: Why Flying Triggers Head Pain

Symptom Guide · 9 min read

The seatbelt sign comes on. The plane noses into its descent, and twenty minutes out from the gate a hot spike of pain plants itself behind your right eye. Sharp enough that you press your palm against your brow and close one eye. Then the wheels touch down, the cabin door opens, and within half an hour the pain has evaporated as suddenly as it arrived.

If that scene is familiar, you are not imagining it, and you are not alone. A headache that appears only in connection with flying — and especially during the landing phase — has a name in the medical literature: airplane headache, formally listed as "headache attributed to aeroplane travel" in the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3). It is one of the most stereotyped headaches known to medicine: severe, brief, usually one-sided, and tied with almost mechanical reliability to a specific stretch of your journey.

This article looks at what is known about it, and at how Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) reads the same experience — not as a mystery, but as a pressure-equalisation problem with a clear internal logic.

A note before we start: This is educational content about how two frameworks describe the same symptom. It is not a diagnosis, and it is not a substitute for medical care. An unusual headache pattern deserves a clinician's attention — especially the first time it happens.

What an Airplane Headache Actually Feels Like

Airplane headache has a signature so consistent that doctors describe it as "stereotyped" — meaning the attacks in one person tend to look almost identical each time. Across published case series, the same features keep appearing:

  • Timing: the pain arrives during the flight, most often during descent and landing — sometimes during take-off, and occasionally at cruise.
  • Speed: onset is abrupt. There is no slow build-up; the pain is simply there.
  • Intensity: described as severe to very severe — strong enough that many passengers press on the area or reach for pain relief.
  • Location: usually one-sided, concentrated around the eye, the eyebrow, or the forehead on that side.
  • Duration: short. In one large series, the pain lasted under 30 minutes in up to 95% of cases.
  • Resolution: it fades on its own once the flight phase that sparked it is over, usually with no lingering symptoms.
  • Absent extras: typically no nausea, no vomiting, no visual aura, and no light sensitivity — which is one of the things that separates it from migraine, even though the pain can feel migraine-like.

Three details are worth noticing. First, it may not happen on every flight — many people find it comes and goes depending on things like whether they have a cold, how tired they are, or which seat they are in. Second, the site of the pain can switch sides between flights. Third, people who get it often start to dread flying because of it — in one survey of 140 cases, more than three-quarters said the headaches had affected their willingness to travel by air.

It is also worth separating this from two lookalike patterns. A high-altitude headache comes from thin air and low oxygen at real altitude — it builds with exertion and eases when you descend the mountain. Weather-related headaches follow changes in outdoor barometric pressure at ground level. An airplane headache is different again: the pressure change is rapid, engineered, and finite — it lasts about as long as the descent does.

Why Descent Is the Trigger: The Pressure-Equalisation Problem

To understand why this happens, it helps to know one fact about the cabin you are sitting in. A pressurised aircraft cabin is not held at sea-level pressure. At cruising altitude it is typically maintained at an equivalent of roughly 6,000 to 8,000 feet (about 1,800 to 2,400 metres). That is a compromise: enough pressure and oxygen to keep passengers comfortable, but not so much that the structure carries unnecessary load.

The consequence is that during descent, cabin pressure rises back toward sea level. Every air-filled space in your body has to keep up. That is why your ears "pop" — the pressure outside the eardrum changes and the middle ear needs to equalise through a narrow tube.

Your sinuses work the same way. They are small, air-filled cavities in the bones of your face, and each one drains and ventilates through a narrow opening called an ostium. When that passage is clear, pressure equalises without you noticing anything at all. When the lining is swollen — from a cold, an allergy, dry cabin air, or simply the way your particular sinuses are built — the cavity becomes, in effect, a sealed room with a stuck door. The air pressure outside changes; the pressure inside cannot follow.

That mismatch produces a vacuum-like effect, described in the literature as a "squeeze" or "reverse squeeze." The lining of the sinus stretches, and the trigeminal nerve branches that supply the area — the same nerve network that refers pain to the forehead and around the eye — start signalling. The result is pain exactly where an airplane headache is usually felt: behind the eye, in the brow, across the forehead on one side.

Two honest complications keep this from being a complete explanation. The first: in a large share of people with airplane headache, imaging and examination find no sinus disease at all. The second: the attacks are inconsistent — same person, same sinuses, but only some flights set them off. Researchers therefore describe it as a multimodal problem: anatomy plus cabin environment plus whatever temporary state you happen to be in that day. Sleep debt, dehydration, alcohol, a mild cold, stress, and even where you are seated in the cabin have all been discussed as contributing factors.

Which is where a different way of reading the body becomes useful.

The TCM View: Clear Yang, the Head's Orifices, and a Sealed Cabin

In TCM theory, the head is described as the meeting place of all the Yang energies — the highest point of the body, and the place that receives the clearest, lightest, most refined Qi. That rising quality is called Clear Yang (清阳). It is what keeps the head bright: clear thinking, sharp senses, open sinuses, an easy sense of pressure equilibrium.

For Clear Yang to reach the head and for the head's "orifices" — the eyes, ears, nose, and sinus passages — to stay open and comfortable, three things need to be working: enough Qi to push upward, enough fluids to keep the passages moist and freely moving, and no obstruction sitting in the way.

Now put that next to a cabin at 38,000 feet. In TCM terms, three stressors arrive at once:

  • Dryness. Cabin air typically carries a fraction of the humidity of ordinary indoor air — often around 10–20%, drier than most deserts. In TCM, that is a direct drain on the body's fluids (Jin-Ye), the moistening layer that keeps membranes supple and passages open.
  • Stagnation. Sitting still for hours, with the neck held in one position and Qi and Blood moving sluggishly, is the classic setup for what TCM calls stagnation — flow that slows, thickens, and stops moving freely through the channels.
  • Pressure as an external event. TCM has long described the body as constantly negotiating with its environment — climate, season, weather, and, in the modern world, an engineered microclimate that changes faster than any weather front.

Here is the analogy that makes it click. Think of your sinuses as the cabin of the aircraft itself: a sealed space that is only comfortable because a vent keeps the pressure matched to the outside. When the vent is clear, you notice nothing. When the vent is plugged — by swollen tissue, thickened fluids, or dryness that makes everything sticky — the sealed room can no longer equalise with the world outside it, and the pressure has nowhere to go but into the walls.

In TCM, that plug is often described as Phlegm-Dampness: the thickened, sluggish product of fluids that stopped moving properly. And the pain itself — sharp, rising, one-sided, behind the eye — is often read as Qi that has nowhere to move, pushing upward against an obstruction.

None of this replaces the barotrauma explanation. It is a different map of the same territory: one describes the mechanics of pressure, the other describes the state of the passages and the flow that keeps them clear. Together they set up an obvious practical question — what can you actually do about it?

Three Patterns Behind an In-Flight Headache

In TCM, the same symptom can arise from different underlying patterns, and the pattern you recognise suggests a different emphasis in what may help. Here are three that fit the airplane-headache picture. Most people recognise themselves in one or two.

Pattern 1: Phlegm-Damp Blocking the Orifices — The Sealed Cabin

This is the pattern closest to the sinus story. Fluids have thickened and are sitting in the passages rather than moving through them. In TCM, the Spleen is responsible for transforming food and drink into clean, usable fluids; when it is overtaxed — by heavy meals, dairy-rich eating, alcohol, or simply being run-down — the result is a heavier, murkier fluid that accumulates rather than circulates.

  • Pressure and fullness across the brow, cheeks, or behind the eyes, often worst on one side
  • Congestion, a blocked nose, or thick postnasal drip that gets worse in dry air
  • A heavy, foggy head — thinking feels slow, the way a room feels humid
  • Pain that tracks with descent, and often flares on flights taken while you have a cold or allergy symptoms
  • Feeling worse after a heavy meal or alcohol, better with warmth and steam

The emphasis here is on thinning and moving what is stuck: fluids, warmth, and keeping the passages moist. For the fuller picture of how TCM reads sinus-related pressure outside of flying, see our guide to sinus headaches.

Pattern 2: Liver Yang Surging Upward — The Pressure Spike

In TCM, the Liver is the organ that keeps movement smooth and helps you adapt to stress — and its energy naturally tends to rise. When stress, poor sleep, and stimulants pile up, that rising energy can lose its anchor and surge toward the head. Add a rapid pressure change, and you have the perfect conditions for a sudden, sharp, one-sided attack.

  • Sharp, severe, throbbing pain around one eye or the eyebrow, arriving within minutes
  • A sense of pressure or heat in the head, sometimes with a flushed face
  • Feeling wired but tired — often after a short night, an early flight, or a stressful trip
  • Coffee, alcohol, or a skipped meal in the hours beforehand
  • A personal or family history of migraine, which many airplane-headache sufferers share

Here the same pain that reads as "pressure in a sealed space" is also read as upward-moving energy with nothing to hold it down — like a pressure-relief valve on a system that is being asked to hold more than it can. This is the in-flight cousin of the Liver Fire headache pattern, but the trigger is the flight itself. For more on the underlying idea, see What Is Qi?

Pattern 3: Dryness with Qi and Blood Not Reaching the Head — The Empty Misting System

When fluids run low and Qi is depleted, the head stops receiving its full supply. There is no dramatic obstruction and no surging energy — just a dull, heavy, global ache that feels like the lights have been dimmed. Long-haul flights, dehydration, and alcohol are the classic recipe.

  • Dull, band-like pressure over the whole head rather than one spot
  • Dry eyes, dry nose, dry mouth, scratchy throat, tight skin
  • Fatigue, light-headedness on standing, and a feeling of being wrung out
  • Worse on long flights, worse with alcohol, worse if you barely drank water
  • Often lingers a little past landing rather than vanishing the moment the wheels touch down

The emphasis here is rehydration and rebuilding, not clearing — a very different response from Pattern 1. For a deeper look at how low fluids produce head pain, see our guide to dehydration headaches.

The Flight-Day Protocol: Before, During, and After

Because airplane headache is tied to a predictable timeline, it is one of the few headache patterns you can plan around in advance. Here is a sequence organised by the phase of travel — with the caveat that any medication decisions belong with your clinician.

Before You Board (24–48 hours out)

  • Arrive hydrated, not dehydrated. Drink steadily in the day before the flight. In TCM terms you are topping up the fluids that the dry cabin will draw down.
  • Protect your sleep the night before. Sleep debt shows up in the "surging" pattern more than almost anything else.
  • Go easy on alcohol the night before and skip it in the air. Alcohol is drying and adds load at exactly the moment your body is already handling pressure change.
  • If you always fly congested, talk to a clinician ahead of time. Published case series describe anti-inflammatory medication taken about an hour before take-off being associated with fewer attacks, and nasal decongestants and antihistamines being discussed as alternatives for people who cannot take that class of pain relief. Those are clinical decisions — not something to self-prescribe at the gate.

At Boarding and During Cruise

  • Sip water throughout, rather than drinking a large amount at once. Small and steady matches how the body uses fluids.
  • Keep the nasal passages moist. A simple saline spray or rinse before boarding is a low-risk habit many frequent flyers use; steam from a hot drink helps too.
  • Don't sleep with your neck twisted. A scarf or small pillow that keeps the neck supported avoids hours of accumulated tension at the base of the skull.
  • Move when you can. Standing, ankle circles, and a slow walk down the aisle keep Qi and Blood moving through a body that is otherwise parked.

The Descent Window (the 20 minutes that matter)

  • Start early — before the crew announces descent. Pressure changes begin well before the wheels are down.
  • Swallow, yawn, or chew. These are the natural motions that open the passages between the throat and the middle ear, and they help keep everything equalising.
  • Keep sipping. Swallowing does double duty: it helps equalise pressure and keeps membranes moist.
  • Breathe out slowly and long. A gentle extended exhale — in through the nose, out through pursed lips, twice as long — calms the body and keeps the neck and jaw from clenching.
  • Use warmth. A warm compress, a hot drink, or simply a palm pressed over the brow can ease the "stuck" quality of the pressure. Warmth is traditionally used to help move stagnation.
  • Try the acupressure sequence below. It takes about two minutes and can be done discreetly in your seat.

After You Land

  • Rehydrate properly — water plus something with a little salt and sugar, not just coffee.
  • Give yourself ten quiet minutes. Neck rolls, shoulder release, and a slow breathing pattern help clear the residue of a cramped descent.
  • Log the flight. Note which side hurt, at what point, how congested you were, and how you slept. After three or four flights, patterns become obvious — and obvious patterns are easier to plan around.

Acupressure Points for In-Flight Headaches

Acupressure is the use of firm, steady finger pressure on the same points used in acupuncture. It requires no equipment, which makes it unusually well suited to a cramped economy seat. Press each point with your thumb or index finger in small circles for 30–60 seconds, breathing slowly. Firm but never sharp or painful.

The Five Points

  • LI-4 (Hegu) — in the fleshy web between your thumb and index finger, on the back of the hand. Traditionally used for head and face pain of many kinds. Note: traditionally avoided during pregnancy.
  • GB-20 (Fengchi) — the two hollows at the base of the skull, where the neck muscles meet the skull. Excellent for pain that sits at the back of the head or spreads from the neck. Press upward and slightly toward the opposite eye.
  • EX-HN-5 (Taiyang) — in the temple, about one finger-width behind the midpoint between the outer eyebrow and the outer eye corner. Traditionally used for one-sided, throbbing pain around the eye.
  • Yintang — midway between the inner ends of your eyebrows. Traditionally used for frontal pressure, a heavy head, and a restless mind. Press with a flat fingertip, not a nail.
  • LI-20 (Yingxiang) — in the small hollow beside each nostril, level with the middle of the nose. Traditionally used for nasal congestion and sinus pressure — the "blocked vent" in the cabin analogy.

If queasiness comes with the pain, add PC-6 (Neiguan) — two finger-widths up from the wrist crease, between the two tendons on the inner forearm — which is traditionally used for nausea and chest tightness.

Two practical notes. First, acupressure during a flight is a comfort measure, not an emergency tool — if the pain is severe and unfamiliar, think of it as information rather than just an inconvenience. Second, cleanliness matters on a plane: use hand sanitiser before touching your face, and never press on an area that is injured, swollen, or painful to touch.

What Research Says

Airplane headache is a young field, but it is unusually well documented for such a specific symptom.

  • It is now a recognised classification. The International Classification of Headache Disorders lists "headache attributed to aeroplane travel" under headaches attributed to disorders of homeostasis — an acknowledgement that the spark is a change in the body's internal environment, not a structural disease.
  • The largest case series to date (Mainardi and colleagues, Cephalalgia, 2012) described 75 patients and confirmed the stereotyped picture: pain lasting under 30 minutes in up to 95% of cases, a clear relationship to the landing phase, unilateral pain, a male predominance, and the absence of accompanying symptoms. The authors considered barotrauma — pressure injury to the sinus spaces — one of the main mechanisms involved.
  • A follow-up series of 140 cases reported that 85% had strictly one-sided pain, that the frontal-orbital region was the most common site, and that mean age at onset was around 36 years. Attacks were severe or very severe, lasted under 30 minutes, and resolved spontaneously. Anti-inflammatory medication taken before the flight was associated with relief or avoidance of attacks in more than half of those who used it, and most sufferers said the problem affected their willingness to fly.
  • The "sealed sinus" mechanism was described in detail by Berilgen and Mungen (Cephalalgia, 2011), who studied 22 patients and reviewed cases in the literature. Their explanation is the one used throughout this article: a sinus cavity whose opening is narrowed becomes a closed air space, and the pressure differential during ascent or descent produces a vacuum effect. In their small series, taking naproxen sodium about an hour before take-off was followed by no attack on subsequent flights in the great majority of cases — a promising but small and uncontrolled observation, not proof.
  • The cabin environment matters. The same papers point to low cabin humidity (often around 10–20%), mild low-oxygen conditions at cabin altitude, and reduced air quality as factors that leave the nasal and sinus linings dry and more reactive — which fits the TCM emphasis on dryness and stuck fluids remarkably well.
  • Secondary causes must be excluded. Case reports describe longer-lasting flight-related headaches in people with chronic rhinosinusitis, which is why a recurring pattern deserves a proper clinical assessment rather than assuming it is the benign variety.
  • On acupressure specifically: no trial has yet studied acupressure for airplane headache. The evidence for point pressure in headache generally is broader but mixed, and best described as "some studies suggest a modest effect." That is an honest description of where the evidence stands — which is exactly why this article presents acupressure as a comfort measure rather than a solution.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ A typical airplane headache is brief, one-sided, tied to a phase of flight, and resolves quickly after landing. Anything outside that pattern deserves medical attention.

Seek emergency care immediately if you experience the worst headache of your life or a sudden "thunderclap" headache, a headache after a head injury, a headache with fever and a stiff neck, or a headache accompanied by vision changes, weakness, numbness, confusion, difficulty speaking, or loss of consciousness.

See a doctor soon if a flight-related headache:

  • Lasts for hours or days, or does not settle after landing
  • Becomes more frequent or more severe over time, or starts happening on every flight
  • Comes with ear pain, muffled hearing, blood from the nose, or facial swelling
  • Occurs alongside a cold, sinus infection, or allergy flare that will not clear
  • Interferes with your work, travel, or daily life

Ear, nose, and sinus barotrauma is usually benign, but it can occasionally cause complications — and the medications discussed in the research literature are not appropriate for everyone, particularly people with stomach, kidney, heart, or bleeding conditions, or those who are pregnant. Talk to a clinician about whether flying is safe for you when you are congested, and about any medication you are considering taking before a flight. Nothing in this article is a substitute for that conversation.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways: your in-flight pressure plan

  1. Learn your timeline. If pain arrives during descent, the problem is pressure equalisation — which means the useful window is the 20 minutes before landing, not after the pain starts.
  2. Hydrate steadily from the day before, and skip alcohol in the air. Dry cabin air plus alcohol is the clearest self-inflicted version of the dehydration pattern.
  3. Keep the passages open and moist. Saline spray before boarding, frequent swallowing, and warm drinks during descent are simple, low-risk habits.
  4. Support your neck, don't sleep twisted in the seat. Hours of cramped neck posture set up the tension that leaves the head more vulnerable.
  5. Keep a two-minute acupressure sequence ready: LI-4, GB-20, Taiyang, Yintang, and LI-20 — plus PC-6 if you feel queasy. Clean hands first, gentle steady circles throughout.
  6. Track the pattern. Side, timing, congestion, sleep, and what you ate or drank. Three flights of notes will tell you more about your own triggers than any general advice can.
  7. Take the unusual cases seriously. Brief and stereotyped is reassuring; long, worsening, or accompanied by other symptoms is a reason to see a clinician.
