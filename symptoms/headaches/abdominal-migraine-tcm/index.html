Abdominal Migraine: When the Pain Shows Up in Your Stomach

Symptom Guide · 10 min read
Before we dive in: This guide is about a migraine that skips the head almost entirely and shows up in the belly. If your episodes arrive with both head pain and dizziness, our companion guide to vestibular migraine covers that pairing, and our ocular migraine guide covers the visual version. For the underlying concept — the Liver putting pressure on the digestive system — see Liver invading the Spleen. For nausea on its own, we have a full guide to nausea from a TCM perspective.

Imagine a migraine that never reaches your head.

No throbbing temple. No light sensitivity. No aura. Instead, the pain arrives in your belly — a deep, cramping ache around the navel that comes in waves, with nausea strong enough that all you want is a dark room and stillness. An hour or two later, it lifts. You feel wrung out, then normal again. Weeks or months pass. Then it happens again, almost identically, as if someone pressed play on the same recording.

If that pattern sounds familiar, and every digestive test has come back normal, you may be looking at abdominal migraine — a real, recognized diagnosis that most people have never heard of, and one that has a surprisingly coherent story inside Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM).

What Abdominal Migraine Actually Is: The Western Picture

Abdominal migraine belongs to a family of conditions called episodic syndromes that may be associated with migraine. It is listed in the International Classification of Headache Disorders — the same reference book clinicians use to classify migraines and cluster headaches — which tells you something important: this is not a vague "sensitive stomach." It is a defined pattern with defined features.

The criteria clinicians look for include:

  • Repeated attacks. Typically at least five separate episodes.
  • Pain lasting 2 to 72 hours when it is not interrupted by medication or sleep.
  • Pain that sits in the middle. Around the navel, at the midline, or poorly localized rather than clearly in one corner of the abdomen. It is usually described as dull or "just sore" rather than sharp and pinpoint.
  • Moderate to severe intensity — enough to stop normal activity.
  • At least two accompanying features during the pain: loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, or pallor (going pale and washed out).
  • No better explanation after a clinician has looked for other causes.

Two details surprise most people. First, abdominal migraine is diagnosed most often in children — school-age children in particular — which is why it is sometimes dismissed as "growing pains" or "a nervous stomach." But adults receive the diagnosis too, especially adults with a personal or family history of migraine. Second, the condition tends to move rather than disappear. Many children who have abdominal attacks go on to develop typical migraine headaches later in life, or alternate between the two presentations across years. For families, this is a familiar echo: a parent with migraines, a child with belly attacks.

There is a close relative worth knowing about: cyclic vomiting syndrome, which follows the same episodic logic — repeated, stereotyped episodes with intense vomiting and complete recovery in between.

And because abdominal migraine is a clinical diagnosis — made by recognizing a pattern and ruling out other conditions, not by a single scan or blood test — it is frequently missed for years. Children and adults alike can go through endoscopies, ultrasounds, allergy panels, and elimination diets before anyone says the word "migraine."

The modern bridge: Western research increasingly describes this as a gut-brain phenomenon. The digestive tract and the nervous system share signaling molecules — including serotonin, most of which actually lives in the gut, and CGRP, a key player in migraine — along with a constant two-way conversation through the vagus nerve. A 2021 narrative review in the journal Headache laid out this gut-brain connection between gastrointestinal disorders and migraine. In other words, the idea that a "head" condition could express itself in the abdomen is no longer fringe — it is where the mainstream conversation is heading.

Same Storm, Different Drainage Basin: How TCM Reads It

Here is the analogy that makes this whole thing click.

Picture a storm system parked over a mountain range. The storm is one event — one mass of energy, one set of conditions. But where the water ends up depends entirely on the terrain beneath it. On one side of the ridge, it pours into a valley and floods a river town. On the other side, it drains into a basin and pools quietly. Same storm. Different drainage basin.

In TCM, the "storm" is a disruption in the smooth flow of Qi — think of Qi as the body's operating system, the signaling and movement layer that keeps everything running in rhythm. The Liver, in TCM theory, is the organ system responsible for keeping that flow smooth and adaptable, especially under pressure. When stress, frustration, overwork, or irregular living keeps the Liver's flow knotted, the pressure has to go somewhere.

Two exits are common:

  • Upward — the pressure rises toward the head, and you get the classic migraine experience: throbbing pain, irritability, light sensitivity, ringing ears. TCM calls this Liver Yang rising.
  • Inward, into the middle — the pressure runs into the body's processing center, the Middle Jiao (Stomach and Spleen), and you get cramping, waves of nausea, loss of appetite, and pallor instead of a headache.

That second route is traditionally described as the Liver invading the Stomach and Spleen — the Wood element overriding the Earth element, if you like the Five Elements vocabulary. Think of a factory floor where one department is running at double speed and keeps shoving work at the next station. Nothing is broken. The loading is wrong, so the line jams.

Another way to picture it: the same alarm bell, wired to a different room. The signal is real and the bell is real — it is just sounding somewhere else.

This framing explains three things that otherwise seem odd about abdominal migraine:

  • Why it comes and goes so completely. Qi disruption is a functional pattern, not a structural injury. When the pressure resolves, the symptoms resolve — which is exactly why the tests look clean.
  • Why it is episodic and stereotyped. The trigger hits the same terrain, so the water finds the same channel every time. Same storm, same basin.
  • Why stress, skipped meals, poor sleep, and hormone shifts all seem to set it off. All four of them put pressure on the same flow. In TCM theory, the trigger is not "the stomach" at all — the stomach is simply where the pressure shows up.

Three Patterns Behind the Belly Pain

In TCM, the same symptom can come from different roots — and the root decides which supports make sense. Here are the three patterns that most commonly show up in episodic abdominal pain. Most people recognize themselves in one, sometimes two.

Pattern 1: Liver Qi Invading the Stomach and Spleen — The Argument in the Middle

This is the stress-linked pattern, and it is the most common shape in children and busy adults alike. The attacks tend to cluster around pressure: school exams, a difficult season at work, family tension, a big deadline, an emotional upset. The pain comes in waves rather than as a steady ache, and it often settles or shifts as the mood shifts.

  • Cramping, gripping pain that comes and goes in waves, often around the navel
  • Pain that flares right after an upset or during a stressful stretch, then eases when the situation resolves
  • Nausea, belching, sighing, and a feeling of a knot in the stomach or throat
  • Loss of appetite at the thought of eating; a person who is often tense, sensitive, or hard on themselves
  • Often worse in the days before a period in menstruating adults
  • Bowel habits that swing — some urgency one week, sluggishness the next

In TCM terms, nothing is damaged — the flow is being squeezed. Approaches traditionally used for this pattern emphasize unblocking the flow: steady breathing with a longer exhale than inhale, moving the body daily, taking pressure off the schedule, and calming the Liver channel with LV-3 and PC-6. Regular meals matter more than perfect ones: an empty stomach is more susceptible to pressure.

Pattern 2: Phlegm-Damp Crowding the Middle — The Swamp

This pattern builds more slowly and feels heavier. The classic TCM image is a drainage ditch clogged with silt: the channel is still there, but the water cannot move, so it backs up and turns murky. People with this pattern typically feel vague and queasy rather than sharply cramping, and their heads often feel foggy at the same time.

  • Dull, heavy, oppressive ache rather than sharp waves; a sense of fullness in the upper abdomen
  • Queasiness and a thick or greasy taste; a feeling of phlegm in the throat; a visible greasy coating on the tongue
  • Worse in humid or damp weather, and after heavy meals — dairy, fried food, sweets, creamy sauces, excess bread
  • Sleepy and foggy after eating; limbs feel heavy; sometimes sticky or sluggish stools
  • Episodes tend to be longer and groggier, with a need to lie down rather than to pace

Support traditionally used here is about drying and lightening: warm, cooked, easily digestible food in smaller amounts, less dairy and fried food, ginger and other warming aromatics, and gentle daily movement — this pattern responds poorly to complete stillness but well to steady, moderate activity.

Pattern 3: Cold in the Stomach and Spleen — The Cold Engine

The third shape is a warmth-and-fuel pattern. In TCM theory, the Spleen and Stomach work like a cooking pot: they need warmth to break food down into usable energy. When that warmth runs low — through cold foods, irregular eating, chronic fatigue, or simply constitution — digestion slows and the middle grows chilly and uncomfortable.

  • Dull, cramping pain that eases with warmth and pressure — a warm pack or a hand on the belly genuinely helps
  • Clearly worse after iced drinks, ice cream, raw salads, or cold fruit, and better after soup or warm tea
  • A pale, washed-out look during attacks; cold hands and feet; low appetite; often a preference for warm drinks
  • Loose or unformed stools; feeling tired both during and after episodes
  • Episodes tend to cluster in cold months or after cold exposure

Approaches traditionally used for this pattern rebuild warmth and rhythm: warm cooked meals at the same times each day, soup and congee, ginger and gentle warming spices, avoiding iced drinks as a daily habit, and regarding rest as part of the plan rather than a failure of willpower.

A note on overlap: these patterns are not boxes. Most people are a mix — a stress-linked pattern layered on top of a cold or damp constitution — and the mix can shift with age, season, and life circumstances. What the patterns give you is a hypothesis to test with a qualified practitioner, not a self-diagnosis.

During an Attack: Settling the Middle

Safety first: if you are actively vomiting or in severe pain, sit or lie down somewhere you cannot fall before doing anything else. Never press any point while standing or while driving. And if the pain is new, severe, or simply different from your usual episodes, get medical attention before experimenting with self-care — the red flags are listed at the end of this article.

During an episode, the goal in TCM terms is not to force the symptoms away — it is to stop adding load to the middle and let the pressure discharge. That translates into four practical moves.

1. Reduce the load

  • Dim, quiet, unstressful space. Loose clothing. No screens if light makes the nausea worse.
  • Do not force food. If appetite is gone, let the stomach rest; when it returns, start with a few spoonfuls of congee, plain rice, or toast rather than a full meal.
  • Sip, do not gulp: small mouthfuls of warm water or warm ginger tea, one at a time.

2. Add gentle warmth (for the Cold and Damp patterns)

  • A warm pack or hot water bottle wrapped in cloth over the abdomen is traditionally used for cramping that eases with heat.
  • If warmth clearly helps, that is useful information about your pattern — note it.
  • Skip the heat if the belly is hot to the touch, tender in a way that feels inflamed, or if there is any fever. In that case, seek medical review instead.

3. Use acupressure on five points

These points are traditionally used for the middle and for nausea. Press gently with the pad of a thumb or finger, in small slow circles or with steady pressure, for 1 to 2 minutes per point on both sides, while breathing with a long, slow exhale. For children, use much lighter pressure and 30 to 60 seconds — and let them lead.

  • REN-12 (Zhongwan) — on the midline of the abdomen, about four finger-widths above the navel, roughly midway between navel and the base of the breastbone. This is the belly's own point: traditionally used for the Stomach and the Middle Jiao. Press gently and only on a comfortable, non-tender area.
  • PC-6 (Neiguan) — on the inner forearm, three finger-widths up from the wrist crease, between the two tendons. The most studied point in TCM, traditionally used for nausea and the sensation of a knot in the chest.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width outside the shin bone. Traditionally used to strengthen the Stomach and Spleen and to bring energy back down into the legs.
  • LV-3 (Taichong) — on the top of the foot, in the hollow between the first and second toes, just above where the bones meet. Traditionally used when a pattern is stress-driven and the flow needs smoothing.
  • SP-4 (Gongsun) — on the inner edge of the foot, in the hollow just below the base of the big toe joint. Traditionally used for Stomach and intestinal discomfort.

4. Breathe with a longer exhale

Slow breathing with an exhale longer than the inhale is one of the few tools that works in both traditions at once: it settles the nervous system, and in TCM terms it helps Qi move downward instead of backing up. Try four counts in, six to eight counts out, for two to three minutes.

If you are pregnant: two points commonly used elsewhere — LI-4 (on the hand) and SP-6 (above the inner ankle) — are traditionally avoided during pregnancy, and neither is included in the list above. More importantly: abdominal pain in pregnancy should be assessed by a clinician promptly rather than managed with self-care. Talk with your healthcare provider before using any point work, herbal tea, or supplement while pregnant or breastfeeding.

Between Attacks: The Trigger and Food Map

Here is the part that matters most in TCM — and the part that is easiest to skip. The episode is the visible event; the terrain between episodes is what decides how often the storm returns. Two weeks of regular meals and honest sleep will not show up on a scan, but they change the channel the water takes.

What a "steady middle" plate looks like

Food energetics is a core TCM idea: foods are not just calories, they have a warming or cooling, drying or moistening quality, and the middle responds to that quality. For episodic belly pain, the general direction is warm, cooked, and regular:

  • Warm cooked grains and soups — rice congee, oatmeal, noodle soups, broths. Congee with a slice of ginger is a classic gentle staple.
  • Cooked vegetables rather than large raw salads, especially in cold or damp weather; pumpkin, carrot, squash, and greens are commonly used.
  • Ginger, in tea or in food, is the most traditional warming aromatic for the stomach and for queasiness.
  • Small, frequent meals instead of two large ones — the pot works better with steady moderate heat than with a flood.
  • Fewer icy drinks, less fried food, less refined sugar, and — for those who notice it — less dairy. Food triggers are individual: keep a simple diary for a month and trust your own data over any list, including this one.

A third pillar is rhythm, and it is the one most families overlook. In TCM theory the Middle Jiao has its own schedule — asking it to handle a large late-night meal is like asking a kitchen to fire up after it has been cleaned and closed. Consistent meal times, a real breakfast, and a lighter evening meal are unglamorous and disproportionately effective.

What Research Suggests

It is worth being precise about the evidence here, because this is a condition where honest framing matters more than enthusiasm.

  • It is a recognized diagnosis, not a vague complaint. Abdominal migraine appears in the International Classification of Headache Disorders, with the criteria described above. Recognition is the first step that most people are missing.
  • The gut-brain link is increasingly well described. A 2021 narrative review in Headache examined the connection between gastrointestinal disorders and migraine, pointing to shared signaling pathways including serotonin and CGRP. This is the modern vocabulary for something TCM described in flow terms centuries ago.
  • The pediatric evidence for interventions remains limited. A 2025 systematic review in The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health assessed interventions for IBS, functional abdominal pain, and abdominal migraine in children — and the honest takeaway from that literature is that the evidence base is still thin and inconsistent. When the mainstream literature says "we need better trials," that is a signal to be humble about any approach, TCM included.
  • Episodic brain-gut syndromes are a recognized family. Cyclic vomiting syndrome — reviewed in Frontiers in Neurology (2020) and in a 2021 Headache management guide — shares the same episodic structure and overlaps clinically with abdominal migraine, supporting the idea that these are related expressions rather than unrelated stomach problems.
  • Acupressure has its clearest evidence for nausea. A 2020 systematic review in Pediatric Anesthesia looked specifically at PC6 stimulation in children, and a 2025 Cochrane network meta-analysis reviewed PC6 stimulation for nausea and vomiting. This is the strongest, most reproducible finding in the whole TCM toolkit — and nausea is a core feature of abdominal migraine, which is why PC-6 appears in every protocol in this article.
  • Mind-body approaches have real pediatric support. A 2022 review in World Journal of Clinical Pediatrics on non-pharmacological management of pediatric functional abdominal pain disorders found the more consistent evidence for approaches such as gut-directed hypnotherapy and cognitive behavioral strategies. The parallel with TCM's emphasis on stress flow and nervous-system settling is worth noticing — two traditions arriving at the same lever from different directions.
  • Dietary approaches are being studied but not settled. A 2023 network meta-analysis in European Journal of Pediatrics examined dietary interventions for pediatric functional abdominal pain disorders; results point toward benefit for some approaches without establishing a single best diet. Individual trigger tracking remains the practical answer.

Read together, these studies are the opening chapters of a research story, not the last word. Nothing here suggests that any traditional approach replaces proper medical evaluation — but the direction of the science and the logic of TCM are, unusually, pointing at the same place: the gut and the nervous system are one conversation.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  • Get the pattern named. If episodes are repetitive and tests are clean, ask a clinician specifically about abdominal migraine and other episodic syndromes associated with migraine — the diagnosis is made by pattern, not by a scan.
  • Note your pattern shape. Waves tied to stress (the Argument), heavy foggy queasiness (the Swamp), or cramping that eases with warmth (the Cold Engine) each point toward different supports. Track across several episodes, not one.
  • Eat by the clock. Three meals at consistent times, warm and cooked by preference, small amounts if appetite is low. Irregular eating is the most modifiable trigger on the list.
  • Keep acupressure in your back pocket. PC-6 for nausea, REN-12 and ST-36 for the middle, LV-3 when stress is the driver, SP-4 for belly discomfort — 1 to 2 minutes each. Skip LI-4 and SP-6 entirely if you are pregnant, and speak with your provider first.
  • Think of the days between episodes as the real work. Sleep timing, meal rhythm, daily gentle movement, and honest stress management set the terrain. The episode is where the storm lands; the ordinary days decide where the water goes.
  • Build a team, not a replacement. TCM is a complementary perspective. Keep your gastroenterologist or pediatrician in the loop, and tell every practitioner what else you are doing.

When to See a Doctor

Seek emergency medical care immediately if abdominal pain comes with any of the following:

  • Severe pain that is new, sudden, or clearly different from your usual episodes — especially pain that makes the abdomen rigid, swollen, or too tender to touch
  • Vomiting that is green or blood-stained, or vomit that looks like coffee grounds
  • Blood in the stool, or black tarry stools
  • Fever with abdominal pain, or pain with a rapid heartbeat and feeling faint
  • Signs of dehydration — especially in a child: no urine for many hours, no tears when crying, sunken eyes, drowsiness or unusual lethargy
  • Yellowing of the eyes or skin, unexplained weight loss, or pain that repeatedly wakes the person from sleep
  • Any new weakness, numbness, confusion, slurred speech, or a headache that peaks within seconds

Also see a clinician — not urgently, but soon — if episodes are frequent enough to disrupt school, work, or daily life; if the pattern has never actually been diagnosed; if you are considering herbal medicine, supplements, or point work for a child, during pregnancy, or alongside prescription medication; or if the episodes are changing in character. Abdominal migraine is a clinical diagnosis that requires other conditions to be excluded first, so please do not self-diagnose. TCM is a complementary approach, not a substitute for proper evaluation.
