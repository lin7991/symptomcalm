Tension Headache Self-Care: Daily Prevention Protocols from a TCM Perspective

Symptom Guide · 10 min read

You feel the familiar tightness creeping across your forehead by mid-morning. By lunch, it's a dull band of pressure around your entire head. You stretch your neck, massage your temples, maybe reach for ibuprofen. The pain subsides for a few hours, then returns — sometimes worse than before.

Tension headaches affect up to 78% of people at some point in their lives. They are the most common headache type in the world — and yet most of us only react to them once they've already arrived.

This article focuses on something different: what you can do before, during, and after a tension headache — from a TCM perspective. For a broader overview of what tension headaches are in TCM theory and how they relate to Qi stagnation, see our general guide to tension headaches in TCM. Here, we focus on the how: specific protocols, acupressure sequences, daily routines, and self-massage techniques you can integrate into your day.

Step 1: Confirm It's a Tension Headache

Before reaching for any protocol, it's worth confirming you're dealing with a tension pattern rather than a different headache type. The wrong technique can aggravate certain patterns — for example, firm acupressure on a Liver Fire headache may intensify the throbbing.

Feature Tension Headache Not Tension (Consider Other Patterns)
Pain quality Dull, pressing, band-like, squeezing Throbbing, pulsating, stabbing, electric
Location Both sides of head, forehead, back of head, band-like One-sided, temple, behind one eye, top of head only
Neck/shoulders Almost always tight or sore May or may not be involved
Worse with Prolonged sitting, screen time, stress accumulation Light, sound, movement, specific foods
Nausea or vomiting Rare Common in migraine patterns

If your headache matches the tension profile, proceed with the protocols below. If you're unsure or the pain pattern is different, consider browsing our guide to identifying your headache type in TCM.

The 5-Minute Real-Time Relief Protocol

When you feel a tension headache building — that first whisper of tightness in the temples or the familiar stiffness at the base of your skull — this is the critical window for intervention. The following sequence takes less than five minutes and targets the primary channels involved in tension headaches.

Think of it like this: The tension you feel is Qi caught in a traffic jam in the channels of your neck, shoulders, and head. The goal isn't to force the energy through — it's to release the blockages that are preventing smooth flow. Each point below acts like opening an exit ramp on a congested highway.

Point 1: GB-21 (Jian Jing) — Shoulder Gateway

Location: Midway between the base of your neck and the tip of your shoulder — the highest point of the shoulder muscle. You'll feel a tender depression when you press.

Technique: With your opposite hand, press firmly into GB-21 using your thumb. Apply steady pressure while breathing slowly — hold for 15-20 seconds. You should feel a deep ache or release sensation that may radiate down the arm.

Why it works: GB-21 is a major command point for releasing neck and shoulder tension. In TCM theory, the Gall Bladder channel carries Qi stagnation from the shoulders to the head — releasing GB-21 opens the upstream pressure.

Point 2: GB-20 (Feng Chi) — Base of Skull Release

Location: In the hollows at the base of your skull, between the two vertical neck muscles, about two finger-widths apart.

Technique: Place both thumbs on the GB-20 points and the fingers of each hand on top of your head for support. Press upward and slightly inward — as if you're trying to push through the base of your skull. Hold for 20-30 seconds with steady, gentle pressure while tilting your chin slightly downward.

Why it works: GB-20 is the primary point where Wind (external pathogens) enters the body and where Qi stagnation accumulates at the neck-head junction. It's one of the most commonly used points in TCM for any headache involving the back of the head and neck.

Point 3: LI-4 (He Gu) — General Headache Point

Location: In the webbing between your thumb and index finger. Squeeze the thumb and index finger together — LI-4 is at the highest point of the muscle bulge.

Technique: With your opposite thumb, press firmly into LI-4 using a circular kneading motion for 20-30 seconds. This point is typically sensitive — the sensation should be a deep ache, not sharp pain. Switch hands and repeat.

Why it works: LI-4 is the most famous facial and headache point in TCM. The Large Intestine channel runs to the face and head, and LI-4 has a powerful effect on moving Qi in the head region. Research has found LI-4 stimulation to be associated with activation of brain regions involved in pain modulation.

Point 4: Tai Yang (EX-HN5) — Temple Relief

Location: In the depression at the temple, about one finger-width behind the outer edge of the eyebrow.

Technique: Use your middle fingers to press both Tai Yang points simultaneously. Start with gentle circular pressure — small clockwise circles — for 30 seconds. Gradually increase pressure as the muscle softens.

Why it works: The temple region is where tension headaches announce themselves most clearly. Tai Yang is an extra point (not on a specific meridian) that directly targets the tension pattern in the temporal region.

⚠️ Important: If any point produces sharp, electric, or radiating pain beyond a deep ache sensation, release pressure immediately. Acupressure should never cause sharp pain. Avoid LI-4 during pregnancy — it is traditionally contraindicated due to its strong Qi-moving effect on the pelvic region.

The Micro-Break Routine: Desk Workers' Prevention Protocol

For most people with tension headaches, the desk is ground zero. Prolonged sitting with forward head posture creates a steady, low-grade obstruction in the Bladder and Gall Bladder channels that accumulates throughout the day.

This sequence takes 90 seconds and should be done every 45-60 minutes during desk work:

  1. Chin tuck (15 seconds). Sit up straight. Draw your chin straight back (not down) as if making a double chin. Hold 5 seconds, release. Repeat 3 times. This releases tension at the suboccipital muscles where GB-20 sits.
  2. Shoulder rolls (15 seconds). Roll both shoulders backward in a full circle 5 times, then forward 5 times. Focus on the full range of motion — most people barely move their shoulders through half of their available range.
  3. Ear-to-shoulder stretch (15 seconds each side). Gently tilt your head toward one shoulder — not pulling with your hand, just gravity. You should feel the stretch along the opposite side of the neck. Hold 15 seconds, switch sides.
  4. GB-20 self-massage (20 seconds). Press both thumbs into GB-20 as described above. While holding, gently nod your head "yes" 5 times — this moves the suboccipital muscles through the acupoint. You'll feel the release deepen with each nod.
  5. Stand and breathe (15 seconds). Stand up, place one hand on your lower abdomen, and take 3 slow deep breaths — inhale through your nose, exhale through your mouth. The standing position clears the channel obstruction caused by prolonged sitting.

Many people find that the first day of doing this routine, the tension feels more noticeable — because you're actually paying attention to it. By day three or four, you may notice the baseline level of daily tension is measurably lower.

The Morning Prevention Routine

In TCM theory, stagnation patterns are easiest to address in the morning, when Qi is naturally rising and the channels are most responsive. This 3-minute morning routine sets the channel system up for smooth flow throughout the day:

  1. Scalp tapping (30 seconds). Use your fingertips to gently tap across your entire scalp — forehead, temples, crown, back of head. The tap should be firm but not painful. This stimulates blood and Qi circulation through the scalp's many channel endpoints. Many people report a sense of mental clarity within seconds.
  2. Neck rolling (30 seconds). Slowly roll your head in a half-circle — chin to left shoulder, back to center, chin to right shoulder. Do not roll the head backward (this compresses the cervical spine). Repeat 5 times each direction.
  3. LI-4 activation (20 seconds each hand). Before you start your day, activate LI-4 on both hands with firm circular pressure. This is like opening a circuit — ensuring Qi flow to the head is unrestricted before the day's stress begins to accumulate.
  4. Brisk 3-minute walk or stair climb. Nothing moves Qi like physical movement. Even three minutes of brisk walking activates the body's Qi circulation, particularly in the Liver and Gall Bladder channels that govern smooth flow. If you can extend this to 10-15 minutes, the preventive effect is even stronger.

Evening Wind-Down: Releasing the Day's Accumulated Tension

By evening, the day's Qi stagnation has settled into predictable patterns. The evening routine focuses on releasing this accumulated tension before it becomes tomorrow's headache.

Self-Massage Sequence (3 minutes)

Using gentle to moderate pressure, work through this sequence in order. Use your thumb or middle finger for precision, or the flat of your palm for broader areas:

  1. Trapezius squeeze. Reach across and squeeze the muscle on top of your shoulder (the trapezius) between your thumb and fingers. Walk your fingers along the muscle from neck to shoulder tip. Spend extra time on any knots or tight spots.
  2. Suboccipital release. Place your fingertips at the base of your skull, just below the GB-20 hollows. Press in and slightly upward, holding for 10 seconds. You may feel a release sensation as the suboccipital muscles relax.
  3. Scalp combing. Use your fingertips to firmly comb through your scalp from front to back, as if you're parting your hair. Apply enough pressure to move the scalp over the skull. Repeat 10 times.
  4. Jaw release. Tension headaches often involve the jaw. Place your fingertips on the masseter muscles (cheek muscles) and press gently while opening and closing your mouth slowly 3 times. Then press at the temporomandibular joint (just in front of your ear) while gently opening and closing.

Herbal Tea Support

Certain herbal teas have traditionally been used to support smooth Qi flow and release tension patterns. These are gentler than concentrated herbal formulas but can be a pleasant addition to an evening wind-down routine:

  • Chrysanthemum tea (Ju Hua). Traditionally used for headaches involving the head and eyes. Cooling and light, it helps calm Liver-related tension that accumulates from screen time and mental strain.
  • Peppermint tea. Its aromatic nature (the "acrid" quality in TCM terminology) helps move stagnant Qi in the head and neck. Some research suggests peppermint's aromatic compounds may have a mild muscle-relaxing effect.
  • Lemon balm tea. Traditionally used for stress-related patterns. It has a gently calming effect that supports the evening transition from "doing" to "resting."
  • Chamomile with a mint leaf. Chamomile settles the Shen (spirit), while mint adds the Qi-moving quality. Simple, accessible, and pleasant as a nightly ritual.
A note on caffeine and tension headaches: Many people turn to coffee or caffeinated tea when a tension headache strikes. Caffeine can provide temporary relief by constricting blood vessels and masking fatigue. But in TCM theory, it drives Qi upward and outward — a short-term fix that can deepen the pattern of Qi rising excessively. If you use caffeine, consider doing so in the morning only, and keep evening beverages caffeine-free to allow Qi to settle naturally.

When Tension Headaches Become Chronic: Identifying Your Dominant Pattern

If you experience tension headaches 15 or more days per month, the general prevention routines above may help, but a more targeted approach based on your specific TCM pattern could be more effective. The following are the three most common underlying patterns for chronic tension headaches:

Pattern 1: Liver Qi Stagnation Dominant

Key signs: Tension worsens with stress, improves with movement or exercise, accompanied by irritability or frustration. Headaches are often worse on the sides of the head. You tend to sigh frequently and feel a lump in the throat when stressed.

Priority interventions: Movement-based approaches work best — brisk walking, qigong, or any rhythmic exercise. Gall Bladder channel work (GB-20, GB-21) is especially effective. Evening stretching or yoga that opens the sides of the body (side bends, hip openers) can release accumulated Liver Qi stagnation.

Foods to emphasise: Mint, basil, scallion, radish, turmeric, and other pungent, aromatic foods that "move" Qi.

Pattern 2: Spleen Qi Deficiency with Dampness

Key signs: Tension headaches with a heavy, foggy sensation. The head feels wrapped or "full." Headaches worsen after meals, especially heavy or greasy food. You feel mentally sluggish, physically heavy, and your digestion tends toward bloating or looseness.

Priority interventions: Dietary adjustments are central — lighter meals, reducing dairy and fried foods, emphasising warming cooked foods like soups and congee. The micro-break routine (above) is especially important after meals. Midday rest or a short walk after lunch can prevent the afternoon headache buildup.

Foods to emphasise: Ginger, warm foods, soups, congee, barley, pumpkin, and other Spleen-supporting foods.

Pattern 3: Blood Deficiency Pattern

Key signs: Tension headaches that are worse with fatigue, especially late in the day or after mental exertion. You may feel dizzy or lightheaded. The pain is often dull rather than pressing. Palms and soles may feel dry or numb. Sleep is light or unrefreshing.

Priority interventions: Rest is medicine. Pushing through the headache makes it worse. Prioritise 7-8 hours of quality sleep, and consider an earlier bedtime (10:00-10:30 PM) during high-stress periods. Gentle acupressure at ST-36 (below the knee) and SP-6 (inner leg above ankle) can support Qi and Blood production.

Foods to emphasise: Dark leafy greens, beets, black sesame seeds, goji berries, bone broth, dates, and other Blood-nourishing foods.

Pattern self-assessment: Your tension headaches may involve a combination of these patterns. The most common hybrid is Liver Qi stagnation with underlying Spleen Qi deficiency — the stress of modern life (Liver) compounded by poor dietary habits (Spleen). Start with the pattern that feels most aligned with your experience, and incorporate elements from others as needed.

What Research Says About TCM Approaches for Tension Headaches

While the following research should not be considered conclusive, it provides context for the approaches described above:

  • A 2021 meta-analysis in BMJ Open found that acupuncture may be more effective than sham acupuncture and conventional drugs for tension-type headaches, with effects maintained at follow-up.
  • A 2019 systematic review in Frontiers in Neurology found that massage therapy was associated with reduced tension headache frequency and intensity in several small trials.
  • Research on acupressure for headaches (International Journal of Nursing Practice, 2018) suggested that self-administered acupressure may help reduce headache intensity in some individuals, though more rigorous studies are needed.
  • Some Chinese-language studies have explored specific herbal formulas (particularly variations of Chuan Xiong Cha Tiao San) for tension headaches classified as Qi stagnation patterns, with preliminary findings suggesting symptom reduction. Larger, higher-quality trials are needed to draw firm conclusions.

These findings are promising but not definitive. TCM is a complementary approach that works alongside — not in place of — conventional medical care.

Practical Takeaways

  1. Catch it early. The window between the first sensation of tension and a full-blown headache is your best opportunity for intervention. The 5-minute acupressure protocol is most effective when used at this stage.
  2. Move every hour. Qi stagnation is the root of tension headaches, and the single most effective prevention is physical movement. The 90-second micro-break routine is designed to fit into any workday.
  3. Match your approach to your pattern. The same acupressure point works differently for different people. If a point doesn't feel effective, try a different one — or consider whether a different underlying pattern may be at play.
  4. Build the morning routine. Five minutes in the morning spent on scalp tapping, neck rolling, and LI-4 activation can prevent five hours of headache later in the day.
  5. Consider professional support. Acupuncture (from a licensed practitioner), Tui Na therapeutic massage, and customised herbal formulas can offer deeper, longer-lasting relief than self-care alone. When to See a Doctor

⚠️ Seek emergency medical attention if you experience: the worst headache of your life (sometimes called a "thunderclap headache"), headache after a head injury, headache with fever and stiff neck, headache accompanied by vision changes, confusion, weakness on one side of the body, or trouble speaking. If your headache pattern changes significantly — becoming more frequent, more intense, or different in character — consult a healthcare provider to rule out other causes. This is especially important if you are over 50 and experiencing new headaches, or if you have a history of cancer or immune suppression. TCM is a complementary approach and does not replace emergency care or proper medical diagnosis.
