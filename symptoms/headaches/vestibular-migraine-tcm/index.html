Vestibular Migraine: A TCM View of Dizziness and Head Pain

Symptom Guide · 9 min read

The room tilts. You put a hand on the wall, and in the same breath the familiar throb starts up behind one eye. Within minutes you are both dizzy and in pain, and you cannot tell which symptom caused which — because it feels less like two problems and more like one event wearing two faces.

If that pairing sounds familiar, you may have spent a long time getting answers that didn't quite fit. Your hearing test was normal. Your MRI was normal. Someone suggested it was anxiety, or your neck, or maybe low blood sugar. And you went home still spinning.

There is a name for this particular pairing, and it is called vestibular migraine. What makes it interesting — and what makes it such a good fit for a Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) lens — is that the two symptoms are not coincidences. They travel together. In TCM, that is not an accident to explain away. It is the whole clue.

Before we dive in: This guide is about the coupling — when the spinning and the head pain arrive as one event. For the concept underneath it, see our TCM Basics article on Internal Wind, the body's stability system. For dizziness on its own, without a headache attached, our companion guide to vertigo and dizziness covers the broader landscape. And if your episodes are pure head pain with no spinning, the general migraine pattern guide is the better starting point.

What Is Vestibular Migraine? The Western Picture

For a long time, "dizziness plus headache" had no tidy home in Western medicine. It was filed under vague labels like migraine-associated vertigo and regarded as a bit of an orphan. That changed in 2012, when the Bárány Society and the International Headache Society published a joint consensus defining vestibular migraine as a distinct diagnosis (Lempert et al., Journal of Vestibular Research, 2012). Since then it has moved from the margins toward the center — a 2024 update in Current Opinion in Neurology notes it is now recognized as a distinct migraine phenotype rather than a vague side effect.

The agreed diagnostic criteria are worth knowing, because they describe a pattern most people recognize instantly once they see it written down:

  • At least 5 episodes of vestibular symptoms at moderate or severe intensity
  • Each episode lasting 5 minutes to 72 hours — a wide window that catches everything from a brief swirl to a multi-day fog
  • A current or previous history of migraine, with or without aura
  • Migraine features during at least half of the episodes — this is the "50% rule," and it is the heart of the diagnosis. In those episodes you get the migraine signature alongside the dizziness: a headache with migraine-like qualities (one-sided, pulsating, moderate to severe, worse when you move), or sensitivity to light and sound, or a visual aura

If someone meets only part of that list, clinicians use the term probable vestibular migraine — which is a way of saying "this looks like the pattern, let's keep watching."

Who tends to have it? It shows up at any age, but women are affected considerably more often than men — reported ratios range from roughly 1.5 to 1 up to 5 to 1. Because the criteria require a 5-minute minimum, it also sits in a diagnostic neighborhood crowded with lookalikes: BPPV (the brief positional spinning caused by displaced crystals in the inner ear), Ménière's disease, and persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD), a chronic unsteadiness that can follow a vestibular event. Distinguishing these is genuinely a job for a clinician, and a 2026 review in Headache was written specifically about the difficulty of telling PPPD and vestibular migraine apart.

What the episodes actually feel like varies. Some people get true rotational vertigo. Others get a rocking or swaying sensation, a feeling of being on a boat, or a sudden intolerance to head movement. Nausea is common. Light and sound often become unbearable. Sometimes there is ear fullness or pressure — but significant hearing loss points elsewhere, which is an important clue.

One Storm, Two Symptoms: The TCM View

Here is where TCM offers something genuinely useful rather than merely quaint.

Western medicine reads the pairing as a diagnostic signature — the fact that both symptoms appear together is what identifies the condition. TCM takes that same observation and pushes it one step further: if two symptoms reliably arrive together, share triggers, and share a timeline, then approaching them as separate problems is working against the grain. One storm, two visible effects.

The TCM category that describes this is Internal Wind. In TCM theory, "Wind" is not a literal weather event — it is the label for anything that moves, shakes, sways, comes and goes, and tends to affect the head and the senses. Think of it as the body's stability-and-motion signature. The classic markers of Wind are exactly the markers of a vestibular migraine episode: spinning, visual disturbance, symptoms that shift location, a sudden onset and a sudden clearing.

The second half of the picture is where that Wind comes from. In TCM, the Liver system is associated with smooth, even flow throughout the body — and it is the system most affected by stress, frustration, sleep loss, and alcohol. When flow is under pressure for long enough, the traditional description is that the pressure rises upward toward the head, carrying Wind with it. That is the "storm" framing: pressure below, turbulence above, and the senses caught in the middle.

But Wind is not the only current in play. A second, very common story runs through Phlegm-Dampness — the TCM category for heaviness, turbidity, and sluggishness. If the first pattern is a storm, this one is a fog: a dense, clouded, weighed-down quality that thickens the senses instead of rattling them. And a third story is depletion — an underlying reserve that has quietly run low, so that episodes cluster whenever you are run down.

Notice how closely this mirrors the modern clinical picture. Western criteria say that at least half of your dizzy episodes must also carry migraine features for the diagnosis to hold. TCM notices the same statistical coupling from the other direction: stress, skipped meals, disrupted sleep, alcohol, weather shifts, and hormonal swings tend to move both the spinning and the head pain at once. Two traditions, describing the same underlying wiring.

Three TCM Patterns Behind Vestibular Migraine

Most people reading this will recognize themselves in one of the three descriptions below — or in a blend of two. Patterns in TCM are not boxes; they are tendencies, and they can shift over months.

Pattern 1: Liver Yang Rising with Wind — The Storm

What it feels like: The dizziness arrives fast and hard, and the headache is throbbing or pulsating, often on one side and around the temple. Movement makes everything worse. Light and sound feel like an assault. You may notice irritability in the hours before an episode, along with tension in the neck and shoulders, a bitter taste in the mouth, ringing in the ears, or a shimmering disturbance in your vision.

What tends to set it off: A stressful stretch at work. A short night. Alcohol. Skipping lunch. A jolt of caffeine after weeks without it. The pattern is one of accumulated pressure finding a release valve — and the head is the release valve.

Traditionally supported by: Settling and cooling what has risen. Acupressure at LV-3 (Taichong) and GB-20 (Fengchi). Cooling, light foods — cucumber, celery, pear, watermelon, mint, chrysanthemum tea. Protecting sleep with a consistent bedtime. An honest look at alcohol and caffeine. A slow, long exhale when you notice the pressure building.

Pattern 2: Phlegm-Damp Misting the Head — The Fog

What it feels like: The spinning is less like a violent rotation and more like a rocking, swaying, or tilting — as if the floor has developed a gentle roll. The head feels stuffed, heavy, or cottony. Nausea is prominent and often the worst part. Thinking is slow and thick, and the whole episode has a cloudy rather than electric quality. It is frequently worse in humid or damp weather, and worse after heavy, greasy, or very sweet meals.

What tends to set it off: Rich food, creamy or fried dishes, excess sugar, dairy for some people, long stretches of sitting, and damp climates. This pattern builds slowly and lingers.

Traditionally supported by: Drying and lightening what has become heavy. Acupressure at PC-6 (Neiguan) for the nausea and ST-36 (Zusanli) to support digestion. Moving away from heavy, greasy, and sugary food toward warm, cooked, simpler meals. Ginger tea, which has a long traditional association with settling the stomach. Gentle daily movement — this pattern generally responds poorly to stillness and well to steady, unhurried activity.

Pattern 3: Kidney-Liver Yin Deficiency with Empty Heat — The Depleted Reserve

What it feels like: A longer history, with episodes that cluster when you are run down, under-slept, or overworked. The dizziness is often lower-grade but relentless, with ringing in the ears, dry eyes and mouth, disturbed sleep, a sense of heat in the palms or at night, and a profound tiredness in the day or two after an episode. This pattern is common in the years around menopause, when the body's cooling and reserve systems are shifting.

What tends to set it off: Overwork, chronic short sleep, pushing through exhaustion, and anything that draws on your reserves without replenishing them.

Traditionally supported by: Nourishing and rebuilding rather than clearing. Acupressure at KI-3 (Taixi) and LV-3. Slow-cooked, mineral-rich foods — black sesame, goji berries, cooked leafy greens, eggs, bone broth, warm fluids. Protecting sleep as a non-negotiable rather than a nice-to-have. Reducing high-intensity exercise during depleted stretches in favor of walking, qigong, or restorative movement.

The Episode Timeline: Support at Each Stage

Where TCM is at its most practical with vestibular migraine is in staging. Different phases of an episode call for different responses, and trying to do the wrong thing at the wrong time is one of the most common frustrations people report. Here is how the timeline usually runs.

Stage 1: The Build — Hours to a Day Before

Many people, once they learn what to look for, can feel an episode coming. The signals are individual but often consistent: a peculiar irritability, neck and shoulder tension, a shimmer or blur at the edge of vision, unusual thirst or appetite changes, yawning, or a heightened sensitivity to light and smell.

This is the highest-leverage window, because you are still upright and functional. Consistent sleep tonight rather than "one more episode of the show." Eat a real meal at a normal time. Keep alcohol off the table. Lower the intensity of whatever you were about to do. A few minutes of GB-20 and LV-3 acupressure, slow exhales, and a dimmer evening may not stop the episode — but in TCM terms, you are reducing the pressure that feeds the Wind rather than adding to it.

Stage 2: The Acute Episode — Safety First

When the spinning is active, the rules change. Safety is the first priority: sit or lie down somewhere you cannot fall, ideally in a low-light, quiet room. Do not drive. Do not try to push through a workout or a commute.

From a TCM perspective, this is not the moment for a complicated regimen — it is the moment for gentle, settling contact. Slow breathing with a long exhale. Small sips of water or warm ginger tea rather than large gulps. Light pressure at PC-6 for nausea, and at Yintang or Taiyang if you can tolerate touch around the head. Keep the room dim and the sound low. Many people find that forcing themselves to "get on with it" lengthens the episode considerably.

Stage 3: The Aftermath — The Day After

The migraine hangover is real and under-discussed. Even once the spinning stops, you may feel flattened, foggy, and emotionally raw for a day or more. This phase is about refilling, not catching up on everything you missed.

Warm, easy-to-digest food. More fluid than you think you need. Gentle movement only — a short walk rather than a session. An early night. If you can avoid scheduling anything demanding in this window, do; regarding the aftermath as part of the episode rather than a return to normal is one of the more useful mindset shifts available here.

Stage 4: Between Episodes — The Foundation

This is where the real work lives, and where TCM and modern vestibular medicine quietly agree. Regularity is the theme: consistent sleep and wake times, meals at predictable hours, steady hydration, and a caffeine intake that does not swing between zero and too much. For the Dampness pattern, add daily movement and a lighter, less greasy diet. For the depletion pattern, add genuine rest — not scrolling rest, but sleep and stillness.

If persistent unsteadiness lingers between episodes, ask your clinician about vestibular rehabilitation. It is a graded, exercise-based approach to retraining balance, and it is one of the better-supported western approaches for central vestibular disorders (discussed further in the research section below). In TCM language, it is a very sensible way of retraining the body's stability system — the two frameworks are pointing in the same direction.

Acupressure for the Spinning and the Throb

Acupressure is simply self-applied pressure on traditional points. It costs nothing, needs no equipment, and is generally a low-risk practice — but it is a comfort measure, not a substitute for medical care.

Safety first: if you are actively spinning, sit or lie down before you press anything. Never press while standing or driving. Stop if a point makes your symptoms worse. And if you have a red-flag symptom (see below), seek medical attention rather than reaching for acupressure.

  • GB-20 (Fengchi) — in the two soft hollows at the base of your skull, where the neck muscles meet the skull. In TCM this is a classic point for Wind affecting the head. Press inward and slightly upward with both thumbs for 1–2 minutes, breathing slowly.
  • Yintang — the midpoint between your eyebrows. A calming point; gentle pressure here pairs well with slow breathing.
  • Taiyang — the hollows at your temples. Useful for the throbbing component. Use a light, small circular pressure — never dig in.
  • PC-6 (Neiguan) — on the inner forearm, about three finger-widths up from the wrist crease, between the two prominent tendons. Traditionally used for nausea, and one of the most-studied points in the whole TCM repertoire for that purpose.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — about four finger-widths below the kneecap, slightly outside the shin bone. A grounding, energy-supporting point that suits the Dampness and depletion patterns.
  • LV-3 (Taichong) — on the top of the foot, in the trough between the first and second toes. A go-to point for the Liver pressure pattern in TCM theory.

Technique is simple: firm but never painful pressure, small slow circles or steady hold, 1–2 minutes per point, both sides, once or twice daily. Pair it with a long, slow exhale — the breath does a surprising amount of the work.

Pregnancy note: LI-4 (Hegu, in the webbing between thumb and index finger) and SP-6 (Sanyinjiao, above the inner ankle) are traditionally avoided during pregnancy and are therefore not included in the list above. If you are pregnant, speak with your healthcare provider before using acupressure for these symptoms.

Building Stability Between Episodes

If you take one idea from this article, take this one: with vestibular migraine, what you do on your ordinary days has more influence than what you do mid-episode. The episodes are the storm; the between-times are the climate.

Regularity as a TCM Strategy

Sleep and meals are the two biggest levers, and they are unglamorous. A consistent bedtime and wake time — including weekends, as much as life allows — supports the body's internal rhythms. Eating at predictable hours reduces the fuel dips that TCM associates with Spleen weakness, and that in modern terms track with the well-documented relationship between skipped meals and headache onset. Hydration matters too: even mild dehydration is a recognized headache trigger.

Food, By Pattern

Rather than a universal list, TCM organizes food by what your pattern needs. A few directions that people often find useful:

  • Storm pattern: favor cooling and light — cucumber, celery, leafy greens, pear, watermelon, mint, chrysanthemum tea. Go easier on alcohol, spicy food, and heavy caffeine.
  • Fog pattern: favor warm, cooked, simple meals — soups, steamed vegetables, whole grains, ginger. Go easier on fried food, excess sugar, rich sauces, and heavy dairy.
  • Depleted pattern: favor nourishing, slow-cooked, mineral-rich food — bone broth, eggs, black sesame, goji berries, cooked dark leafy greens. Aim for warm and regular rather than raw and erratic.

Movement and Environment

Movement helps most people, but the dose matters. During a stable stretch, gentle daily activity — walking, tai chi, qigong — tends to support the Dampness pattern especially. During a depleted stretch, scale back rather than push. And use the environment as an ally: dim screens in the evening, matched screen and room brightness, sunglasses in harsh light, and awareness of how humid or stormy weather tends to affect you. Many people with vestibular migraine track weather as a genuine trigger, and honoring that pattern is not weakness.

What Research Suggests

It is worth being precise about what is established and what is still emerging, because vestibular migraine sits in a field that has moved fast in the last decade.

  • The diagnosis itself is now well-defined. The 2012 Bárány Society and International Headache Society consensus criteria (Lempert et al., Journal of Vestibular Research) established the 5-minute-to-72-hour episode length and the "50% rule" requiring migraine features in at least half of episodes. A 2024 update in Current Opinion in Neurology describes vestibular migraine as an increasingly recognized distinct migraine phenotype.
  • The mechanism is still being mapped. A 2024 narrative review in Current Pain and Headache Reports summarizes current thinking on vestibular migraine pathophysiology, involving both central and peripheral vestibular processing and shared trigeminal-vascular pathways — which is one reason the symptoms couple so tightly.
  • Vestibular rehabilitation has meaningful support. A 2019 review in Current Opinion in Neurology describes graded, exercise-based vestibular rehabilitation as useful in central vestibular disorders, which some clinicians apply to persistent dizziness between episodes.
  • Acupuncture specifically for vestibular migraine is early-stage. Some studies suggest potential benefit, but the evidence is still thin and should be described honestly: published work includes a 2021 randomized controlled trial protocol for prophylaxis in Frontiers in Neurology, a 2022 feasibility study of electroacupuncture in Neuromodulation, and a 2024 single case report in Acupuncture in Medicine. Protocols, feasibility studies, and single cases are the beginning of a research story, not the end of one.
  • Getting the diagnosis right matters. A 2026 review in Headache examines how difficult it can be to separate persistent postural-perceptual dizziness from vestibular migraine — a reminder that "dizziness plus headache" still requires proper clinical assessment.

What this means in practice: the diagnostic framework is solid, the mechanism is actively being studied, and TCM approaches like acupressure are best understood as low-risk complementary support rather than a replacement for evaluated care.

When to See a Doctor

Seek emergency care immediately if dizziness or vertigo comes with any of the following — these can indicate a stroke or another serious event:

  • Double vision, slurred speech, facial drooping, or difficulty swallowing
  • Weakness, numbness, or clumsiness on one side of the body
  • Sudden difficulty walking or a loss of coordination
  • The worst headache of your life, or a headache that peaks within seconds ("thunderclap")
  • Confusion, fainting, or loss of consciousness
  • Dizziness with fever and a stiff neck

Book an appointment with a healthcare provider — and do not attempt to self-diagnose — if you experience:

  • New or recurring episodes of vertigo or dizziness, especially with head pain
  • Sudden hearing loss or a significant change in your hearing in one ear (this needs prompt assessment)
  • Dizziness following a head injury
  • Falls, near-falls, or a sense that your balance is progressively worsening
  • Episodes that are becoming more frequent, longer, or more severe despite your efforts
  • Any symptom that frightens you, or that is interfering with driving, working, or daily function

A proper assessment can distinguish vestibular migraine from BPPV, Ménière's disease, PPPD, and other causes — and that distinction genuinely changes what helps. Vestibular migraine is a clinical diagnosis, meaning it is identified by history rather than by a single scan or blood test. That is also why the pattern-tracking approach in TCM pairs so naturally with it: both traditions rely on careful observation of your own history over time.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  • Read the two symptoms as one event. Dizziness and head pain arriving together is a signature, not a coincidence — tracking them together reveals your real triggers.
  • Identify your pattern using the self-check table, then let it guide which foods, points, and habits you favor.
  • Work the build phase. The hours before an episode are your highest-leverage window: consistent sleep, a real meal, no alcohol, lower intensity.
  • Make regularity the foundation — consistent sleep and wake times, meals at predictable hours, steady hydration, and no caffeine whiplash.
  • Use acupressure as gentle support — GB-20 and LV-3 for the storm pattern, PC-6 and ST-36 for the fog, KI-3 for the depleted. Sit or lie down first, and skip LI-4 and SP-6 if pregnant.
  • Protect the aftermath. The day after an episode is part of the episode. Refill rather than catch up.
  • Get properly assessed. A clinical diagnosis distinguishes vestibular migraine from its lookalikes, and that changes what helps.
