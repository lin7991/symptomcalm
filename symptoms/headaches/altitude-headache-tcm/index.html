Altitude Headache: Why Your Head Hurts Above 2,500 Meters

Symptom Guide · 10 min read

You flew in yesterday. Or you drove up from the valley, or you hiked the last 900 meters of switchbacks with a pack that somehow weighed twice what it did at home. This morning you wake with a headache that wasn't there before — a dull, heavy band across your forehead, a pressure sitting behind your eyes, or a throb at the back of your skull that gets noticeably worse every time you stand up, bend over, or climb the stairs to breakfast.

You drink water. It helps a little. You take something for the pain. It helps for a few hours, then fades back. Because the problem isn't only the pain — it's that you are doing business in air that holds meaningfully fewer oxygen molecules per breath than the air you live in, and your head is the loudest room in the house when supply drops.

Altitude headache is the most common symptom of going high. In a survey of 150 trekkers staying above 2,500 m in the Himalayas, headache was reported by almost no one at 2,500–3,000 m — and by roughly half of everyone at 4,500–5,000 m. It is the cardinal symptom of acute mountain sickness (AMS), and it has its own entry in the International Classification of Headache Disorders.

In this guide: what altitude actually does to your head, how Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) reads the pattern behind the pain — including what the location of your headache may be telling you — and what you can practically do before, during, and after the climb.

Before we dive in: This article focuses specifically on the headache — where it sits, why it behaves the way it does, and what may support comfort at elevation. For the wider picture of altitude illness (the full body syndrome, acclimatization timelines, and preparation), see our companion guide to altitude sickness from a TCM perspective. Why Your Head Hurts Above 2,500 Meters

The mechanism starts with physics, not with your fitness. As you gain altitude, atmospheric pressure falls. The percentage of oxygen in the air stays roughly the same, but the pressure pushing those molecules into your lungs drops — so each breath delivers fewer oxygen molecules to your blood. At 3,500 m you are effectively breathing air that behaves like a lower-oxygen mixture. Your body notices within minutes.

The medical name for what follows is high-altitude headache, and it has formal diagnostic criteria. The International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) describes it as a headache that develops in relation to ascent above 2,500 m, typically:

  • Bilateral — on both sides of the head rather than one
  • Mild to moderate in intensity
  • Worse with exertion, movement, straining, coughing, or bending down
  • Improving or resolving within about 24 hours of descending below 2,500 m

That last criterion is the giveaway. If your headache melts away on the drive back down to the valley, you've just run a very convincing diagnostic experiment on yourself.

The proposed mechanism is worth understanding because it explains why the pain behaves the way it does. Low oxygen causes the cerebral arteries to dilate — blood flow to the brain actually increases in the early hours at altitude, as the brain works to keep oxygen delivery steady. That dilation stretches and sensitizes pain-sensitive tissue around the vessels, and signaling molecules such as nitric oxide and CGRP appear to be involved. Some researchers also point to mild fluid shifts and swelling in the brain. The result is a headache that pulses with your heartbeat, worsens when you bear down, and feels like pressure from the inside — because, in a sense, it is.

Risk factors reported in the medical literature are revealing: a personal history of migraine, lower oxygen saturation, high perceived exertion, drinking less than two liters of fluid in 24 hours, poor sleep, a high resting heart rate, and elevated anxiety scores. Notice that only two of those — saturation and heart rate — are things you can't influence. The rest are habits and states.

The TCM View: Clear Yang and the Thin Air Problem

TCM has no word for barometric pressure, but it has spent a very long time thinking about what happens when energy and blood fail to reach the head.

In classical theory, the head is described as the meeting place of all the Yang channels — the body's brightest, lightest, most upward-moving energies. Health at the top of the body depends on Clear Yang rising: clean Qi and Blood ascending to nourish the brain, the eyes, and the senses. When Clear Yang rises properly, the head feels light, the mind feels sharp, and the senses feel open. When it doesn't, the head feels heavy and foggy — like a room where the ceiling lights have dimmed.

🏔 Think of a building with a water-pressure problem
Your body is a tall building. Qi is the pressure in the pipes. The head is the penthouse — the highest, most demanding floor, and the last one to receive supply when pressure drops. At sea level, the system has plenty of slack, so the penthouse never notices a bad night's sleep or a skipped lunch. Take the building up to 4,000 meters and everything changes at once: the pump (Lung Qi) has less to work with, the pipes (channels) are constricting in the cold, and the fluid reserves are draining faster in dry air. The pressure gauge drops, and the penthouse complains first. That complaint is your headache.

Three forces are working against you at once, and TCM names all three:

  • Lung Qi and Gathering Qi (Zong Qi) — the Lungs draw in Air Qi and combine it with the food-derived Qi from the Spleen. That combination, sometimes translated as Gathering Qi, is what powers the chest and pushes clean Qi upward to the head. Thin air means less raw material going into that mixture.
  • Cold and Wind at the surface — altitude is cold, dry, and almost always windy. In TCM, Cold contracts and Wind moves and penetrates. Together they bind the outer channels, particularly along the back of the neck and skull, and restrict the movement of Qi and Blood through them.
  • Fluid depletion (Jin-Ye) — you lose water fast at altitude: cold dry air, faster breathing, higher urine output as your body adjusts. Blood that is thicker and slower does not nourish the head as well, and in TCM theory long enough stagnation becomes stasis.

This is the same set of problems the body faces in dehydration and in overexertion — which is not a coincidence. Altitude simply applies all of them simultaneously, at a pressure your system hasn't adapted to yet.

Where the Pain Sits: A Location Map

One of the most practical tools in TCM headache theory is location. Rather than reading "headache" as one thing, TCM asks which channel territory is hurting. At altitude this is unusually useful, because the location often points toward which part of your system is under the most strain.

Read your own headache against this table the next time you go high. It won't tell you whether you should turn around — only the red flags in the section below do that — but it will tell you which of the following patterns fits you best, and therefore which points and habits are worth your attention.

Three Patterns Behind an Altitude Headache

Most altitude headaches cluster into one of three pictures. Many people recognize themselves in two at once — that's normal, and the practical steps overlap.

Pattern 1: Clear Yang Not Rising — The Dimmed Penthouse

This is the "heavy head" headache. Because the body's upbearing function depends on Qi produced from food and air, and because both are compromised at altitude, clean Qi fails to reach the top. The presentation is dullness rather than drama.

  • Heavy, band-like pressure across the whole head; foggy thinking; difficulty concentrating
  • Worse with fatigue and after exertion; better lying down, worse standing and moving
  • Accompanied by deep tiredness, poor appetite, slight nausea, cold hands or feet
  • Often the first sign that acclimatization is not keeping up with your ascent rate

In TCM terms the pivot here is the Spleen and Stomach — the organs that raise clear Qi and lower turbid Qi. This is why appetite and digestion matter so much at altitude, and why heavy, greasy, or alcohol-loaded meals tend to make the head worse the next morning.

Pattern 2: Wind-Cold Binding the Surface — The Frozen Pipes

Here the pain sits at the back of the head, the skull base, and the neck. Cold contracts; Wind drives in. Together they tighten the superficial channels, and Qi and Blood movement through the neck and back of the skull slows. This is the pattern that feels like the mountain itself has grabbed you by the collar.

  • Pain starting at the back of the head and neck, sometimes spreading over the crown or behind the eyes
  • Stiff neck and shoulders; discomfort turning the head; sensitivity to cold and wind
  • Chills, blocked or runny nose, no sweating, a general feeling of being "seized up"
  • Worse in wind, in the early morning, or after being underdressed on a summit push

Practically, this is the pattern most responsive to warmth: covering the neck, keeping the head and upper back insulated, avoiding wind on a sweaty body, and gentle heat over the upper back and neck. The traditional logic is straightforward — Cold was what bound the channels, so warmth is what loosens them.

Pattern 3: Blood Stasis from Thin, Slow Flow — The Sludge in the Line

The third pattern shows up later in the trip, or in people who already get migraines. Low fluids, cold, and reduced oxygen all make Blood move less freely. In TCM theory, when flow gets sluggish enough, it begins to pool — and pooled, fixed Blood produces a pain that is sharp, throbbing, and stubbornly in one place.

  • One-sided or fixed, throbbing pain that pulses with your heartbeat
  • Worse when bending forward, coughing, or climbing stairs; worse at night or on waking
  • May persist for a day or two after descending, rather than clearing quickly
  • Common in people with a personal or family history of migraine, and in those who got badly dehydrated

This is the pattern that most rewards the boring fundamentals: steady hydration, warmth, real rest, and avoiding alcohol — all of which keep blood volume and circulation where they need to be.

Before You Go Up: Building Your Altitude Reserve

TCM and modern altitude medicine agree on the single most important variable: how fast you ascend. No point, herb, or habit compensates for going too high too fast. Within that constraint, these preparations are traditionally used to build the reserves that altitude draws down.

  • Arrive rested, not depleted. Starting a trip after three short nights undoes your margin before you begin. In TCM terms, you are climbing on an already-low Kidney reserve and can't afford the first 24 hours of adaptation.
  • Build Blood-supporting nutrition the week before. Cooked, warm, easy-to-digest meals with enough protein and iron-rich foods — the traditional logic is that nourished Blood carries oxygen better and copes better with thin air.
  • Go easy on alcohol in the days before. Alcohol is drying and adds heat, and it disrupts the sleep your body needs for acclimatization. Most altitude guidance advises avoiding it entirely for the first 24–48 hours at height.
  • Stage your ascent if the itinerary allows. Sleeping one night at an intermediate altitude is the closest thing to a universal rule in altitude medicine — and it maps neatly onto the TCM idea of giving the Spleen and Lung time to rebuild Qi before demand increases again.
  • Train the breath, not just the legs. Slow, diaphragmatic breathing with a longer exhale is a simple practice many people find steadies them in thin air. In TCM theory it supports the descending function of Lung Qi instead of letting energy crowd the chest and head.
  • Pack for warmth and wind. A warm hat, a neck gaiter, and a windproof layer are TCM-relevant equipment, not just comfort items. The back of the neck is precisely where Pattern 2 takes hold.

The First 24 Hours at Altitude: What May Help

The first day at a new altitude is when the head is most likely to complain. These are the measures traditionally used and most commonly recommended for that window.

  • Drink steadily, not in bursts. Aim for pale-yellow urine rather than a specific number of liters. Cold, dry air and faster breathing mean you lose more than you feel.
  • Take your time with food. Small, warm, cooked meals — soups, stews, rice, noodles, oats, eggs — are easier on a system whose digestive capacity has temporarily dropped. Traditional advice often favors warm ginger, which is warming and settling.
  • Move at conversation pace. Exertion is a documented aggravator of altitude headache. If you can't speak a full sentence, you're working harder than your oxygen supply can comfortably support.
  • Take the daytime nap seriously — and keep it short. Rest helps. Long daytime sleeps, however, often worsen breathing patterns at altitude and can leave you foggier; many travelers find short rests more helpful than deep afternoon sleeps.
  • Keep the head and neck warm at all times. Especially in the first two days and especially after any sweating. Cooling down after exertion is a common way to invite the Wind-Cold pattern.
  • Skip alcohol and heavy sedatives. Both blunt the breathing drive during sleep, and both are discouraged in altitude guidance for the first day or two.
  • Descend if the picture is escalating. This is medical common sense, not a TCM recommendation, and it takes precedence over everything above.

Acupressure Points for Altitude Headache

Acupressure is the self-applied version of a practice traditionally used for headaches and for altitude discomfort. It's gentle, portable, and requires no equipment — useful when you're on a trail or in a lodge at 4,000 m. Press each point with steady, small circles or a gentle kneading pressure for 1–2 minutes per side. Expect a distinct, slightly deep sensation — not sharp pain.

The Five Points

  • GB-20 (Fengchi, "Wind Pool") — in the two hollows at the base of the skull, between the neck muscles and the skull. The classic point for Wind-Cold headaches and neck stiffness, which makes it the first choice for Pattern 2. Press upward and slightly toward the opposite eye.
  • EX-HN5 (Taiyang) — in the temple hollows, about one finger-width behind the midpoint between the eyebrow and the outer eye corner. Traditionally used for one-sided and throbbing head pain — a natural fit for Pattern 3.
  • LI-4 (Hegu) — in the fleshy web between the thumb and index finger, on the back of the hand. One of the most widely used points for head and face pain. Press toward the index finger bone. Note: traditionally avoided during pregnancy.
  • DU-20 (Baihui) — on the top of the head, in the midline, roughly where a line from the ear tips meets the crown. Traditionally used to "raise" clear Qi — the reasoning behind its use when the head feels heavy and foggy (Pattern 1). Press with a flat fingertip, no digging.
  • PC-6 (Neiguan) — two finger-widths up the inner forearm from the wrist crease, between the two prominent tendons. Traditionally used for nausea, chest tightness, and queasiness — often the most welcome point when altitude has upset your stomach as well as your head.

If you only have thirty seconds on a cold ridge, cover the neck first (GB-20 and the surrounding muscles), then LI-4. Warm hands work better than cold ones, and pressing through two layers of clothing is fine — warmth and pressure both matter.

Practical Takeaways: A Cold-Day Checklist
  • Cover the back of the neck before you need to — a gaiter or scarf is the single most useful item for the Wind-Cold pattern.
  • Hydrate below thirst — sip steadily through the day rather than catching up at dinner.
  • Eat warm, small, and cooked — soup, noodles, oats, rice. Avoid alcohol and heavy fried meals for the first 24–48 hours.
  • Press GB-20 and LI-4 for 1–2 minutes per side, with warm hands, at the first sign of a stiff neck or a building headache.
  • Slow your ascent, not your breathing. Exertion aggravates altitude headache; a slower schedule is the most reliable lever you have.
  • Know the line where TCM stops and medicine starts. Confusion, vomiting, or unsteadiness means descend and seek medical help — not press another point.

What Research Says

Modern research on altitude headache is a useful bridge between two very different ways of reading the same problem. From a Western perspective:

  • Altitude headache has formal criteria. The International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) recognizes high-altitude headache as developing in relation to ascent above 2,500 m, typically bilateral, mild to moderate, and aggravated by exertion, straining, or bending — and resolving within about 24 hours of descent below 2,500 m.
  • Inflammation appears to play a role. A randomized controlled trial published in Annals of Emergency Medicine (Lipman et al., 2012) found that ibuprofen taken before and during ascent reduced the incidence of acute mountain sickness compared with placebo — 43% versus 69% — which supports the view that inflammatory signaling contributes to the problem rather than oxygen shortage alone.
  • A second field trial reached a similar conclusion about the headache itself. The ASCENT trial on the Everest approach reported lower incidence of high-altitude headache in the ibuprofen group in the analysis that included all randomized participants (42% versus 61%) — though notably, the effect did not hold in the smaller group that completed the full protocol, a limitation the authors discussed openly.
  • The mechanism involves blood flow, not just oxygen. Reviews of hypoxic headache mechanisms describe increased cerebral blood flow and dilation of cerebral arteries at altitude, with sensitization of pain-sensitive perivascular tissue involving nitric oxide and CGRP pathways — a close parallel to the TCM idea of rising, unbounded Yang and congested head channels.
  • Acupoint stimulation is being studied at altitude. A small single-blind randomized trial of auricular acupressure before ascent reported reductions in AMS symptoms including headache, insomnia, and fatigue. Separately, researchers designing a randomized trial for acute mountain sickness selected exactly the kind of points used above — PC-6, LI-4, Taiyang (EX-HN5), and Ren-12 — for a 48-hour acupoint protocol. The evidence base remains small, and larger trials are needed.
  • Risk factors line up with the TCM reading. Reported risk factors include dehydration (under two liters of fluid in 24 hours), poor sleep, high perceived exertion, and higher anxiety scores — precisely the states TCM would describe as depleted fluids, unsettled Shen, and overworked Qi.
What this means for you: Think of acupressure and warmth-based habits as comfort measures that work alongside sound altitude practice — staged ascent, hydration, rest, and warmth — not as substitutes for them. And never think of them as substitutes for descent when descent is what the situation requires.

When to See a Doctor

Altitude illness can become a medical emergency, and headache is often the first warning. The information in this article is educational and is not a substitute for medical assessment. If you are at altitude and any of the following are true, seek medical help immediately — and descend if you can do so safely:

  • Confusion, disorientation, or difficulty thinking clearly — a sign of high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE)
  • Loss of balance or unsteadiness, such as being unable to walk a straight line heel-to-toe
  • Breathlessness at rest, a persistent cough, gurgling or rattling breathing, or bluish lips or fingertips — signs of high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE)
  • Vomiting that won't stop, or being unable to keep fluids down
  • Severe headache that does not respond to pain relief, or that keeps worsening despite rest, fluids, and descending
  • Drowsiness, extreme lethargy, or difficulty being woken
  • Chest pain, palpitations, or fainting

Also see a doctor promptly if a headache at altitude comes with fever and a stiff neck, follows a head injury (including a fall on the trail), or arrives with weakness, numbness, slurred speech, or vision changes. And if a headache persists for more than a day or two after you have returned to low altitude, have it evaluated rather than assumed to be altitude-related.

Do not attempt to manage AMS or HACE with acupressure, herbs, or rest alone. The definitive measure in worsening altitude illness is descent, and descent should not be delayed. People with heart or lung conditions, pregnant travelers, and anyone planning to go above 3,500 m should discuss the trip with a healthcare provider in advance.

Closing: The Same Mountain, Two Maps

Western altitude medicine tells you what thin air does: it dilates the vessels, shifts the fluids, sensitizes the pain pathways, and punishes fast ascents. TCM tells you how that lands in a body already carrying its own history of fatigue, cold, tension, and hydration — and it offers a way to read the location of your pain and respond to it in the moment.

Neither map replaces the other, and at altitude the practical advice converges more than it diverges: go slow, stay warm, drink steadily, eat lightly, rest properly, and turn around when the mountain says so.
