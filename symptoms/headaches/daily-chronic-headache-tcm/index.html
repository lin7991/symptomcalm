Chronic Daily Headache and TCM: When Head Pain Becomes Constant

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

Somewhere along the way, your headache stopped visiting and moved in. You wake up with a dull pressure already in place, carry it through meetings and meals and conversations, and go to bed with it still there — softer, maybe, but never gone. You have stopped mentioning it, because describing the same pain every single day is exhausting in its own right. If this sounds familiar, you are not imagining it, and you are not alone.

If you have headaches on 15 or more days per month for three months or longer, Western medicine calls this chronic daily headache (CDH). It is not one specific type of headache — it is a state that any headache pattern can fall into. Roughly one in twenty adults lives with it.

When Headache Moves In: The Western Picture

Chronic daily headache is an umbrella term. Underneath it sit chronic tension-type headache, chronic migraine, and new daily persistent headache — a headache that starts one day and simply never leaves. What unites them is frequency: 15 or more headache days per month, for at least three months. That frequency is what makes CDH feel so different from an ordinary headache. An ordinary headache is an event. CDH is a backdrop.

How does an episodic headache become chronic? In most cases, it is a slow process rather than a single dramatic event. Repeated acute episodes pile up alongside risk factors — stress, disrupted sleep, caffeine swings, and, in a large share of cases, the overuse of acute pain medication. Over time, the pain system itself changes. Researchers call this central sensitisation: the nervous system's alarm becomes louder and easier to trigger. Things that never used to hurt — a tight hat, screen glare, a mildly stiff neck — now register as pain. It is as if the volume knob on the pain alarm has gotten stuck at eight, and the wiring behind it is re-calibrated to expect trouble.

Two things matter here. First, because the alarm system is now part of the problem, CDH deserves a proper medical workup — it is a diagnosis made after other causes are ruled out, and a doctor should always be involved. Second, if you are taking acute pain medication on more than ten days per month, that pattern itself can be driving the frequency. We cover that cycle in detail in our guide to rebound and medication-overuse headaches.

The TCM Read: An Alarm That Has Learned to Stay On

In TCM theory, a headache is a signal. The head is the highest point of the body, and getting Qi and Blood up to it — and circulating them smoothly once they arrive — is a constant job. When that circulation is blocked or under-supplied, the head sends a pain signal. That framework is easy enough to accept for an occasional headache. The harder question is the one CDH forces: what does it mean when the signal never stops?

Think of it like a smoke detector that has learned to stay on. A beeping alarm during an actual fire is useful information. A detector that beeps all day every day, in a house with no smoke, has stopped being information — it has become the problem. But unplugging it (silencing the pain) does not fix whatever first made it sensitive. TCM asks a different question: what is keeping the alarm circuit live?

The TCM answer, for long-standing pain, is usually stagnation that has congealed. There is a classical idea in TCM that captures this: jiu bing ru luo (久病入络) — long-standing illness enters the network. What starts as a functional slowdown — Qi moving sluggishly, like a garden hose with a kink — can, over months and years, become more solid. Qi stagnation slows Blood; slow Blood settles and thickens; and in TCM theory that settled, sluggish state is called Blood Stasis. The traffic jam stops being an event and becomes part of the road. For a full introduction to this concept, see our TCM Basics article on Blood Stasis.

TCM does not count headache days the way a diary does. Instead, it reads the quality of constant pain — where it sits, how it feels, when it peaks, what makes it worse. In that reading, three patterns commonly underlie chronic daily headache. Most people will recognise themselves in one of them, or in a blend.

Pattern 1: Blood Stasis — The Stuck River

Blood Stasis (The Stuck River)

You might have this if: The pain has a fixed home — one temple, one spot on the crown, one side of the forehead — and it has lived there for months. It feels like a heavy, boring pressure rather than a sharp attack, sometimes with a stabbing jab buried inside the constant ache. It is worse at night or at rest, and it barely responds to the usual remedies. You may have a history of head injury, decades of desk-bound tension, or long-standing stress behind it.

TCM view: In TCM theory, this is the pattern that has gone structural. The Qi stagnation that once caused episodic headaches has, over time, slowed the Blood itself — like a river current weakening until silt settles into a permanent bank. The pain is fixed because the stagnation is fixed. The classic TCM saying for chronic pain of any location is "where there is fixed pain, there is Blood Stasis" — and the head is a common first address.

What may support this pattern: Movement is the single most useful daily lever — walking, gentle cardio, anything that keeps the body's circulation honest. Warmth on the neck and shoulders helps Blood move. Acupressure at GB-20 (Fengchi / 风池), the hollows at the base of the skull, and LV-3 (Taichong / 太冲), on the top of the foot, are traditionally used to promote flow. Prolonged stillness — hours of sitting without a break — works against this pattern more than most people realise.

Pattern 2: Liver Qi Stagnation — The Tightened Spring

Liver Qi Stagnation (The Tightened Spring)

You might have this if: The pain wraps the whole head like a band, or collects at the temples and the back of the skull. Your jaw is often clenched without your noticing; your shoulders sit somewhere around your ears. The headache is present on calm days and worse on frustrating days — and it has a habit of never fully releasing overnight, so you wake up with yesterday's tension still half-wound.

TCM view: In TCM theory, the Liver system governs the smooth, even movement of Qi — the body's traffic controller. When stress, deadlines, and held-in frustration arrive faster than the system can process them, Qi gets stuck. The Liver channel runs up through the neck, jaw, and temples, which is exactly where a stagnation headache lives. Unlike the episodic version, where a good night's sleep resets things, the daily-grind version of this pattern never gets a full reset — the spring rewinds a little tighter every day.

What may support this pattern: Anything that gives the tension somewhere to go. Acupressure at GB-21 (Jianjing / 肩井), midway between the neck and shoulder, and LV-3 (Taichong / 太冲) are traditionally used for this pattern. A daily extended-exhale breathing practice — in for four counts, out for six to eight — directly counteracts the clenched, held-in state. Micro-breaks from the desk, a short walk after work, and an honest outlet for frustration all matter more than a perfect stretching routine. For the broader picture of how TCM reads tension-type headaches, see our tension headache guide; if your pain flares with anger or feels hot and pounding rather than tight, the Liver Fire pattern may be a better match.

Pattern 3: Dampness — The Fog That Never Lifts

Dampness (The Fog That Never Lifts)

You might have this if: The headache feels less like pain and more like weather — a heavy, wrapped, cotton-wool pressure that sits on the head and refuses to clear. It is worst in the morning, worse in humid weather, and it drags brain fog along with it: fuzzy thinking, sluggish digestion, a coated tongue, and a body that feels heavier than it should. Rich, oily, or sugary meals make the next morning worse.

TCM view: In TCM theory, Dampness is a turbid heaviness produced when the Spleen system — the body's food processor — cannot keep up with what it is asked to process. Instead of clear nourishment, it generates a thick, cloudy residue that rises and settles in the head, like fog rolling into a valley. The hallmark of a Dampness headache is exactly what you feel: it is oppressive rather than sharp, and it sits there for days because fog does not drain on its own.

What may support this pattern: Lightening the load. Warm, cooked, easy-to-digest meals — soups, congee, steamed vegetables — are traditionally favoured over heavy, greasy, or sugary food. Acupressure at ST-40 (Fenglong / 丰隆), on the outer lower leg, is the classic point for transforming Dampness, alongside SP-9 (Yinlingquan / 阴陵泉), below the knee. Steady hydration and daily movement help the body keep fluids moving. For the deeper concept, see our TCM Basics article on Dampness in TCM.

Which Pattern Is Yours? A Quick Self-Check

Constant pain blurs together, so a simple grid can help you read your own headache the way a TCM practitioner would. Ask yourself about this week's headaches, not the ones you had years ago.

Signal Blood Stasis (Stuck River) Liver Qi (Tightened Spring) Dampness (Fog)
Where One fixed spot; same place every day Band around the head, temples, back of skull Whole head, heavy and diffuse
Quality Boring pressure, occasional stab Tight, squeezing, with neck and jaw tension Oppressive, wrapped, cotton-wool
Timing Worse at night or at rest Builds through the day; worse under stress Worst in the morning; worse in humid weather
Company it keeps History of injury or years of tension Clenched jaw, sighing, irritability Brain fog, sluggish digestion, coated tongue

Many people find they are a blend — Blood Stasis with a Liver Qi flavour, or Dampness layered over a stressed Liver. That is normal. Start with whichever column described the majority of your pain.

Daily Support: Small Levers, Pulled Every Day

Here is the honest part: a headache that has become constant did not arrive overnight, and it rarely leaves overnight either. In TCM thinking, chronic states respond to consistent, small levers — the daily equivalent of compound interest — rather than heroic one-off efforts. These are the levers most commonly suggested:

  • Move the body every day. Aerobic exercise — a brisk walk, a bike ride, swimming — is one of the most consistently supported habits for reducing headache frequency. A 2018 randomised trial in BMJ Open found that three sessions of aerobic exercise per week were associated with fewer headache days in people with tension-type headache. In TCM terms, movement is the simplest way to keep Qi and Blood from settling.
  • Warm the neck and shoulders. A warm shower, a heat pack, or a scarf on a cool day helps the muscles and channels of the upper back release. Blood Stasis and Liver Qi patterns both respond to warmth; it encourages circulation where it is most stuck.
  • A three-minute acupressure reset. Start with GB-20 (Fengchi / 风池) at the base of the skull for one minute of gentle, small circles, then spend two minutes on your pattern point: LV-3 for Liver Qi, ST-40 for Dampness, or LI-4 (Hegu / 合谷), between thumb and index finger, for general head and face pain. Note: LI-4 is traditionally considered contraindicated in pregnancy — skip it if you are pregnant.
  • Reset the breath daily. Two minutes of extended-exhale breathing — in for four counts, out for six to eight — is a direct signal to the nervous system that it is safe to power down. It is the cheapest lever on this list and often the most underused.
  • Steady sleep, fixed wake time. Sleep disruption is one of the most commonly reported triggers for chronic headache. The single most powerful habit is a consistent wake time — even on weekends — because the body's alarm system runs on rhythm.
  • Watch hydration and caffeine. Both under-hydration and caffeine swings can push a sensitive system over the edge. If you drink coffee, keep the timing and amount steady rather than bouncing between zero and three cups. See our guide to caffeine and headaches for the details.
  • Audit the painkiller pattern. If you are reaching for acute medication on more than ten days per month, that pattern itself may be driving the frequency. This must be discussed with a doctor — do not change medication habits on your own. Our rebound headache guide walks through why this matters and how to approach it safely.

What Research Says

Chronic daily headache has a growing research base, and several findings line up with the TCM framework:

  • It is common. A classic epidemiological study in Headache (1999) found chronic daily headache in roughly 4.7% of the general population — about one in twenty adults, matching the estimate above.
  • The alarm really does get louder. Central sensitisation — the nervous system amplifying pain signals — is now a well-established mechanism in chronic pain research (Woolf, Pain, 2011). It is a precise scientific description of what TCM calls a channel that has become stuck: the system stops being a neutral messenger and starts generating signal on its own.
  • Acupuncture has a solid evidence base for chronic headache. A 2016 Cochrane review of acupuncture for tension-type headache found that acupuncture was associated with reduced headache frequency compared with sham acupuncture, including in chronic forms. This is one of the stronger areas of TCM research.
  • Exercise helps. The 2018 BMJ Open trial mentioned above found aerobic exercise reduced headache days in tension-type headache — an effect consistent with the TCM emphasis on keeping Qi and Blood moving.
  • Self-administered acupressure shows promise. Small trials have found that daily self-administered acupressure over several weeks is associated with reduced headache frequency and intensity in people with chronic headache, though larger studies are needed.
  • Stress is the most commonly reported trigger. Across studies, psychological stress is the single most frequently reported trigger for chronic headache — which aligns with the Liver Qi stagnation pattern's central role.

As with most TCM research, the evidence is promising but not definitive, and studies vary in quality. TCM approaches are best used as a complementary layer alongside — never instead of — conventional care.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ Chronic daily headache always deserves a proper medical evaluation, because the diagnosis is made by ruling out other causes. Seek emergency care immediately for: the worst headache of your life (sometimes called a thunderclap headache), headache with fever and stiff neck, headache after a head injury, or headache accompanied by weakness, numbness, vision changes, difficulty speaking, or confusion. Make an appointment promptly if you are over 50 and have developed a new type of headache, if your headache wakes you from sleep or is worst on waking with vomiting, or if you have been taking acute pain medication on more than ten days per month — that pattern needs a doctor-led plan, not a do-it-yourself withdrawal.

Practical Takeaways

  1. Frequency is the story. If pain shows up on 15+ days a month for three months, you are dealing with a chronic state, not a run of bad luck — and it deserves a medical workup.
  2. Read the quality, not just the count. Fixed and boring points to Blood Stasis; tight and stress-linked points to Liver Qi stagnation; heavy and foggy points to Dampness. Your self-check grid is the map.
  3. Chronic pain responds to daily levers. Movement, warmth, a three-minute acupressure reset, breath work, steady sleep, and steady caffeine — pulled every day, these are the TCM version of compound interest.
  4. Check the medication pattern. If acute painkillers are a near-daily habit, that may be part of the mechanism — talk to your doctor about it, and see our rebound headache guide.
  5. Complementary means alongside. TCM offers a useful lens and supportive practices, but it works with conventional care, not instead of it.
