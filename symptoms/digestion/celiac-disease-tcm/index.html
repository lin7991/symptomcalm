Celiac Disease and Gluten Sensitivity: A TCM View of the Spleen

Symptom Guide · 8 min read

You finally figured it out. After years of bloating, brain fog, fatigue, and digestive chaos that doctors kept calling "stress" or "IBS," someone mentioned gluten. You got tested. The results came back. Celiac disease — or maybe just a gluten sensitivity your body has been protesting about for years.

So you cut out the bread, the pasta, the soy sauce you never suspected. And here's the part nobody warned you about: even on a strict gluten-free diet, your digestion can still feel fragile. Newly diagnosed people often tell us the same story — they removed the trigger, but their gut still feels raw, heavy, and unpredictable. They healed the obvious problem and are now staring at a longer, slower one.

Traditional Chinese Medicine has spent more than two thousand years mapping exactly this kind of digestive vulnerability. Its view of the Spleen — the organ system TCM treats as your body's food processor and power plant — offers a surprisingly practical frame for life after gluten. Not as a replacement for the gluten-free diet. As a way to understand what's happening underneath it.

Before we dive in: This article focuses on how gluten sensitivity and celiac disease interact with the Spleen, and what supportive self-care looks like within a gluten-free lifestyle. For the broader framework, see our guides on the Spleen in TCM, Dampness in TCM, and Defensive Qi (Wei Qi). If dairy is also part of your story, our article on lactose intolerance through a TCM lens covers a related — but different — sensitivity pattern.

What Celiac Disease Actually Is: The Immune Misidentification

Before the TCM view makes sense, it helps to be precise about what celiac disease is — because it's not an allergy, and it's not a typical intolerance.

In celiac disease, the immune system mistakes gluten — a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye — for a threat. Every time gluten is present, the body's defenses mount an attack on the small intestine's lining, where the villi (the tiny finger-like projections that absorb nutrients) get damaged and flattened. That's why celiac is classified as an autoimmune condition: the trigger is external, but the damage is caused by the body's own immune response.

Non-celiac gluten sensitivity (NCGS) is different. People with NCGS experience real digestive and systemic symptoms from gluten, but without the same immune attack and intestinal damage. The symptoms are real; the mechanism is less clear-cut.

Here's the image TCM practitioners often reach for: think of your immune system as a security team, and gluten as a delivery truck that keeps pulling up to the loading dock. In most bodies, the guard waves it through — "just wheat, nothing to see here." In celiac disease, the security team has a file on that truck. The alarm sounds, the response team rushes in, and in the process of "defending" the building, the loading dock itself (the intestinal lining) takes the damage. The guard isn't broken; the threat assessment is.

This distinction matters because it shapes the whole approach: with celiac, gluten is genuinely off the table — medically, not just preference-wise. TCM never argues with that. What TCM adds is a different question: why does one person's body mount this response, and how do you support the system while the trigger is removed?

The TCM View: A Vulnerable Spleen and a Triggered Defense

In TCM theory, digestion is the domain of the Spleen — the system that transforms food and drink into Qi (the body's operating energy) and Blood. If the Stomach is the cooking pot, the Spleen is the stove: it provides the warmth and processing power that turns raw ingredients into fuel your body can actually use.

When Spleen Qi is strong, digestion is robust. Foods are processed cleanly, nutrients get distributed, and the gut is resilient — things pass through without much drama. When Spleen Qi is deficient or sluggish, the digestive environment becomes what TCM calls "thin-skinned": more reactive, more easily overwhelmed, slower to recover. In this view, gluten isn't the cause of the sensitivity so much as the test — the stimulus that exposes how much processing capacity the system has at that moment.

Two people eat the same sandwich. One body processes the gluten without incident; the other's defense system flags it and mounts a response. TCM's answer to why: the terrain differs, not just the trigger. A well-supported Spleen is like well-drained soil — rain passes through and settles. A depleted Spleen is waterlogged ground — the same rain pools, lingers, and breeds trouble.

Wheat and Dampness: The Traditional View

There's a specific traditional observation worth knowing: in Chinese dietary therapy, wheat and wheat products — especially refined ones like white bread and pasta — are traditionally described as Dampness-forming when eaten in excess. Dampness in TCM is exactly what it sounds like: an internal "humidity" that makes the body feel heavy, sluggish, foggy, and congested. Think of walking around in waterlogged clothes — everything becomes effort.

This is why so many gluten-sensitive people describe their symptoms as more than digestive: heavy limbs, fuzzy thinking, a sense of being "stuck in mud." In TCM theory, that's the Spleen failing to transform and transport — leaving partially processed material to accumulate as Dampness instead of becoming clean energy. The bloating, the post-meal fog, the sticky feeling — all classic Dampness signals.

Three Patterns That Often Show Up With Gluten Sensitivity

One of TCM's most useful habits is recognizing that the same trigger can express itself differently in different bodies. Here are three patterns commonly seen alongside gluten sensitivity and celiac disease. See which one sounds most like you — they call for slightly different support.

Pattern 1: Spleen Qi Deficiency with Dampness — The Sluggish Processor

This is the classic presentation: digestion that's not broken, just slow and heavy.

What you might notice:

  • Bloating and a heavy, full feeling after meals — even gluten-free ones
  • Loose, sticky stools, or a sensation of incomplete evacuation
  • Afternoon brain fog, especially following lunch
  • Heavy limbs and low energy that improves with movement
  • A thick, slightly greasy coating on the tongue in the morning
  • Craving for sweets or carbs that leaves you feeling worse, not better

What contributes: A history of irregular meals, cold and raw foods (iced drinks, salads, smoothies), too much sugar and refined starch — including the gluten-free kind — and insufficient rest. Many people who go gluten-free unknowingly replace bread with heavy GF substitutes that are even harder to process.

What may help: Warm, cooked, simple food becomes the backbone of support. Congee (rice porridge), soups, stews, and steamed vegetables reduce the digestive load dramatically. Ginger — fresh, in small amounts, in cooking or tea — is traditionally used to warm the Spleen and dry Dampness. And crucially: less processed gluten-free substitutes, not more. Whole-food carbs like rice, millet, sweet potato, and pumpkin are Spleen-friendly in a way that GF breads and pastas often aren't.

Pattern 2: Spleen-Kidney Yang Deficiency — The Depleted Long-Haul

This pattern shows up when sensitivity has been running for years, especially with undiagnosed celiac. It's the "I'm just tired all the time" presentation — and it reflects deeper depletion, not just sluggish digestion.

What you might notice:

  • Deep fatigue that rest doesn't fully fix
  • Cold hands and feet, or feeling cold easily
  • Loose stools, especially early in the morning
  • A dull ache or weakness in the lower back and knees
  • Low appetite, or food that "just sits there"
  • A pale, puffy-looking complexion

What contributes: Years of malabsorption quietly deplete the body's reserves. In TCM theory, the Kidneys store the body's deepest energy bank (Jing), and a chronically struggling Spleen draws on that bank to keep going. Undiagnosed or poorly managed celiac is a classic slow drain.

What may help: Warmth, rhythm, and rebuilding. Bone broths and slow-cooked soups are traditionally valued for supporting this pattern. Cooked root vegetables, oats (certified gluten-free), and small amounts of warming spices like cinnamon and cardamom fit the Spleen-Kidney framework. Regular meal times matter — the body needs predictable fuel. And gentler exercise (walking, qigong, tai chi) is traditionally preferred over high-intensity training while reserves are rebuilding.

Pattern 3: Blood and Yin Deficiency with Empty Heat — The Aftermath of Malabsorption

Because celiac damages the intestinal lining's ability to absorb nutrients, long-term cases can leave the body short on the raw materials it needs to make Blood and Yin — the body's nourishing, cooling resources. This is the "I eat well but I'm still depleted" pattern.

What you might notice:

  • Pale complexion, dull hair, brittle nails
  • Fatigue that feels different from Pattern 2 — more like "running on fumes"
  • Dry skin, dry eyes, or a dry mouth despite drinking water
  • Irritability, poor sleep, or feeling "wired but tired" in the evening
  • Dizziness when standing up quickly, or feeling light-headed between meals
  • In women: scanty or irregular periods

What contributes: In TCM theory, Blood is built from the nutrients the Spleen extracts from food. If the intestinal surface has been damaged for a long time, the supply line runs thin — regardless of how well you eat now. This is also where Western and Eastern medicine agree most directly: iron, B12, folate, and vitamin D deficiencies are common in celiac disease precisely because absorption is compromised.

What may help: Nutrient-dense, easily absorbed foods: well-cooked dark leafy greens, eggs, liver (if tolerated), dates, and black beans for iron support; bone broths and gelatinous foods for rebuilding. TCM traditionally pairs this with rest and earlier nights — Blood and Yin are rebuilt during deep sleep, not during busy days. And please: get your iron, B12, and vitamin D levels checked by your doctor. If they're low, food alone may not close the gap quickly enough — supplementation decisions belong with your healthcare team.

Living Gluten-Free the Spleen-Friendly Way

Here's where the TCM view becomes genuinely practical. A gluten-free diet removes the trigger — that's the medical foundation, and nothing here replaces it. But how you eat within that framework can support or further tax an already sensitive digestive system.

What Traditionally Supports the Spleen

  • Warm and cooked over cold and raw. Steamed, simmered, and roasted foods are easier to process. When digestion is fragile, a warm bowl of rice porridge or soup beats a cold smoothie every time.
  • Whole-food gluten-free carbs. Rice, millet, oats (certified GF), buckwheat, quinoa, sweet potato, pumpkin, and taro are Spleen-friendly staples. They're gentle, grounding, and don't pile on Dampness.
  • Small amounts of ginger, cinnamon, or cardamom in cooking — traditionally used to warm the digestive system.
  • Regular meal rhythm. The Spleen thrives on predictability. Skipping meals then eating a huge one late at night is hard on any digestive system, and harder on a sensitive one.
  • Sitting down to eat. In TCM, eating on the run is "eating with a distracted Spleen." Even ten unhurried minutes changes the digestive environment.

What to Go Easy On (Even If It's Gluten-Free)

  • Processed GF substitutes — GF breads, pastas, crackers, and snacks are often heavy on refined starch and additives, which can feed Dampness. Think of them as occasional, not foundational.
  • Iced drinks and cold beverages with meals. Traditional dietary therapy recommends warm or room-temperature drinks to avoid "putting out the digestive fire."
  • Excess sugar and alcohol — both are classic Dampness generators in TCM theory, and both are common hidden sources of digestive stress.
  • Large late-night meals, especially heavy or rich ones.

Acupressure Points for Digestive Support

Acupressure — pressing, not needles — is a gentle, self-directed way to support the digestive system between meals. In TCM theory, these points are like switches on the body's control panel: specific, accessible, and traditionally used for specific jobs.

ST-36 (Zusanli) — The Digestive Workhorse

Located about four finger-widths below the kneecap, just outside the shinbone. ST-36 is traditionally considered one of the most important points for digestive strength and overall energy. Press with steady, small circular motions for 1–2 minutes on each leg.

SP-6 (Sanyinjiao) — The Spleen's Support Point

Located about four finger-widths above the inner ankle bone, just behind the shinbone. SP-6 is traditionally used to support the Spleen and nourish Blood. Note: skip this point during pregnancy.

CV-12 (Zhongwan) — The Stomach's Control Center

Located midway between the belly button and the bottom of the breastbone. CV-12 is traditionally used for bloating, fullness, and sluggish digestion. Gentle clockwise circles with two fingers for 1–2 minutes, especially after meals.

ST-40 (Fenglong) — The Dampness Point

Located halfway between the knee and the ankle, about two finger-widths outside the shinbone. ST-40 is traditionally known as the point that helps "transform Dampness" — useful for that heavy, foggy, waterlogged feeling that often accompanies gluten sensitivity.

A note on consistency: Acupressure works like brushing your teeth, not like taking a pill. A few minutes most days, integrated into a routine (morning, before bed, or after meals), tends to be far more useful than an intensive session once a month. Press firmly but comfortably — you're looking for a deep, achy sensation, not sharp pain.

What Research Says

TCM's framework for gluten sensitivity developed over centuries of clinical observation. Several lines of modern research offer interesting parallels — and, importantly, confirm where the medical guardrails are.

Celiac diagnosis is well-defined. The current standard involves a blood test for specific antibodies (tTG-IgA) followed by an endoscopic biopsy of the small intestine. Some studies suggest the antibody test is highly sensitive for celiac disease — which is exactly why testing matters: celiac is a medical diagnosis, not something to self-diagnose from symptoms alone.

The gluten-free diet is the established management. Research consistently confirms that strict gluten avoidance allows the intestinal lining to recover in most people. But recovery is slow: studies of adults suggest that villi may take a year or more to fully recover, and some people continue to experience symptoms even on a strict diet. This matches the TCM observation that removing the trigger is necessary but not always sufficient — the system itself needs time and support to rebuild.

Nutrient deficiencies are common at diagnosis. Iron, folate, B12, vitamin D, and zinc deficiencies are frequently found in people with untreated celiac disease — a direct physiological echo of the TCM Blood and Yin deficiency pattern described above. This is a strong argument for getting levels checked rather than guessing.

Not everyone who reacts to gluten has celiac. A widely cited crossover study (Biesiekierski et al., 2011) found that many people with self-reported gluten sensitivity actually responded to a low-FODMAP diet, suggesting that for some, fermentable carbohydrates — not gluten itself — were the trigger. The takeaway: precise testing and food tracking matter, because the right support depends on the right diagnosis.

Acupuncture and digestive symptoms. Several systematic reviews have explored acupuncture for functional digestive complaints like bloating and discomfort after meals, with some studies suggesting symptom improvement. The evidence base is still developing, but it offers a preliminary scientific parallel to TCM's traditional use of acupressure and acupuncture for digestive support.

When to See a Doctor

If you suspect celiac disease, get tested before going gluten-free. This is important: the antibody test only works while gluten is still in your diet. If you remove gluten for weeks and then test, the result may be falsely negative — and you'll have made a major dietary change without the medical clarity you need.

See a healthcare provider promptly if you experience any of the following:

  • Unexplained weight loss, chronic diarrhea, or severe abdominal pain
  • Persistent fatigue, anemia, or nutrient deficiencies despite eating well
  • Children with poor growth, delayed development, or unexplained digestive problems
  • Symptoms that continue despite a strict gluten-free diet — this needs medical evaluation, not more dietary guesswork
  • New neurological symptoms such as tingling, numbness, or balance problems
  • A family history of celiac disease, especially with any digestive symptoms

Remember: TCM is a complementary perspective. For celiac disease, the gluten-free diet is the medically established foundation — TCM's role is to support the digestive system's recovery within that framework, not to replace it.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:
  • Get the diagnosis right first. If you suspect celiac, test before going gluten-free — the medical clarity shapes everything that follows.
  • Removing gluten is the foundation; rebuilding is the work. Strict gluten avoidance allows the gut to recover, but recovery is slow and the system needs support along the way.
  • Eat warm, cooked, and simple. Rice porridge, soups, stews, and steamed vegetables are the Spleen's comfort food. Go easy on processed gluten-free substitutes, iced drinks, sugar, and alcohol.
  • Identify your pattern. Sluggish and heavy suggests Spleen Qi Deficiency with Dampness; deep fatigue and cold suggests Spleen-Kidney Yang Deficiency; pale, dry, and depleted suggests Blood and Yin Deficiency. Each calls for slightly different support.
  • Support digestion daily. A few minutes of acupressure at ST-36, CV-12, and ST-40, plus regular meal times and unhurried eating, can meaningfully shift the digestive environment over weeks.
  • Check your nutrient levels with your doctor — iron, B12, folate, and vitamin D in particular. Long-term malabsorption can leave real gaps that food alone struggles to close.
