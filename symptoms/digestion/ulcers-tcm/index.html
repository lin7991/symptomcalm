Stomach Ulcers and Gastric Health: A TCM Approach to Spleen-Stomach Balance

Explore · 7 min read

You know that gnawing pain in your upper abdomen. It shows up a few hours after eating, or maybe it wakes you in the middle of the night. Antacids help briefly, but the burning comes back. Your doctor mentioned an endoscopy, gave you a prescription, and said “watch what you eat.”

But what if the ulcer isn't just an erosion in your stomach lining? In Traditional Chinese Medicine, an ulcer is the endpoint of a longer process — a breakdown in the Spleen-Stomach system that has been building for months or years before the first symptom appeared.

Stomach Ulcers: The Western View

From a medical perspective, most peptic ulcers are caused by either H. pylori infection or long-term use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Treatment typically involves antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) that reduce stomach acid, and protective agents for the stomach lining.

This approach works — ulcers do heal, recurrences drop. But many people continue to experience digestive discomfort, bloating, or sensitivity long after the ulcer itself has healed. The lining has mended, but something in the digestive system still feels fragile.

TCM asks a different question: what allowed the stomach lining to become vulnerable in the first place?

The TCM View: Why the Stomach Can't Protect Itself

In TCM theory, the Stomach is the body's “food processor” — it receives food and begins the process of breaking it down. The Spleen provides the energy (Qi) that powers this process. Together, the Spleen-Stomach system maintains a healthy digestive environment.

Think of the stomach lining as the inner surface of a cooking pot. Under normal conditions, a thin layer of fluid protects the pot's surface. But when the heat is too high (Stomach Heat), or the pot's material becomes thin and brittle (Stomach Yin Deficiency), or someone keeps banging the pot while it's cooking (Liver Qi attacking the Stomach), the surface becomes damaged.

The classic TCM patterns behind stomach ulcers include:

Stomach Yin Deficiency Burning pain, dry mouth/thirst, nighttime hunger, low appetite, dry stools The stomach's protective lining (Yin) is depleted — like a pot with worn non-stick coating
Spleen Qi Deficiency with Cold Dull ache that improves with warmth/pressure, fatigue, loose stools, cold hands The digestive “fire” is too low — food sits undigested and irritates a weakened lining
Liver Qi Invading the Stomach Pain that worsens with stress, sour reflux, belching, chest tightness, irritability Stress diverts Liver Qi sideways into the Stomach instead of flowing smoothly — like someone squeezing your stomach while you're digesting
Stomach Heat with Blood Stasis Sharp, fixed burning pain, dark or bloody stools, nausea, bitter taste Chronic inflammation (Heat) has damaged the microcirculation — blood pools instead of nourishing the tissue

Why Ulcers Recur: The Spleen-Stomach Axis

Many people who heal an ulcer with medication find it returns months or years later. From a TCM perspective, this isn't surprising. The ulcer is the symptom — the underlying vulnerability of the Spleen-Stomach system hasn't been addressed.

The Spleen in TCM is responsible for transforming food into Qi and Blood. When Spleen Qi is weak, less energy is available to maintain the stomach lining. It's like trying to keep a building in good repair with half the maintenance budget — the paint peels, the roof leaks, and eventually the structure itself shows wear.

Three common factors keep the Spleen-Stomach system compromised:

  • Irregular eating habits — skipping meals, eating late at night, or rushing through meals forces the stomach to work when its energy reserves are lowest
  • Chronic stress — ongoing stress overwhelms the Liver (responsible for smooth energy flow), which then “attacks” the Stomach, disrupting its function
  • Dietary patterns that deplete Yin — spicy, greasy, or dry-roasted foods gradually exhaust the Stomach's Yin, leaving the lining less protected

Dietary Principles for Gastric Support

In TCM theory, diet is the first line of defense for the Stomach. The goal isn't to “eat healthy” in a generic sense — it's to reduce the digestive burden while providing the building blocks for repair.

What TCM Traditionally Recommends for Stomach Support

🫕 Congee — The Gentle Stomach Builder

Congee (rice porridge) is considered one of the most stomach-friendly foods in TCM. Slow-cooked rice releases easily digestible starches and forms a protective coating over the stomach lining. A simple congee with a small piece of fresh ginger is the traditional first step for anyone recovering from gastric irritation.

🍠 Spleen-Supporting Foods

Sweet potato, pumpkin, oats, well-cooked root vegetables, and bone broth are traditionally used in TCM dietary therapy for Spleen Qi Deficiency. These foods are neutral or slightly warm in thermal nature and require minimal digestive effort.

🥣 Yin-Nourishing Foods

For Stomach Yin Deficiency patterns, foods that are moistening and cooling support the stomach lining. Okra, slippery elm, aloe vera gel, pear, and small amounts of honey can help replenish the protective mucous layer. These foods are traditionally prepared as warm, soft preparations — never iced or cold.

What to Avoid During Recovery

  • Raw and cold foods — these extinguish the digestive fire (Spleen Yang) and require extra energy to process
  • Spicy, fried, and greasy foods — they generate Stomach Heat, which further inflames an already compromised lining
  • Alcohol and caffeine — both are drying and warming, worsening Yin Deficiency patterns
  • Large meals late at night — the Stomach has lower energy in the evening, making digestion harder

Acupressure Points for Gastric Comfort

Acupressure is a gentle, non-invasive way to support the Spleen-Stomach system between meals. These points can be stimulated when pain flares or as a daily maintenance practice.

To stimulate a point: use your thumb or index finger to apply firm, steady pressure in a small circular motion for 1-2 minutes. Breathe deeply while pressing. The sensation should be a mild ache or tingling, never sharp pain.

What Research Suggests About TCM and Ulcers

While more research is needed, several studies have explored the interface between TCM concepts and gastric health:

  • A 2019 meta-analysis in Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine of 28 randomized trials (2,460 participants) found that certain TCM herbal formulas, when used alongside standard triple therapy for H. pylori eradication, were associated with higher eradication rates and reduced gastrointestinal side effects from antibiotics.
  • A 2021 study in Frontiers in Pharmacology reviewed the pharmacological mechanisms of several TCM herbs commonly used for gastric complaints, noting that certain compounds demonstrated protective effects on gastric mucosa in animal models — potentially through anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and mucosal-repair pathways.
  • Acupuncture research, including a 2017 review in Acupuncture in Medicine, suggests that needling of ST36 and CV12 may influence gastric acid secretion and mucosal blood flow, mechanisms relevant to both ulcer prevention and healing.
  • A 2022 review in Journal of Integrative Medicine noted that stress reduction techniques, including acupuncture and certain meditative movement practices, may support the autonomic nervous system balance that enables proper digestion — relevant to the Liver-Stomach connection in TCM.

These findings are preliminary and should not replace standard medical care for diagnosed stomach ulcers. However, they suggest that the TCM framework of Spleen-Stomach health may align with emerging research on gut mucosal integrity and the gut-brain axis.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ Seek Medical Attention If:

  • You have black, tarry, or bloody stools
  • You vomit blood or material that looks like coffee grounds
  • Your abdominal pain is sharp, sudden, or severe
  • Unexplained weight loss or appetite loss
  • You have difficulty swallowing or feel food getting stuck
  • Your symptoms persist despite over-the-counter remedies for more than 2 weeks
  • You have a history of peptic ulcers and are experiencing new or different symptoms

These may indicate bleeding, perforation, or other complications requiring immediate medical intervention. TCM approaches are complementary and should not delay emergency care.

Practical Takeaways

How to Support Your Spleen-Stomach System

  1. Start with congee — begin your day with warm rice porridge (congee) with a slice of ginger as a gentle, protective breakfast for the stomach lining
  2. Eat regular, moderate meals — avoid skipping meals or overeating; the Stomach works best on a predictable schedule with meals of moderate volume
  3. Eat warm, cooked foods — reduce raw, cold, and fried foods during recovery; warm soups, stews, and steamed vegetables require less digestive energy
  4. Address the stress-digestion connection — if your symptoms flare during stressful periods, consider daily acupressure on LV3 and PC6, or gentle movement practice to release Liver Qi stagnation
  5. Give the stomach time to heal — TCM approaches often work gradually; consistent dietary and lifestyle adjustments over 3-6 months may support lasting gastric resilience
