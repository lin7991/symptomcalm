Gastritis in TCM: Stomach Heat, Spleen Deficiency, and the Inflamed Lining

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

It starts as a gnawing ache an hour or two after meals — a hollow, burning discomfort just below your breastbone. You reach for an antacid. It helps for a while. Then it comes back. Your doctor runs some tests and tells you it's gastritis: inflammation of your stomach lining. The advice is usually the same — eat smaller meals, avoid spicy food, maybe a course of acid reducers. But if you're like many people, the pain keeps returning, and nobody quite explains why your lining keeps getting inflamed in the first place.

That question — why does inflammation persist? — is where a TCM perspective becomes genuinely useful. Gastritis isn't just "too much acid." In TCM theory, it's a story about a protective lining under attack, a repair crew that can't keep up, and the daily habits that feed both problems. This guide walks through the patterns behind gastritis and what you can do at home to support your stomach — alongside, never instead of, your doctor's care.

If you've been told you have an ulcer, our stomach ulcer guide covers the damaged-lining deep dive. This article focuses specifically on gastritis: the inflamed lining itself, before it becomes an ulcer.

Gastritis in the Western Picture: An Inflamed Lining

Gastritis is inflammation of the gastric mucosa — the protective layer that lines the inside of your stomach. Western medicine splits it into two broad categories:

  • Acute gastritis — sudden inflammation, often from a specific irritant: a heavy drinking episode, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs like ibuprofen or naproxen), food poisoning, or intense stress. It usually settles once the trigger is removed.
  • Chronic gastritis — long-standing, low-grade inflammation that can persist for months or years. Common drivers include H. pylori infection, bile reflux (bile backing up from the small intestine), long-term NSAID use, and ongoing stress. Many people with chronic gastritis feel nothing at all; others live with that nagging gnawing pain, bloating, and nausea.

The uncomfortable truth from the research: chronic gastritis is extremely common, and it tends to recur. Endoscopy studies consistently find it in a large share of people who report persistent upper-gut symptoms. Acid reducers can quiet the symptoms — but the inflammation itself is a tissue-level process, and it has a habit of returning when the underlying conditions don't change.

The TCM View: A Hull Whose Paint Is Wearing Thin

Think of your stomach as a steel ship and your stomach lining as the paint protecting its hull. Seawater — your stomach acid — is constantly pressing against that hull. As long as the paint is intact, the steel stays safe. The moment the paint gets scratched, seawater reaches bare metal, and corrosion begins to spread beneath the damaged patch.

That spreading corrosion is gastritis in this analogy. And in TCM theory, the paint job depends on two very different systems:

  • Stomach Heat is the abrasive friction — the irritants and internal heat that keep scraping the paint off the hull. Spicy food, alcohol, coffee on an empty stomach, certain medications, and unresolved emotional heat (especially frustration and suppressed anger) all add friction.
  • Spleen Qi is the shipyard crew — the system that manufactures and renews the protective coating. In TCM, the Spleen's job is to transform food into Qi and Blood and to keep the digestive environment nourished. When Spleen Qi is strong, the lining repairs itself efficiently. When it's weak, the paint dries thin and patchy, and the hull is always one small scratch away from corrosion.

This is why gastritis persists. It's rarely one problem. It's a loop: irritation damages the lining → the weakened Spleen can't fully repair it → the lining stays vulnerable → the next meal, stress episode, or glass of wine scrapes it again. Until something breaks the loop — by reducing the friction and supporting the repair crew — the inflammation has a reason to keep coming back.

The TCM Patterns Behind Gastritis

TCM doesn't see all gastritis as the same condition. The same diagnosis can feel completely different from one person to the next, and the pattern — not the label — determines what kind of support is traditionally used. Here are the four most common patterns:

Pattern 1: Stomach Heat — The Burning Friction

The defining sensation is burning: a hot, gnawing pain that may feel worse an hour or two after meals, a bitter taste, strong thirst, and sometimes sour regurgitation. The tongue often looks red, especially at the tip and edges, and may have a yellow coating. This pattern is fed by spicy food, alcohol, coffee, smoking, or emotional heat — sustained frustration that "bubbles upward" in TCM theory. The traditional approach focuses on cooling and soothing rather than adding more stimulation.

Pattern 2: Spleen Qi Deficiency — The Understaffed Repair Crew

Here the pain is more of a dull, heavy ache than a burn — worse after meals, often paired with bloating, fatigue, a feeling of fullness, and loose stools. The tongue may look pale with a white coating. This is the "crew too small for the job" pattern: digestion itself is underpowered, so even normal food becomes a burden, and the lining never quite gets rebuilt. It's common in people who skip meals, eat on the run, or have spent years on restrictive diets.

Pattern 3: Damp-Heat in the Stomach — The Humid Workshop

A heavy, stuffy, queasy discomfort — like the stomach is a hot, humid room with no ventilation. Symptoms include nausea, a sticky or bitter taste, poor appetite, a greasy tongue coating, and a sense of fullness that doesn't settle. This pattern tends to flare after rich, fried, or greasy food, heavy drinking, or late-night eating. The image is one of congestion: heat and moisture trapped together in the digestive workshop.

Pattern 4: Liver Qi Invading the Stomach — The Stress Flare

This pattern answers to your stress level. When you're under pressure, the discomfort appears or worsens; when you relax, it eases. It often comes with belching, chest tightness, irritability, and a sensation of food "getting stuck." In TCM, the Liver governs the smooth flow of Qi — and stress is like a hand squeezing the stomach mid-digestion. The same meal that sits fine on a calm day causes pain on a stressful one. This is one of the most common patterns for modern gastritis.

Self-Check: Which Pattern Fits You?

Signal Most Likely Pattern
Burning pain, bitter taste, thirst, worse after spicy food or coffee Stomach Heat
Dull ache, bloating, fatigue after meals, loose stools Spleen Qi Deficiency
Nausea, sticky taste, heavy fullness, greasy tongue coating Damp-Heat in the Stomach
Discomfort tracks your stress, belching, chest tightness, irritability Liver Qi Invading the Stomach

Many people have more than one pattern — a common combination is Stomach Heat on top of an underlying Spleen deficiency. The self-check isn't a diagnosis; it's a way to notice which dials are turned up in your body so you can adjust the right ones.

What You Can Do: Supporting the Lining Day to Day

These are traditionally used, food-and-lifestyle approaches — complementary support, not a replacement for medical care.

Cool the friction (especially for Heat patterns)

  • Cut back on the obvious irritants: spicy food, alcohol, coffee on an empty stomach, smoking, and very acidic drinks like citrus juice or soda.
  • Watch NSAIDs. If you regularly take ibuprofen or naproxen for headaches or joint pain, talk to your doctor — long-term use is one of the most common drivers of both acute and chronic gastritis.
  • Cooling, soothing foods traditionally used for Heat: cucumber, celery, watermelon, banana, pear, and lightly cooked leafy greens. Keep them cooked and warm — raw, cold salads can be hard on an inflamed stomach.

Feed the repair crew (especially for Spleen patterns)

  • Eat warm, cooked, easy-to-digest meals: congee or rice porridge, oatmeal, stewed vegetables, soups, and soft-cooked eggs. Think "already partly digested."
  • Smaller, more frequent meals — five small meals beat three large ones when the lining is angry.
  • Chew thoroughly and eat without rushing. Digestion starts in the mouth, and in TCM theory the Spleen's workload is directly lightened by thorough chewing.
  • Consider foods traditionally used for mucosal support: cooked cabbage (the classic remedy — and, notably, early clinical research in the 1940s-50s reported that fresh cabbage juice appeared to speed healing of stomach ulcers), okra, and slippery foods like well-cooked oats.

Take the pressure off (especially for Liver patterns)

  • Notice the connection between your stress level and your stomach. If pain flares on busy weeks, the Liver pattern is likely in play.
  • Build in a genuine wind-down: a 15-minute walk after dinner, slow breathing, or gentle stretching — movement that lets tension move through instead of lodging in your gut.
  • Don't eat under stress. If you're mid-crisis, wait until you've regulated before you sit down to a meal.

Acupressure points traditionally used for gastric support

Press each point gently with a thumb or fingertip in slow circles for 1-2 minutes, both sides where applicable, once or twice daily:

  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — four finger-widths below the kneecap, just outside the shinbone. The classic point for strengthening the whole digestive system; supports the Spleen's repair work.
  • CV-12 (Zhongwan) — midway between your belly button and the bottom of your breastbone. Directly over the stomach; traditionally used to settle fullness, nausea, and discomfort.
  • PC-6 (Neiguan) — on the inner forearm, about three finger-widths above the wrist crease, between the two tendons. The go-to point for nausea and for "rebellious" stomach sensations like sour regurgitation.
  • ST-44 (Neiting) — on the top of the foot, between the second and third toes. A classic point for clearing Stomach Heat — useful for the burning pattern.
  • LV-3 (Taichong) — on the top of the foot, in the depression between the big toe and second toe. Moves Liver Qi; the stress-pattern point.

What Research Suggests

The research on complementary approaches to gastritis is early but genuinely interesting. A few lines of evidence worth knowing:

  • Fresh cabbage juice — the classic clinical reports from the 1940s and 1950s found that fresh cabbage juice appeared to accelerate healing of stomach ulcers, and it remains a traditional remedy for gastric complaints. Modern researchers attribute part of this to glutamine and vitamin U-like compounds that support the mucosal lining. Evidence is historical rather than contemporary RCT-level, so think of it as a food-level support, not a treatment.
  • Mastic gum — a resin from the mastic tree traditionally chewed for stomach complaints. Small randomized trials have reported that mastic gum may reduce symptoms in functional dyspepsia (a condition that overlaps heavily with chronic gastritis), though findings have been mixed and studies are small.
  • Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL) — a form of licorice with the blood-pressure-affecting compound removed. Some older clinical studies reported that DGL appeared to support ulcer healing, and it is still widely used as a soothing supplement for gastric irritation. It's not a replacement for medical treatment, and any licorice product should be discussed with a pharmacist or doctor.
  • Acupuncture and acupressure — meta-analyses of acupuncture for functional dyspepsia report possible symptom benefit, and studies on the points ST-36 and CV-12 suggest they may influence gastric acid secretion and mucosal blood flow. Most trials are small and designs vary — promising, not conclusive.

None of this replaces your doctor's plan. If you have H. pylori, eradication therapy is the medically established approach. If you're on acid reducers, keep taking them as prescribed and talk to your doctor before changing anything.

When to See a Doctor: Gastritis sometimes signals something that needs urgent medical attention. Seek care promptly if you have black, tarry stools; vomit that contains blood or looks like coffee grounds; severe or worsening abdominal pain; unintentional weight loss; difficulty swallowing; or persistent vomiting. If you've been treating your stomach with antacids for more than two weeks without improvement, it's worth a medical review — and if you're 45 or older with new-onset symptoms, don't wait it out.
Practical Takeaways:
  • Gastritis is an inflamed lining, not just "too much acid" — TCM sees it as friction (Stomach Heat) wearing down a lining the Spleen can't fully repair.
  • Find your pattern: burning (Heat), dull ache and fatigue (Spleen Qi Deficiency), nausea and heaviness (Damp-Heat), or stress-triggered (Liver Qi).
  • Cool the friction: cut spicy food, alcohol, coffee on empty stomach, smoking, and long-term NSAIDs — talk to your doctor before stopping any medication.
  • Feed the repair crew: warm cooked meals, congee, oatmeal, cabbage, small frequent portions, and thorough chewing.
  • Use acupressure daily — ST-36, CV-12, PC-6, ST-44, and LV-3 — 1-2 minutes per point.
  • See a doctor for black stools, blood in vomit, severe pain, weight loss, or symptoms persisting beyond two weeks of self-care.
