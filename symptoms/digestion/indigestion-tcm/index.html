Indigestion and Poor Digestion: A TCM Perspective

Symptom Guide · 8 min read

Your stomach feels heavy after meals — like food is sitting there for hours. You feel full after just a few bites of a meal you looked forward to. Maybe you belch, feel vaguely nauseous, or carry a dull epigastric ache that comes and goes without clear reason.

This is indigestion. And if you've seen a doctor, you've probably heard: "Your tests are normal. Maybe try smaller meals."

Western medicine calls this functional dyspepsia — a diagnosis that essentially means "your stomach is bothering you but we can't find anything structurally wrong." The standard toolkit includes antacids, prokinetics, and stress management. These help some people, but for many, the relief is partial or temporary.

TCM takes a different approach. It doesn't ask "what's broken in your stomach?" It asks why your digestive process has lost its rhythm.

This article focuses specifically on indigestion — the upper-digestive discomfort, early fullness, belching, and sluggishness that follows meals. For more targeted issues like gas and bloating, see our guide on Gas and Bloating Relief. For acid reflux patterns, see Acid Reflux and GERD from a TCM Perspective.

The Stomach in TCM: More Than a Food Processor

In TCM, the Stomach is the body's ripener. Its job is to receive food and begin breaking it down — like a slow cooker that has just been turned on. The Spleen (in TCM terms — not the physical organ you'd see on an ultrasound) is the transformer. It takes the partially processed material from the Stomach and converts it into Qi and Blood — the body's usable energy.

Key TCM Concept — The Stomach-Spleen Axis: Think of your digestive system as a manufacturing plant. The Stomach is the receiving dock where raw materials arrive and initial processing begins. The Spleen is the factory floor — it turns those materials into finished products (Qi and Blood) and ships them to every department in the body. When the receiving dock operates inefficiently, the entire factory downstream suffers. That inefficiency is what TCM calls indigestion.

For this system to work, the Stomach's Qi must descend. Food enters the mouth and should move downward — through the esophagus, into the Stomach, then into the intestines. When Stomach Qi descends properly, digestion feels effortless. When it rebels — rises instead of descends — you get belching, reflux, nausea, and a feeling of stuckness in the upper abdomen.

This concept of "Stomach Qi not descending" is at the heart of indigestion in TCM theory. The question is: what's blocking the descent? The answer varies, and that's why TCM distinguishes between several indigestion patterns rather than treating all cases the same.

Four Common Indigestion Patterns

Pattern 1: Spleen Qi Deficiency — The Weak Digestion

This is the most common pattern underlying chronic poor digestion. The Spleen lacks the energy to transform food properly, so the stomach empties slowly and the whole digestive process becomes sluggish.

What you might notice:

  • Fatigue after meals — you feel like you need to lie down after eating
  • Loose stools or undigested food in stool
  • Poor appetite, especially in the morning
  • A pale complexion and feeling cold easily
  • Bloating that's worse in the afternoon

What contributes: Over time, eating cold or raw food, irregular meal timing, overthinking and worry (which directly weakens the Spleen in TCM theory), and insufficient rest.

What may help: Warm, cooked meals are foundational — soups, stews, congee (rice porridge). Ginger in small amounts. Avoiding iced drinks completely. Eating smaller, more frequent meals. For a deeper look at this pattern, see our guide on Spleen Qi Deficiency.

Pattern 2: Food Stagnation — The Overloaded Stomach

In this pattern, the Stomach has received more food than its digestive capacity can handle in a reasonable time. Food sits and "rots" in the Stomach instead of being processed and passed downward.

What you might notice:

  • Belching that tastes like the food you just ate
  • A sour or rotten taste in the mouth after meals
  • A feeling of fullness that is uncomfortable rather than satisfying
  • Nausea that improves temporarily after vomiting or belching
  • Thick tongue coating (you can see this in the mirror)

What contributes: Eating too much at once, eating too quickly, heavy greasy meals, eating late at night, or eating when you're not truly hungry.

What may help: Lighter meals with smaller portions. Taking a short, gentle walk after eating (10-15 minutes, not intense exercise). Hawthorn tea or diluted apple cider vinegar before meals. Allowing at least 3 hours between the last meal and bedtime.

Pattern 3: Liver Qi Stagnation Invading the Stomach — Stress-Related Indigestion

This is the pattern for people whose indigestion is clearly linked to stress. In TCM, the Liver governs the smooth flow of Qi throughout the body. When stress and frustration build up, Liver Qi becomes stagnant. Instead of flowing smoothly, it "attacks" the Stomach — disrupting the Stomach's normal downward movement.

What you might notice:

  • Indigestion that flares up during stressful periods or when you're angry
  • A feeling of tightness or pressure in the upper abdomen and ribcage
  • Belching that provides brief relief but returns quickly
  • Nausea when stressed
  • Worse symptoms when eating in a rushed or annoyed state

What contributes: Chronic stress, suppressed frustration, irregular eating schedule due to a demanding lifestyle, eating while working or scrolling on your phone.

What may help: Eating in a calm state — even 5 minutes of deep breathing before a meal can shift this pattern. Peppermint or chamomile tea after meals. Acupressure at PC6 (Neiguan, on the inner wrist) and LV3 (Taichong, on the top of the foot). For more on how stress affects digestion from a TCM perspective, see our guide on Liver Invading the Spleen.

Pattern 4: Stomach Yin Deficiency — The Dry, Sensitive Stomach

This pattern is less common but distinctly different. Stomach Yin refers to the fluids that moisten and protect the stomach lining. When these fluids run low, the stomach becomes dry, sensitive, and easily irritated.

What you might notice:

  • Feeling full after eating very small amounts
  • A mild burning sensation in the upper abdomen (different from acid reflux)
  • Dry mouth and thirst, especially in the afternoon
  • A preference for cool, moist foods
  • Constipation with dry, hard stools

What contributes: Long-term spicy or fried food consumption, chronic stress, insufficient fluid intake, and age-related changes. This pattern is more common in people over 50 and those who have used antacids or PPIs for extended periods.

What may help: Cooling, moistening foods — pears, cucumber, cooked leafy greens, bone broth. Avoid spicy food, alcohol, and excessive caffeine. Small, frequent meals rather than large ones. For a deeper exploration, see our guide on Stomach Yin Deficiency.

How You Eat Matters as Much as What You Eat

In TCM, the circumstances of eating are part of the digestive process itself. They are not external factors — they determine how well digestion functions. The following practices are simple, evidence-informed habits that support smooth digestion regardless of which pattern you resonate with:

Eat in a settled state. Your nervous system needs to be in parasympathetic mode for digestion to work properly. Even a shallow breathing practice for 30 seconds before a meal can shift the digestive environment.

Chew until liquid. TCM has long emphasized thorough chewing as the first stage of digestion. In modern terms, thorough mastication increases the surface area for enzymatic breakdown and signals the stomach to prepare for incoming food.

No iced drinks with meals. Temperature matters in TCM. Cold drinks are thought to "extinguish" the digestive fire — analogous to the modern observation that gastric blood flow decreases when cold liquid enters the stomach.

Watch the clock. Eating within a consistent window (e.g., 8 AM to 7 PM) and allowing 3-4 hours between meals gives the stomach time to complete one round of processing before the next one begins.

Acupressure Points for Indigestion Relief

When you feel discomfort building after a meal, these points can be used for gentle self-care. Apply firm, circular pressure for 1-2 minutes each:

ST36 (Zusanli) — Four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width toward the outside of the shinbone. This is the most well-known digestive support point in TCM and one of the most researched in modern acupuncture studies. It's traditionally used to strengthen the Spleen and Stomach and support overall energy production.

CV12 (Zhongwan) — On the midline of the abdomen, halfway between the navel and the bottom of the breastbone. This point sits directly over the Stomach area. Gentle, clockwise pressure helps Stomach Qi descend and relieves post-meal fullness.

PC6 (Neiguan) — Two finger-widths above the wrist crease, between the two tendons on the inner forearm. This point is traditionally used for nausea, acid reflux, and stress-related digestive discomfort. It's also well-studied for its anti-nausea effects.

⚠️ Note on self-care: Acupressure is a complementary practice. Avoid applying pressure to points near wounds, varicose veins, or areas of acute inflammation. Discontinue if pain occurs. These techniques are not a substitute for medical evaluation of persistent or severe digestive symptoms.

What Modern Research Suggests

The TCM framework for indigestion may sound unfamiliar to Western ears, but several lines of research are beginning to align with its core insights:

Acupuncture for functional dyspepsia. A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis (Medicine, 12 RCTs, 1,244 participants) found that acupuncture was associated with improvement in functional dyspepsia symptoms compared to sham acupuncture or medication alone. The review noted that symptom improvement was most notable for postprandial fullness and early satiety — the two core symptoms of indigestion in TCM's "Stomach Qi not descending" framework.

ST36 (Zusanli) and gastric motility. Research published in Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine (2018) found that electroacupuncture at ST36 was associated with increased gastric motility and vagal nerve activity in participants with functional dyspepsia. This aligns with the traditional use of ST36 for supporting Stomach and Spleen function.

Gut-brain axis and stress-related dyspepsia. A 2021 review in Frontiers in Neuroscience highlighted the role of the gut-brain axis in functional dyspepsia, noting that psychological stress alters gastric accommodation, motility, and visceral sensitivity. These findings parallel the TCM observation that Liver Qi stagnation (stress) directly disrupts Stomach function.

Ginger for nausea and gastric emptying. A 2019 systematic review in Nutrients confirmed that ginger accelerates gastric emptying and reduces nausea — consistent with its traditional use for Stomach patterns where Qi is not descending properly.

When to See a Medical Doctor

TCM offers a useful framework for understanding chronic digestive patterns, but some symptoms require immediate medical evaluation:

  • Blood in your stool (bright red or black/tarry)
  • Unexplained weight loss
  • Severe or worsening abdominal pain
  • Difficulty swallowing or pain with swallowing
  • Persistent vomiting, especially if blood is present
  • A family history of gastrointestinal cancer
  • Anemia that hasn't been evaluated

If you're over 45 and experiencing new-onset indigestion, it's especially important to get a medical evaluation before assuming it's a functional pattern.

Practical Takeaways

Summary: What to Do About Indigestion
  • Identify your pattern: Does your digestion slow down after meals (Spleen Qi Deficiency)? Do you feel uncomfortably full and belch (Food Stagnation)? Is it clearly stress-related (Liver Qi attacking the Stomach)? Or do you feel full from tiny amounts with a mild burn (Stomach Yin Deficiency)? The right approach depends on the right pattern.
  • Warm, cooked, simple meals: Soups, congee, steamed vegetables, and smaller portions reduce the digestive load regardless of your pattern.
  • Eat in a calm state: The nervous system directly regulates digestive function. A few deep breaths before eating is not just mindfulness — it's physiology.
  • Acupressure as a tool: ST36 (below the knee), CV12 (mid-abdomen), and PC6 (inner wrist) offer gentle support for post-meal discomfort.
  • Rule out medical causes first: Particularly if you're over 45 or have alarm symptoms. TCM complements medical care — it doesn't replace a proper workup.
