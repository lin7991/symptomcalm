Insulin Resistance: The TCM View on Metabolic Balance

Symptom Guide · 8 min read

Your blood test comes back with a phrase you half-recognize: "insulin resistance." Your doctor explains it as a warning sign — your cells have stopped responding to insulin the way they should, and your pancreas is working overtime to compensate. The advice you get is familiar: lose weight, eat less, move more.

And maybe you've tried that. Maybe the numbers barely moved anyway. Or maybe you're not even overweight — and yet the lab results still say your metabolism is struggling.

This article looks at insulin resistance through a different lens. In TCM theory, the problem isn't simply "too much sugar in the bloodstream." It's a story about fuel delivery — how your body transforms food into usable energy, moves it where it needs to go, and stores the reserve. When that delivery system slows down, blood sugar is just the most visible symptom.

In TCM theory, insulin resistance is a fuel-delivery problem. The Spleen system — the body's food-to-energy conversion plant — fails to transform what you eat into clean, usable Qi. The unconverted residue accumulates as Dampness and Phlegm, the metabolic "sludge" that slows the whole system down. Understanding which part of this chain has broken may open doors that another diet never will.

The Problem Your Doctor Sees — and the Layer Underneath

Here's what conventional medicine has mapped out clearly: after a meal, carbohydrates break down into glucose. Glucose enters the bloodstream, and the pancreas releases insulin — the hormone that tells cells to open their doors and let glucose in for energy. In insulin resistance, cells gradually stop responding to that signal. The pancreas compensates by producing more insulin. Blood sugar stays high, and over years, that strain can lead to prediabetes, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular risk.

It's a precise, well-studied picture. And yet, two people can eat identical meals and show very different metabolic responses. One stays energetic and metabolically flexible. The other spikes, crashes, and craves more carbs an hour later. Why?

Conventional medicine points to genetics, muscle mass, sleep, stress, and microbiome differences — all real factors. But the practical guidance still tends to loop back to "eat less, move more." TCM asks a complementary question: not just "how much are you eating," but "what is your body doing with what you eat?"

The TCM View: Blood Sugar as a Fuel-Delivery Story

In TCM theory, the body runs on Qi — the vital energy extracted from food and air. The organ system responsible for this extraction is the Spleen: the body's food processor and power plant rolled into one. Its job is to transform what you eat into Qi and Blood, then transport those resources to every tissue that needs them. For a deeper look at this system, our TCM Basics guide to the Spleen covers the full picture.

Now translate that into metabolic terms. Glucose is the fuel. Insulin is the delivery truck. The cell doors are the receiving docks. The Spleen's transformation function is the distribution network that keeps the whole system running smoothly. When Spleen Qi is strong, fuel moves efficiently: energy in, energy delivered, waste cleared. When it's weak, three things can go wrong:

  1. Transformation lags — food isn't fully converted into usable energy, so glucose lingers in the bloodstream instead of reaching cells
  2. Fluids turn to Dampness — unprocessed material accumulates as a heavy, turbid "sludge" that TCM calls Dampness (湿), the pattern most closely associated with metabolic syndrome in TCM literature
  3. Reserves get drained — the Kidney system, which governs long-term energy storage, slowly depletes under the strain

From this view, insulin resistance isn't a sugar addiction or a willpower failure. It's an energy-processing bottleneck — and the bottleneck can sit at different points in different people. That's why TCM doesn't prescribe one metabolic diet. It works with patterns.

Three TCM Patterns Behind Insulin Resistance

Not everyone with insulin resistance experiences it the same way. TCM distinguishes several patterns, each with its own signature. Read through these and see which one sounds most like you.

1. Spleen Qi Deficiency — "The Sluggish Converter"

You might have this if: You crash after meals, especially carb-heavy ones. You crave sweets and refined carbs, and coffee only rescues you for an hour or two. You feel tired after eating, your stool is loose or sticky, and you struggle with brain fog in the afternoon. You may not be overweight at all.

TCM explanation: The Spleen's conversion plant is underpowered. Food goes in, but the transformation into usable Qi is inefficient — like a laptop plugged in that isn't charging. Glucose sits in the bloodstream, undelivered, and your energy never quite arrives. The constant craving for sugar is the body asking for fuel it can't actually use.

Traditional approach: Strengthen the Spleen with warm, cooked, easily digested foods — congee, pumpkin, yam, oats, and gently cooked vegetables. Herbs like Astragalus (Huang Qi), Atractylodes (Bai Zhu), and Poria (Fu Ling) are traditionally used in formulas that support this transformation. Small, regular meals matter more than large ones.

2. Phlegm-Dampness — "The Clogged Pipes"

You might have this if: You carry weight around your middle, feel heavy and sluggish, and wake up foggy even after a full night's sleep. Your tongue may look thick with a greasy coating. You crave sweet, greasy, or starchy foods, and your digestion feels slow. Snoring, or feeling like your body is "waterlogged," fits this pattern.

TCM explanation: When the Spleen can't process fluids and food fully, the residue accumulates as Phlegm-Dampness — thick, turbid sludge that clogs the body's channels. Think of a water treatment plant that can't keep up with the inflow: instead of clean water flowing through, it releases thick sludge that settles in the pipes. In TCM theory, this is the pattern most closely tied to metabolic conditions like insulin resistance, fatty liver, and elevated lipids.

Traditional approach: Drain and transform the Dampness. Foods that help include barley, adzuki beans, winter melon, celery, cucumber, and lotus leaf tea. Cutting refined sugar, dairy, and fried foods reduces the sludge load. Movement matters more here than in other patterns — the body needs to circulate to clear the buildup. Acupressure on SP-9 and ST-40 is traditionally used to support this.

3. Kidney Yin Depletion with Empty Heat — "The Drained Reserve"

You might have this if: You're lean — even athletic — but your lab work shows insulin resistance anyway. You feel restless, wake at night, or run warm (especially palms, soles, or chest at night). You're thirsty, prone to dry skin, and your energy runs in uneven waves. You may have a history of long work hours, chronic stress, or years of pushing through fatigue.

TCM explanation: The Kidneys store the body's deepest energy reserve (Jing). Chronic stress and overwork drain this reserve, and the Yin — the cooling, moistening foundation of the system — runs low. With less Yin to anchor it, the system runs "hot" and unstable: metabolic signals get noisy, and even a healthy weight doesn't protect against imbalance. This is the pattern behind the "thin outside, metabolically stressed inside" paradox.

Traditional approach: Nourish Yin and protect the reserve. Cooling, moistening foods — black sesame, mulberries, goji berries, mild fish, bone broths — are traditionally used. Early, consistent sleep is prioritized, and intense late-night stimulation is reduced. Gentle restorative movement (Qigong, walking) replaces exhaustive training during recovery phases.

Foods and Eating Patterns That May Support Metabolic Balance

In TCM theory, metabolic support starts not with restriction but with how you eat. A few principles recur across nearly every pattern:

Meal structure and timing

  • Food order matters. Eating vegetables and protein before carbohydrates at the same meal is associated in some research with smaller post-meal glucose and insulin spikes. It's a simple change that fits any cuisine.
  • Warm, cooked meals. The Spleen processes cooked food more easily than raw, cold, or iced items. A warm breakfast — congee, oatmeal, eggs, cooked greens — is traditionally considered the strongest start to the day.
  • Bigger meal earlier. The TCM Organ Clock maps peak digestive energy to the morning. Keeping dinner lighter and earlier gives the system time to process before rest.
  • Regular meal times. Erratic eating forces the Spleen to restart its engine repeatedly. Consistency supports steady energy conversion.

What TCM advises reducing

  • Refined sugar and liquid calories — provide "empty fuel" that taxes the Spleen's conversion system
  • Iced drinks with meals — extinguish digestive warmth and slow transformation
  • Fried and heavily processed foods — dense, greasy inputs that generate Dampness and Phlegm
  • Excessive dairy — a common Dampness generator in many people
  • Late-night eating — the digestive system runs at lower capacity while the body prepares for rest

Acupressure and Movement for Metabolic Support

Several acupoints are traditionally used to support digestion, fluid metabolism, and the energy systems involved in metabolic balance. Self-applied acupressure is gentle and can be done at home:

  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — below the knee, traditionally the master point for strengthening Qi and supporting the Spleen and Stomach. Press for 1-2 minutes, both legs.
  • SP-6 (Sanyinjiao) — on the inner calf, supports Spleen function and fluid metabolism. Skip during pregnancy.
  • SP-9 (Yinlingquan) — inner side of the knee, drains Dampness from the lower body.
  • ST-40 (Fenglong) — outer calf, the classic "resolve Phlegm-Damp" point in TCM practice.
  • KI-3 (Taixi) — behind the inner ankle, supports the Kidney reserve for Yin-depletion patterns.

Gentle pressure in small circles, 1-2 minutes per point, once or twice daily. Never press on broken, infected, or swollen skin.

Movement is a second lever. A short walk after meals — even 10 minutes — has been associated in research with lower post-meal glucose responses, and it aligns perfectly with TCM's emphasis on keeping Qi and fluids moving rather than sitting still after eating. For longer-term support, gentle practices like Qigong, Tai Chi, and walking suit Spleen patterns better than exhaustive training that further drains the system.

What the Research Says

The research on TCM and metabolic health is still emerging, but several areas show promising preliminary findings:

  • A 2019 systematic review in Acupuncture in Medicine found that acupuncture, combined with lifestyle measures, was associated with improvements in insulin sensitivity and fasting glucose in people with insulin resistance, though the authors called for larger, higher-quality trials.
  • Randomized controlled trials of berberine — a compound from the TCM herb Huang Lian — have shown glucose-lowering effects comparable to conventional medications in some studies, making it one of the most researched natural compounds in metabolic medicine.
  • A 2015 study from Weill Cornell Medicine found that the order in which food is eaten (vegetables and protein before carbohydrates) was associated with lower post-meal glucose and insulin spikes in people with type 2 diabetes and prediabetes.
  • Research in Diabetologia found that short walks after meals — as little as 10 minutes — were associated with meaningfully lower post-meal blood sugar compared to a single longer walk.
  • Emerging microbiome research has begun to intersect with TCM's concept of Dampness: some studies suggest that TCM Dampness and Phlegm-Dampness patterns correlate with distinct gut microbiota profiles, hinting at a biological bridge between the traditional framework and modern metabolic science.

What researchers consistently emphasize: TCM approaches should be viewed as complementary tools alongside — never instead of — established medical care for insulin resistance and metabolic conditions.

Practical Takeaways: Where to Start

If you've been told you have insulin resistance — or you suspect it — here's a simple starting point that honors both the lab results and the TCM framework:
  1. Know your numbers. Ask your doctor about fasting insulin, fasting glucose, and HOMA-IR. You can't work with a map you haven't seen.
  2. Change the order of your plate. Vegetables and protein first, carbohydrates last — at every meal. It costs nothing and has research behind it.
  3. Warm up your breakfast. A warm, cooked breakfast (congee, oatmeal, eggs, cooked greens) supports the Spleen at the moment the body is traditionally considered most ready to transform food.
  4. Walk 10 minutes after meals. Not a workout — a short, gentle walk to keep fuel and fluids moving.
  5. Try a 5-minute acupressure routine. ST-36 and SP-6 morning and evening, gently, 1-2 minutes each.
  6. Protect your sleep and your evenings. The Kidney reserve is rebuilt at night. Consistent, early-ish sleep and reduced late-night stimulation are metabolic practices, not luxuries.

When to See a Doctor

Insulin resistance is a medical diagnosis — and some situations require professional evaluation before any self-care approach:

  • Fasting glucose of 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or higher on repeated tests, or an HbA1c of 5.7% or higher
  • Acanthosis nigricans — dark, velvety patches of skin, often on the neck, armpits, or groin
  • Unexplained weight gain or difficulty losing weight despite consistent effort
  • Signs of high blood sugar: unusual thirst, frequent urination, blurry vision, or slow-healing wounds
  • A history of gestational diabetes, PCOS, or a family history of type 2 diabetes
  • If you already take medication for blood sugar or blood pressure — always coordinate any dietary or lifestyle changes with your healthcare provider

TCM is a complementary perspective. Use it alongside — not instead of — professional medical guidance.

Bottom Line

Insulin resistance can feel like a verdict: your metabolism is failing, and you're the one to blame. The TCM view reframes it. This isn't a character flaw — it's a fuel-delivery system working under strain. Whether the bottleneck sits in Spleen transformation, Dampness accumulation, or a depleted Kidney reserve, the framework points to a specific set of habits that may support the system where it actually needs help.

No approach — TCM or otherwise — promises a quick fix. But understanding the pattern behind your numbers may turn a discouraging lab result into something more useful: a starting point.
