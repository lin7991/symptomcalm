Hiatal Hernia: A TCM Perspective on Stomach Qi and Reflux Symptoms

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

You've stopped eating big meals — you just can't. Mid-meal, something "catches" at the bottom of your chest. A sour taste climbs your throat, and leaning forward to tie your shoes seems to invite it back up. Antacids take the edge off, but the sensation keeps returning. Eventually a doctor mentions the words "hiatal hernia" — a phrase that sounds mechanical, structural, almost architectural. And in a sense, it is.

This guide looks at hiatal hernia through a TCM lens. We'll explore why TCM treats this condition as a problem of direction and pressure — Stomach Qi that should move downward, and a "door" between your chest and abdomen that isn't holding — and what traditionally used approaches may support it. For the broader symptom layer — the patterns behind heartburn and acid reflux itself — see our guides on Stomach Rebellious Qi and GERD from a TCM perspective. This article focuses specifically on the structural story underneath.

The Door Between Your Chest and Abdomen

Let's start with the anatomy, because hiatal hernia is one of those conditions where understanding the structure makes everything else click.

The diaphragm is a dome-shaped muscle that separates your chest (thorax) from your abdomen. It's the engine of breathing — every breath you take, it contracts and flattens. But it also serves a second, less famous role: it's the floor between two body compartments.

Near the middle of that floor is a small opening called the hiatus — essentially a doorframe through which your esophagus passes on its way down to the stomach. In a hiatal hernia, part of the stomach pushes up through that opening into the chest. The lower esophageal sphincter (LES) — the muscular valve that normally keeps stomach contents down — sits right at the edge of this doorframe. When the stomach migrates upward, the valve loses its anchor, and the door no longer closes properly. That's when reflux symptoms begin.

Think of a two-story building with an elevator shaft. The diaphragm is the floor between the stories. The hiatus is the elevator door. When the doorframe is sound, even a crowded lower floor stays where it belongs. When the frame is damaged, normal activity — a full stomach, bending over, straining — creates pressure in the lower story that pops the door open, and the elevator (stomach contents) rides up into the upper floor.

This is why hiatal hernia is so often diagnosed alongside reflux, heartburn, and regurgitation: the structural compromise makes Stomach Qi rebellion physically easier.

The TCM View: When the Downward Flow Meets a Weak Door

In TCM, the diaphragm region sits at the boundary between the Upper Burner (the chest, where the Lungs and Heart govern) and the Middle Burner (the abdomen, where the Spleen and Stomach process food). One of the most important principles in TCM digestion is that Stomach Qi naturally descends — 胃气下行为顺, "descending is the smooth course of Stomach Qi." Food and fluids are meant to move down through the digestive tract in an orderly way.

When that downward flow reverses — 胃气上逆, "Stomach Qi rebelling upward" — you get the classic symptom family: acid rising, heartburn, regurgitation, belching, nausea. That's the functional layer, and it's covered in depth in our Stomach Rebellious Qi guide.

A hiatal hernia adds a structural layer on top. TCM doesn't separate structure and function the way modern medicine often does. The doorframe (diaphragm and hiatus) is part of the system that keeps Qi flowing in the right direction. When the door is weak, the system loses its ability to hold. And crucially, TCM asks: what is pushing on that door from below?

What Pushes on the Door: Pressure From Below

Here, the TCM and modern views converge remarkably. In Western physiology, reflux happens when pressure in the abdomen exceeds the resistance of the door — the LES and the crural diaphragm (the muscle fibers of the diaphragm that wrap around the esophagus like a second sphincter). In TCM, the same logic appears in different language: three common patterns create pressure from below, and each one has a specific character.

Pattern 1: Liver Qi Stagnation — The Jammed Doorframe

Stress, frustration, and rushing are the classic triggers. When Liver Qi stagnates, tension tightens the chest and the diaphragm area, and digestive movement slows. Meals eaten under stress sit heavily; the pressure builds, and the weak door gives way.

  • How it feels: reflux or heartburn worse during stressful periods, a tight chest, frequent sighing, belching that briefly relieves the pressure, flare-ups after rushing through meals or arguments.
  • Traditionally used approach: slow meals in a calm setting, gentle movement to keep Qi flowing, breathing practices (see below), and acupressure at LV-3 (Taichong) to release constrained Liver Qi.

Pattern 2: Spleen Qi Deficiency — The Loose Hinge

The Spleen in TCM transforms food into Qi and Blood and helps hold everything in its proper place. When Spleen Qi is deficient, digestion is sluggish, and the body's "holding power" weakens — a concept TCM also links to organs sagging or sinking. A weakened hold on the stomach's position can make the hiatus door looser.

  • How it feels: reflux alongside fatigue, bloating after meals, loose stools, a heavy or full sensation in the upper abdomen, feeling worse after large or cold meals.
  • Traditionally used approach: warm, cooked, easily digestible foods; smaller frequent meals; resting after eating (upright); and acupressure at ST-36 (Zusanli) to strengthen the digestive system.

Pattern 3: Dampness and Excess Weight — The Weight on the Door

Dampness in TCM is the "heavy, sluggish" quality — often accompanied by excess weight, especially around the middle. From a mechanical standpoint, abdominal weight increases intra-abdominal pressure: it literally presses on the door from below, hour after hour. Rich, fried, and sweet foods generate Dampness in TCM terms and are also the foods most likely to relax the LES in Western terms — a rare double agreement.

  • How it feels: reflux worse after heavy, rich, or greasy meals; a sluggish, heavy feeling; possible phlegmy throat or post-nasal drip; symptoms worse when carrying extra abdominal weight.
  • Traditionally used approach: light, cooked meals; less fried food, alcohol, and sugar; gradual, sustainable weight management; and acupressure at ST-40 (Fenglong), the classic point for transforming Dampness.

What May Help the Door Hold: Practical Approaches

None of this is about "fixing the hernia" — a hiatal hernia is an anatomical finding, and most people with one never need surgery. The goal, in both TCM and modern management, is to reduce the pressure on the door and strengthen the system around it. Here are the approaches most worth trying, in order of impact.

1. Breathe with your belly — it trains the door's second sphincter

The crural diaphragm — the part of the diaphragm that wraps around the esophagus — functions as a second sphincter, contributing a meaningful share of the pressure that keeps stomach contents down. Slow, deep, belly-focused breathing exercises train exactly this muscle. In TCM terms, this is also a way of calming Liver Qi and helping Lung Qi descend, which supports the whole downward-flowing system.

A simple daily practice: sit upright, one hand on your chest and one on your belly. Inhale slowly through your nose for about 4 seconds, letting your belly expand (your chest hand should stay nearly still). Exhale slowly through your mouth for 6–8 seconds. Repeat for 5 minutes, ideally twice a day — for example, before breakfast and after dinner. Small research studies suggest this kind of breathing practice may reduce reflux symptoms over several weeks.

2. Change how you eat — not just what you eat

  • Eat smaller meals. A full stomach increases pressure in the abdomen — directly on the door. Six small meals may beat three large ones.
  • Eat slowly and sit upright. Both reduce the volume and pressure load at any one moment.
  • Don't lie down for 2–3 hours after eating. Lying down removes gravity's help and increases reflux exposure.
  • Avoid bending and heavy lifting right after meals. Both spike abdominal pressure.
  • Wear loose clothing. Tight belts and waistbands are literally external pressure on the abdomen.

3. Manage the pressure habits you control

  • Elevate the head of your bed by 6–8 inches (with blocks under the bed frame, not just extra pillows) — this helps reduce nighttime reflux.
  • Don't strain on the toilet. Straining forces pressure upward through the door; if constipation is a factor, see our TCM guide to constipation.
  • Move after meals — gently. A short, easy walk supports digestion; vigorous exercise too soon after eating can push the door open.
  • Address excess abdominal weight gradually. Even modest, sustained weight loss reduces intra-abdominal pressure and reflux symptoms in many people.

4. Acupressure points to support the system

Acupressure is a gentle, self-applied complement. Press each point with a fingertip or thumb in small circles for 1–2 minutes, both sides where applicable:

  • PC-6 (Neiguan) — inner forearm, two finger-widths above the wrist crease, between the two tendons. Traditionally used to calm Stomach Qi rebellion, nausea, and chest tightness.
  • CV-12 (Zhongwan) — midline of the abdomen, halfway between the navel and the base of the breastbone. The "center of the stomach" point, used for digestive support.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — below the knee, four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width outside the shinbone. The classic point for strengthening digestion.
  • LV-3 (Taichong) — top of the foot, in the depression where the big toe and second toe meet. Used to smooth Liver Qi when stress drives symptoms.
  • ST-40 (Fenglong) — halfway between the knee and ankle, about two finger-widths outside the shinbone. The classic point for transforming Dampness.

What the Research Says

TCM is not a substitute for medical evaluation of a hiatal hernia — but several strands of research align with the "pressure on the door" model above:

  • Breathing exercises for reflux: a small clinical study (Eherer et al., American Journal of Gastroenterology, 2012) found that patients who practiced abdominal breathing exercises for several weeks showed reduced reflux symptoms and improved quality of life. The proposed mechanism — training the crural diaphragm — matches the "second sphincter" role described by gastroenterologists.
  • The crural diaphragm as a sphincter: reviews of LES physiology (e.g., Mittal & Balaban, American Journal of Medicine, 1997) describe the diaphragm's muscle fibers wrapping around the esophagus and contributing to the pressure barrier — a physiological basis for why breathing and posture matter.
  • Abdominal pressure and reflux: studies consistently link obesity, waist circumference, and tight clothing with increased intra-abdominal pressure and more reflux episodes — the mechanical version of "pressure from below."
  • Position and reflux: research on head-of-bed elevation and postprandial positioning supports the practical advice to stay upright after meals and elevate the head of the bed at night.

Studies are small and findings vary, so view these as supporting context rather than proof — and always discuss your situation with a doctor.

When to See a Doctor

Hiatal hernia symptoms overlap with far more serious conditions, and some complications are emergencies. Seek medical attention promptly if you experience:

  • Chest pain — especially pain that spreads to the arm, jaw, or back, or that comes with shortness of breath, sweating, or nausea. This can be a sign of a heart problem, not just reflux. If it's severe or sudden, call emergency services.
  • Difficulty or pain when swallowing, or food getting stuck in your chest.
  • Vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools.
  • Severe, cramping abdominal or chest pain after eating, which can rarely signal a complication of the hernia itself (strangulation or twisting of the stomach) — this is a medical emergency.
  • Unexplained weight loss or anemia.
  • Choking episodes at night or recurrent pneumonia, which can indicate reflux reaching the lungs.

If you have been diagnosed with a hiatal hernia, discuss with your doctor whether any of your symptoms — including reflux — warrant imaging, medication, or other management. TCM practices can be a complementary layer, not a replacement for that conversation.

Practical Takeaways

If you remember five things from this guide:

  • Think of the diaphragm as a door. Hiatal hernia weakens the doorframe; reflux happens when pressure from below pushes it open.
  • Stomach Qi should move downward. Anything that encourages downward flow and reduces upward pressure supports the system.
  • Train your breath. Slow, belly-focused breathing exercises may strengthen the diaphragm's sphincter function — start with 5 minutes, twice a day.
  • Eat small, slow, and upright. Smaller meals, no lying down for 2–3 hours after eating, no tight waistbands, no bending or lifting right after meals.
  • Match the approach to your pattern. Stress-driven symptoms point to Liver Qi (slow meals, LV-3); fatigue and bloating point to Spleen Qi (warm cooked food, ST-36); heaviness and excess weight point to Dampness (light meals, ST-40, gradual weight management).

Hiatal hernia is a structural finding, but your experience of it is shaped by direction, pressure, and pattern. Addressing those — with a doctor's guidance alongside — is where a TCM perspective may offer genuine support.
