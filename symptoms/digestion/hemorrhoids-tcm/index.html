Hemorrhoids and TCM: The Spleen, Damp-Heat, and Blood Stasis Connection

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

You know the moment: a sharp sting when you wipe, or that dull, heavy ache that shows up after an hour of sitting. A small lump that makes you wince. The urge to ignore it and hope it goes away — because hemorrhoids are one of those topics nobody wants to bring up at dinner.

The creams and wipes give you a few hours of relief, then the cycle returns. You cut back on spicy food, you try more fiber, you shift uncomfortably in your chair. But the flare-ups keep coming back — and no one has really explained why they started in the first place.

That is the question TCM asks. Instead of focusing only on the swollen tissue at the end of the digestive tract, TCM asks what is creating the pressure, heaviness, or stagnation that makes that tissue bulge in the first place. The answer, in TCM theory, points to three patterns: Spleen Qi sinking (lost lift), Damp-Heat (a swamp pooling below), and Blood Stasis (a river that has stopped moving). Understanding which one sounds like you may point toward habits that offer more than temporary relief.

Why TCM Sees Hemorrhoids Differently

In Western medicine, hemorrhoids are swollen vascular cushions — clusters of veins, muscles, and connective tissue that sit just inside the anal canal. When pressure builds in that area — from chronic constipation, straining, prolonged sitting, pregnancy, or a low-fiber diet — these cushions swell, and the tissue around them becomes irritated. The standard advice is familiar: more fiber, more water, less straining, and for stubborn cases, medical procedures.

TCM agrees with the mechanics but adds a layer. In TCM theory, the anus is the body's lowest "gate" — the end point of the digestive tract's downward flow. For everything to hold its place, something has to be pulling up. That upward force is a function of Spleen Qi, the digestive system's energy. The Spleen's job in TCM is to transform food into Qi and Blood and to hold things in place — muscles, organs, and the tissues of the pelvic floor. The Large Intestine handles transport of waste, and the Liver keeps Qi and Blood flowing smoothly through the whole system.

And for more on how moisture accumulates into Dampness and Damp-Heat, see Dampness in TCM. This article focuses on how these patterns show up in hemorrhoid flare-ups and what may support you day to day.

Think of your body's internal suspension system. When the springs and shocks are healthy, the frame holds its height and absorbs load evenly. When the Spleen's lifting Qi loses tension — what TCM calls Spleen Qi sinking — the structures at the bottom of the body bear more weight than they were designed for. The result can be a heavy, dragging sensation and pressure in exactly the area where hemorrhoids develop. Add moisture that isn't draining well (Dampness) and friction from heat (spicy food, alcohol, inflammation), and you have the classic TCM picture of a hemorrhoid flare-up.

The Three Patterns Behind Most Hemorrhoid Experiences

In TCM theory, most hemorrhoid experiences fit one — or a mix — of three patterns. These are not diagnoses; they are lenses for understanding what your body may be telling you.

Pattern One: Spleen Qi Sinking — The Lost Lift

What it feels like: heaviness and a dragging sensation in the lower pelvis; a feeling of incomplete evacuation; flare-ups that are worse after long periods of standing, heavy lifting, or big meals. Digestion tends to run tired in this pattern — bloating, soft stools, fatigue.

The image: a trampoline that has lost its tension. The frame is fine, but the surface sags, and everything resting on it sits lower than it should.

What tends to aggravate it: overwork, irregular or rushed meals, cold and raw foods, prolonged standing, and physical strain.

Pattern Two: Damp-Heat — The Swamp

What it feels like: swelling, itching, and a moist or burning sensation; flare-ups that follow spicy food, alcohol, or greasy meals; stools that feel sticky or hard to wipe clean. In TCM tongue reading, the coating may look thick and yellowish.

The image: a humid room in midsummer. Moisture alone makes things heavy; add heat and everything swells, sticks, and itches. Damp-Heat is that combination pooling in the lowest part of the body.

What tends to aggravate it: alcohol, chili and curry, fried food, excess sugar, and long stretches of sitting in heat.

Pattern Three: Blood Stasis — The Stuck River

What it feels like: a firm, tender lump; sharper, more fixed pain; the lump may look darker or bluish; discomfort that is worse with sitting. This pattern often appears alongside long-standing constipation.

The image: a river where the current slows. Sediment settles, and stagnant pools form where material builds up. Blood that isn't moving well can pool in the lowest part of the body — and a static pool is exactly where a firm, painful lump takes shape.

What tends to aggravate it: prolonged sitting, chronic straining, very little movement, and cold exposure.

Many people have a combination — for example, a Spleen Qi pattern underneath with a Damp-Heat flare on top. That is normal. The purpose of these patterns is to give you vocabulary, not to put you in a box.

Which Pattern Sounds Like You? A Quick Self-Check

  • Heaviness, dragging, incomplete evacuation, worse after standing → your leaning pattern is likely Spleen Qi sinking. Focus on digestive energy, regular warm meals, and reducing strain.
  • Itching, swelling, burning, moistness, worse after alcohol or spicy food → your leaning pattern is likely Damp-Heat. Focus on cooling, draining foods and cutting the classic fuel sources.
  • A firm tender lump with fixed, sharper pain, worse with sitting → your leaning pattern is likely Blood Stasis. Focus on movement, circulation, and breaking up long sitting sessions.
  • Not sure? The most common starting point is supporting the Spleen and reducing strain — both are safe, gentle directions for most people.
One important rule: any rectal bleeding should be evaluated by a healthcare professional. Hemorrhoids are common, but bleeding can also signal other conditions — and only a doctor can tell the difference.

Eating for Spleen and Damp-Heat Balance

In TCM theory, the kitchen is where patterns are either fed or starved. The goal for hemorrhoid support is twofold: give the Spleen food it can easily transform into Qi, and avoid the foods that feed Damp-Heat.

Foods Traditionally Favored

  • Warm, cooked meals — soups, stews, congee, and steamed vegetables are easier for the Spleen to process than cold, raw food.
  • Spleen-supporting staples — pumpkin, sweet potato, millet, oats, and rice are gentle, grounding carbohydrates in TCM food energetics.
  • Gentle fiber with plenty of water — cooked leafy greens, apples, pears, and oats. Fiber is only half the story; hydration is what lets it do its job.
  • Damp-clearing choices — celery, cucumber, mung bean, barley water, and daikon radish are traditionally used to help the body drain moisture.

Foods to Go Easy On

  • Alcohol and spicy food (chili, curry, hot sauce) — the classic Damp-Heat fuel.
  • Fried and greasy food — heavy for the Spleen and slow to clear.
  • Excess sugar and refined carbs — they feed dampness in TCM theory and inflammation in Western terms.
  • Very cold or raw foods when your digestion already feels weak or tired.
Where the two views meet: fiber and hydration are the twin pillars of hemorrhoid comfort in Western medicine. TCM adds a layer — not just how much fiber, but whether your digestion has the energy (Spleen Qi) to process what you eat. Warm, cooked, regular meals support both goals at once.

Sitz Baths and Gentle External Care: The TCM Approach

Sitz baths are one of the most time-honored practices for hemorrhoid flare-ups, and they fit naturally into the TCM framework: warmth relaxes the area, encourages circulation, and helps soothe swollen tissue.

  • Warm sitz bath: sit in a few inches of warm (not hot) water for 10–15 minutes, two to three times a day — especially after a bowel movement. The water should be comfortable, not scalding.
  • Simple traditional additions: some traditions add a cup of cooled chamomile tea or a splash of witch hazel to the bath water — both are known for gentle, astringent, soothing qualities. Always patch-test and stop if anything irritates.
  • After care: pat the area dry gently — never rub. Wear cotton underwear, skip scented wipes and harsh soaps, and consider unscented wet wipes or a bidet to reduce friction.
  • During acute flare-ups, a cool (not icy) compress for a few minutes can take the edge off swelling.

Three Acupressure Points for Hemorrhoid Support

Acupressure is a self-care extension of the same channel logic TCM practitioners have used for centuries. These three points are among the most commonly cited for the patterns above:

DU-20 (Baihui) — the "raise the lift" point

Location: the very top of the head, at the midpoint of the line connecting the tips of both ears.

Why it is used: in TCM theory, DU-20 is a classic point for raising sinking Qi. When heaviness and dragging dominate, this is the point traditionally reached for. Press with a fingertip in small circles for 1–2 minutes.

BL-57 (Chengshan) — the hemorrhoid classic

Location: on the back of the lower leg, in the soft depression just below the belly of the calf muscle — roughly halfway between the back of the knee and the heel.

Why it is used: BL-57 has a long history of traditional use specifically for hemorrhoid discomfort. Press firmly but comfortably for 1–2 minutes on each leg.

ST-36 (Zusanli) — the digestive workhorse

Location: four finger-widths below the bottom edge of the kneecap, one finger-width to the outside of the shin bone.

Why it is used: ST-36 is the classic point for building digestive energy. Supporting the Spleen and stomach here is a natural fit for the Spleen Qi sinking pattern. Press with moderate pressure for 1–2 minutes per leg.

A note on safety: press gently, and stop if anything feels wrong. Acupressure is a complementary practice — it does not replace a medical evaluation, especially when bleeding is involved.

Bathroom Habits and Daily Movement: Less Strain, Less Stress

The habits around elimination matter as much as anything you eat or press. Straining is the direct mechanical trigger for swollen hemorrhoidal tissue, and TCM sees it as forcing Qi downward when the body is already struggling to hold things up.

  • Don't strain. If nothing is moving, get up, walk around, drink water, and try again later. Force is never the answer.
  • Respond to the urge promptly. Ignoring the signal lets stool dry and harden, which sets up the next round of straining.
  • Don't camp on the toilet. Leave the phone outside the bathroom. A few minutes is plenty; prolonged sitting on the toilet ring increases pressure in exactly the wrong place.
  • Elevate your feet. Resting your feet on a small stool so your knees sit above your hips creates a more squat-like angle that eases passage — some studies suggest this position reduces straining.
  • Move every hour. If you sit for work, stand, stretch, or walk briefly each hour. Movement keeps Qi and Blood flowing and stops the "stuck river" from forming.
  • Consider a cushion for long drives or desk days — a donut or supportive seat cushion takes pressure off the area.

What Research Says About Hemorrhoids and TCM Approaches

Western research has mostly studied individual interventions rather than the TCM pattern framework itself. Here is where the evidence stands:

  • Fiber and hydration: a Cochrane review of clinical trials found that increasing fiber modestly improved hemorrhoid symptoms such as bleeding and itching. This aligns closely with TCM's emphasis on Spleen-friendly, fiber-rich foods eaten warm and regularly.
  • Plant flavonoids: a systematic review of phlebotonic agents — plant compounds like diosmin and hesperidin found in citrus fruits — found they may improve symptoms like bleeding, itching, and discharge. These compounds belong to the same families of colorful fruits that TCM traditionally associates with cooling and astringent qualities.
  • Sitz baths: some small studies suggest warm sitz baths may help reduce symptoms during flare-ups, though the evidence is mixed and larger trials are needed.
  • Acupuncture: a systematic review of randomized trials found preliminary evidence that acupuncture may help reduce hemorrhoid symptoms — though most studies were small, and the authors called for higher-quality research.

One honest caveat: the TCM pattern framework itself — Spleen Qi sinking, Damp-Heat, Blood Stasis — has not been validated in large clinical trials. Think of it as an organizing lens: a way to make sense of your symptoms and choose gentler daily habits, rather than a proven diagnostic system. The habits it points to (fiber, hydration, less straining, more movement) are the same ones Western medicine already recommends.

Practical Takeaways

  1. Find your leaning pattern: heaviness and dragging point to Spleen Qi sinking; itching, swelling, and burning point to Damp-Heat; a fixed, tender lump points to Blood Stasis. Most people have a mix.
  2. Eat warm and regular: soups, cooked vegetables, gentle fiber, and plenty of water. Go easy on alcohol, spice, fried food, and sugar.
  3. Sitz bath daily during flare-ups: 10–15 minutes of warm water, especially after bowel movements.
  4. Change the angle: feet on a small stool, no straining, no lingering on the toilet.
  5. Move hourly: short walks keep Qi and Blood flowing — essential if you sit for work.
  6. Know the red flags: any rectal bleeding deserves a professional evaluation, even if you think it's "just hemorrhoids."

When to See a Doctor

Hemorrhoid symptoms overlap with other conditions, so it is important to know when to seek professional care. See a healthcare provider if you experience:

  • Any rectal bleeding — especially if it is a new change, if the blood is dark, or if blood is mixed into the stool itself
  • A lump that persists, grows, or doesn't settle within a week or two
  • Severe or worsening pain, fever, or chills
  • Changes in bowel habits lasting more than a few weeks
  • Unexplained weight loss or fatigue

Only a qualified healthcare professional can confirm what is going on and rule out more serious conditions. This article is educational and not a substitute for that evaluation.
