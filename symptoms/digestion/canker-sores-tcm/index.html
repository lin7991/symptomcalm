Canker Sores from a TCM Perspective: Mouth Ulcers and Internal Heat

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

It starts as a faint tingle. Then the pinch — you bite into something slightly acidic and your whole mouth seizes up. By evening there's a small white crater on the inside of your lip, and every word, every sip, every brushstroke of toothpaste becomes an exercise in avoidance. You wait. It fades. And three weeks later, a new one appears in a different spot, as if on a schedule.

If this sounds familiar, you're in good company: recurrent canker sores (aphthous ulcers) affect roughly one in five people at some point in their lives. Western medicine calls them recurrent aphthous stomatitis and usually offers numbing gels, steroid rinses, and a shrug about why they keep coming back. TCM has a different question. Instead of asking "how do we numb this ulcer?", it asks "what keeps generating heat in this body?" — because in TCM theory, the mouth is one of the clearest exhaust vents your body has.

This article explains why recurring mouth ulcers are understood as a sign of internal Heat in TCM, walks you through the four patterns most commonly associated with them, and offers traditionally used approaches — foods, teas, acupressure, and daily habits — that may support a calmer mouth between flare-ups.

The Mouth: Your Body's Visible Exhaust Vent

In TCM theory, the mouth is described as the "opening" (qiào, 窍) of the Spleen and Stomach — the visible doorway to your digestive tract. The Stomach channel literally begins at the face, wrapping around the lips and running down through the chest and abdomen. The tongue, meanwhile, is described as the "sprout" of the Heart, and the Heart channel connects to the root of the tongue. In other words: the inside of your mouth sits at the top of two of your body's busiest internal highways.

Now think of your body as a house with an internal climate system. Heat naturally rises — that's why the top floor of a house is always the warmest. In TCM theory, when internal Heat builds up below (in the digestive system) or in the center (around the Heart), it has a natural tendency to rise. The mouth, sitting at the top of the body, is where that heat tends to surface. A canker sore, from this view, is not the problem itself. It's the visible evidence of heat escaping through the top vent.

The Four Patterns Behind Recurring Canker Sores

Not all canker sores are the same, and in TCM the location, trigger, and character of the ulcer point to which pattern is generating it. Here are the four most commonly described patterns. None of them are "the right answer" for everyone — the point of the map is to help you recognize your own recurring pattern.

Pattern 1: Heart Fire Rising (The Overheated Core)

Where it shows up: On or near the tip of the tongue — the classic Heart Fire location in TCM, because the tongue is the Heart's sprout.

What it feels like: Small but intensely painful ulcers. Often accompanied by a restless, racing mind, difficulty falling asleep, irritability, and a feeling of being "wired but tired." You may notice a strong thirst and deeper-colored urine during flare-ups.

What fans the flame: Stress, anxiety, late nights, and emotional heat — anything that keeps the mind running hot. In TCM theory, unexpressed frustration and chronic overthinking generate Heat in the Heart system, and the tongue carries the evidence.

What may help: Cooling, settling foods (cucumber, celery, watermelon, mung beans), calming teas (chrysanthemum, peppermint), and a deliberate wind-down routine before bed. The acupressure point HT-8 (Shaofu, in the palm) is traditionally used to clear Heart Fire.

Pattern 2: Stomach Heat (The Kitchen Running Too Hot)

Where it shows up: On the inner cheeks, lips, or gums — the territory of the Stomach channel.

What it feels like: Larger ulcers that burn. Often paired with bad breath, a strong thirst for cold drinks, a preference for anything cooling, and sometimes acid reflux or a burning sensation after meals. The tongue coating may look yellow.

What fans the flame: Spicy food, fried food, alcohol, coffee, and eating late at night — the classic "heat-generating" habits that overwork the Stomach. In TCM theory, when the Stomach digests more heat than it can process, the excess rises to its opening: the mouth.

What may help: A cooling diet during flare-ups (see the food guide below), cutting spicy/fried food and alcohol for a few weeks, and the acupressure points ST-44 (Neiting, between the second and third toes — the Stomach's clearing point) and LI-4 (Hegu, in the fleshy web of the hand).

Pattern 3: Damp-Heat (The Humid, Sluggish Swamp)

Where it shows up: Anywhere in the mouth, but often multiple ulcers at once, with a swollen, "puffy" feel.

What it feels like: Ulcers that are slow to settle, a sticky or greasy taste in the mouth, a thick coating on the tongue, heavy digestion, bloating, and loose stools. You may feel foggy-headed after meals. In TCM theory, this is Heat combined with Dampness — like a warm, humid room where nothing dries out.

What fans the flame: Rich, oily, sugary foods; heavy meals; alcohol; dairy-heavy diets; and a sedentary pattern that leaves fluids "stuck."

What may help: Light, cooked meals; barley and adzuki bean broth (traditionally used to drain Dampness); green tea or mung bean soup; and daily movement to get things flowing. The points SP-9 (Yinlingquan) and ST-40 (Fenglong) are traditionally used to transform Dampness.

Pattern 4: Yin Deficiency with Empty Heat (The Low-Ember Pattern)

Where it shows up: Small, shallow ulcers that come back again and again — sometimes monthly, sometimes with every stressful period. Often on the tongue or inner lip.

What it feels like: Less intensely painful than Pattern 1, but persistent. Accompanied by a dry mouth (especially at night), low-grade afternoon warmth or flushing, night sweats, fatigue, and a sense of being "drained." The tongue may look red with little or no coating.

What fans the flame: Chronic stress, overwork, sleep deprivation, and years of pushing hard without real rest. In TCM theory, Yin is the body's cooling, moistening reserve — like coolant in a car. When the coolant runs low, the engine doesn't break, but it runs hotter than it should, and that low-grade "empty heat" keeps surfacing in the mouth.

What may help: Genuine rest, earlier bedtimes, and Yin-nourishing foods: cooked pears, black sesame, goji berries, eggs, and bone broths. The points KI-3 (Taixi, inner ankle) and SP-6 (Sanyinjiao, above the inner ankle — avoid during pregnancy) are traditionally used to replenish Yin.

Traditionally Used Approaches That May Support a Calmer Mouth

None of these are a substitute for a dentist or doctor's evaluation — and a persistent or unusual ulcer should always be checked. But as complementary, everyday habits, the following are traditionally used in TCM and some are supported by modern research.

Cool the plate during flare-ups

During an active ulcer, favor foods that are traditionally described as cooling and moistening: cucumber, celery, watermelon, pears, cooked leafy greens, mung bean soup, and congee (rice porridge). Reduce or skip spicy dishes, fried foods, alcohol, coffee, and very acidic foods (citrus, tomatoes) while the ulcer is active — not because they cause canker sores in everyone, but because they're traditionally understood to add fuel to Heat patterns, and they physically irritate an open ulcer.

Simple cooling teas

Chrysanthemum tea is the classic cooling flower tea in TCM — traditionally used to clear Heat and soothe the upper body. Peppermint tea has a cooling, calming character, and green tea contains compounds some studies have associated with reduced oral inflammation. Sip them lukewarm rather than hot; very hot liquids irritate the ulcer surface.

Gentle mouth care (the part most people skip)

Some research has associated sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) — the foaming agent in most toothpastes — with more frequent canker sores in susceptible people. Switching to an SLS-free toothpaste for a few weeks is a low-cost experiment. Use a soft-bristle brush, and avoid aggressive brushing over the ulcer itself. A warm salt-water rinse (half a teaspoon in a cup of warm water, swished gently) is a long-standing traditional comfort measure.

Honey: the oldest wound dressing

Honey has been used topically on wounds for thousands of years, and several small clinical studies have explored it for mouth ulcers — most found that applying honey to the ulcer a few times a day was associated with reduced pain and faster recovery compared with conventional gels. It's worth trying on a clean, dry ulcer. Note: never give honey to infants under one year (botulism risk).

Acupressure points

If you'd like to try acupressure, these points are traditionally used for mouth and Heat patterns. Press each with a fingertip in small circles for 1–2 minutes, one side at a time:

  • LI-4 (Hegu) — in the fleshy web between thumb and index finger. The classic "face and mouth" point; traditionally used to clear Heat from the upper body.
  • ST-44 (Neiting) — on the top of the foot, between the second and third toes. The Stomach channel's clearing point for Stomach Heat.
  • HT-8 (Shaofu) — in the palm, where the tip of the little finger rests when you make a loose fist. Traditionally used to clear Heart Fire.
  • KI-3 (Taixi) — in the depression between the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon. Supports the Kidney's Yin reserve — the "coolant" for the low-ember pattern.
  • SP-6 (Sanyinjiao) — four finger-widths above the inner ankle bone, just behind the shinbone. Nourishes Yin and Blood. Avoid during pregnancy (this point is traditionally contraindicated).

What Modern Research Says

The research on canker sores is patchy — recurrent aphthous stomatitis is common, but its causes are still not fully understood. What exists aligns interestingly with the TCM framework:

  • It's remarkably common: epidemiological studies estimate that roughly 20% of the population experiences recurrent aphthous ulcers, with a higher prevalence in younger adults and women. Recurrence is the defining feature — which is exactly what TCM's "internal Heat keeps regenerating" model tries to explain.
  • Stress is a documented trigger: several studies have reported associations between psychological stress and canker sore outbreaks, and many patients report flare-ups during demanding periods. In TCM terms, that's the Heart Fire and Yin-drain pathways in action.
  • Honey shows promise: a 2014 randomized trial in children and several smaller studies found that applying honey to aphthous ulcers was associated with significantly less pain and faster healing than a conventional topical agent.
  • SLS-free toothpaste: a widely cited trial from the 1990s found that switching to SLS-free toothpaste reduced the number of ulcer days in susceptible individuals, though later studies have been mixed. It remains a low-cost thing to try.
  • Nutrient gaps: some research links recurrent ulcers with lower levels of vitamin B12, iron, or folate — a deficiency pattern that in TCM terms resembles the depleted, undernourished base of the Yin-deficiency pattern. A blood test can rule this in or out.
  • Acupuncture: a small number of preliminary trials have explored acupuncture for recurrent mouth ulcers, with some reporting reduced frequency and duration of outbreaks. The studies are small and varied in quality — think of it as early-stage evidence, not a guarantee.

When to See a Doctor

Most canker sores resolve on their own within 1–2 weeks, and home care is reasonable for the typical small, recurring ulcer. But some mouth ulcers need professional evaluation. See a dentist or doctor if:

  • An ulcer lasts longer than 2 weeks without improvement
  • Ulcers are unusually large (bigger than about 1 cm), spreading, or bleeding
  • You have difficulty eating, drinking, or swallowing
  • The ulcer is accompanied by fever, skin rash, or joint pain
  • You develop ulcers after starting a new medication
  • Mouth ulcers come with persistent digestive symptoms (diarrhea, abdominal pain, blood in stool) — this combination can point to conditions like celiac disease or inflammatory bowel disease that need a proper work-up

Persistent ulcers that don't follow your usual pattern, or ulcers in someone who doesn't normally get them (especially after age 50), also warrant a check. And note: cold sores are different from canker sores — they're caused by the herpes virus, appear on the outside of the lip, and need a different approach entirely.

Practical Takeaways:
  • Map your pattern: where the ulcer appears (tongue tip = Heart Fire, cheeks/gums = Stomach Heat), what triggers it (stress vs. food), and what co-signs come with it (insomnia, bad breath, dry mouth, bloating).
  • Cool the plate during flare-ups: cucumber, celery, watermelon, mung bean soup, congee; skip spicy, fried, and acidic foods while the ulcer is active.
  • Try the low-cost experiments: SLS-free toothpaste, a soft brush, warm salt-water rinses, and honey applied to a clean ulcer a few times a day.
  • Add cooling teas: lukewarm chrysanthemum or peppermint tea during flare periods.
  • For the low-ember pattern: if ulcers track your stress and sleep, the most TCM-aligned intervention is rest — earlier bedtimes, and Yin-nourishing foods like cooked pears, black sesame, and goji berries.
  • Rule out nutrient gaps: a simple blood test for B12, iron, and folate can eliminate a common underlying cause.
