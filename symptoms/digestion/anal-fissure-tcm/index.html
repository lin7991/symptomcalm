Anal Fissure: A TCM View of the Tear That Won't Close

Symptom Guide · 8 min read

You know the feeling before it happens. A bowel movement you have started to dread, a sharp, tearing pain like a paper cut in the worst possible place, a few bright red streaks on the tissue — and then a dull, burning ache that can last for hours afterward. It gets better for a week, you relax, and then it happens again. Same place. Same time. Same sharp, searing moment.

An anal fissure is a small tear in the lining of the anal canal, and it is one of the most common reasons people see a colorectal specialist. It is also one of the most quietly miserable: not dangerous in most cases, not something people talk about, and stubbornly repetitive because of where it sits. A cut on your arm closes because it can rest. A tear in a place that gets stretched, opened, and squeezed every single day has almost no chance to rest at all.

The honest starting point: Anal fissures are a medical condition with well-established care — plenty of fluids and fiber, warm sitz baths, topical agents prescribed by your doctor (such as nitroglycerin or diltiazem ointment), and procedures such as botulinum injection or lateral internal sphincterotomy for fissures that stay chronic. Anything below is complementary support, not a substitute. And any rectal bleeding deserves a medical assessment, because bleeding has several possible causes and only a clinician can tell them apart.

Why This Tear Doesn't Close the Way Other Cuts Do

Picture a cut on the knuckle of your finger. It would close in a few days — if you could keep the finger still. Now imagine bending that knuckle twenty times a day, hard. The cut would reopen before it could ever finish repairing. That is the anatomy of a fissure: a wound on a hinge that will not stop moving.

There is a second layer to it. The internal anal sphincter — the ring of muscle around the canal — tends to tighten in response to the pain. That spasm squeezes the small blood vessels that feed the area, which reduces blood flow to the very tissue that needs to repair itself. Less blood flow means slower repair; slower repair means the tissue stays fragile; fragile tissue tears again. The cycle explains why a fissure that is two weeks old can behave exactly like a fissure that is two days old, and why the pain often lasts long after the bowel movement is over.

TCM's contribution to this picture is not a different anatomy — it is a different set of questions. Instead of asking only "how do we stop the tear?", TCM asks: what is making this tissue dry or inflamed, and what is keeping the local Qi and Blood from moving enough to repair it? Those two questions map onto the patterns below.

Three TCM Patterns Behind a Fissure That Won't Close

As always in TCM, a single diagnosis can show up in different internal patterns. Most people recognize themselves in one or two of these, and the picture shifts with stress, diet, and bowel habits. Read them as observation prompts, not as a diagnosis.

Pattern 1: Intestine Heat With Dry Stool (The Parched Riverbed)

Key features: Hard, dry, or pellet-like stools; straining at the toilet; bright red blood on the paper; a burning sensation; thirst; a dry mouth or dry lips; sometimes a red face or feeling overheated; a strong or offensive odor; a yellow coating on the tongue.

Feels like: A riverbed baked dry by heat — the channel has lost its moisture, so what should pass smoothly scours the bank on the way through.

Traditionally used support: More fluids, especially warm water through the day; moistening fruits and vegetables (pear, prune, spinach, okra, cooked leafy greens); reducing very spicy, fried, and heavily grilled food during a flare; avoiding long stretches of sitting. Acupressure: LI-11 and ST-36 (below).

Pattern 2: Spleen Qi Deficiency With Damp-Heat Pouring Downward (The Damp Basement)

Key features: Urgent, loose, or sticky stools that are hard to pass cleanly; burning or itching around the opening; a heavy, boggy feeling; a coated tongue; feeling hot and damp rather than simply dry; symptoms that flare with alcohol, rich food, or a stressful week.

Feels like: A damp basement that keeps spreading — heat and moisture pooling low, keeping the area irritated and slow to settle.

Traditionally used support: Warm, lightly cooked meals; reducing alcohol, sugar, fried food, and dairy as an experiment; barley and adzuki bean soups; keeping the area dry and gently cleaned (pat, do not scrub); acupressure at SP-9 and ST-36.

Pattern 3: Liver Qi Stagnation With Blood Stasis (The Clenched Fist)

Key features: A tight, guarded feeling in the pelvis; pain that lingers or aches for hours after a bowel movement; symptoms that track with stress, travel, or a busy season; a tendency to hold tension in the jaw, shoulders, or gut; a sensation of incomplete emptying; pain that feels fixed and always in the same spot.

Feels like: A fist that never quite opens — the tissue is not being damaged by dryness, it is being held too tightly, and the local circulation is paying for it.

Traditionally used support: Breathing practice with long exhales before and during a bowel movement (deliberately unclenching); physical movement that opens the pelvis (walking, gentle hip circles, squatting); reducing stimulants in the evening; acupressure at LI-4 and BL-57. Stress-related gut patterns are also covered in our guide to Liver Attacking the Spleen.

One more consideration sits across all three patterns: Qi and Blood deficiency, the TCM description of tissue that lacks the resources to rebuild. Long-standing fissures, poor sleep, low food intake, or heavy menstrual bleeding can leave the body described as depleted — and in TCM theory, a depleted body repairs slowly regardless of what caused the original tear.

The Sitz Bath: Where TCM and Modern Care Agree

Of everything in this article, the sitz bath is the one place where traditional and modern advice are almost identical — and it is also the most immediately useful thing you can do at home.

Warm water (comfortably warm, not hot) in a shallow basin or a bath, sitting for 10–15 minutes, once or twice a day and especially after a bowel movement. Plain water is enough. No soap, no added salts, no essential oils — anything that stings on broken skin works against you. Some people find a handheld shower head gentler than sitting in a basin.

Modern medicine explains the benefit partly through relaxation of the sphincter and improved local blood flow. TCM explains it as warmth moving Qi and Blood, resolving stagnation, and easing the constriction that keeps the area stuck. Different language, same practical instruction: warm water, regularly, without additives.

Acupressure Points Traditionally Used for Pain and Pelvic Tension

Acupressure here is gentle pressure with a fingertip or thumb — 1–2 minutes per point, both sides, once or twice daily. It is traditionally used to ease pain and tension, and as self-care support alongside medical care, never instead of it.

  • BL-57 (Chengshan) — "support the mountain." On the back of the calf, in the hollow just below the belly of the calf muscle, about halfway between the knee crease and the ankle. This is the classic point TCM associates with the anal region and with hemorrhoids; press firmly but comfortably.
  • LI-4 (Hegu) — "the joining valley." On the back of the hand, in the web between thumb and index finger, at the highest point of the muscle when you pinch the thumb and finger together. Traditionally used for pain and for moving stagnation. Note: LI-4 is avoided in pregnancy.
  • LI-11 (Quchi) — "pool at the bend." At the outer end of the elbow crease, with the arm bent. Traditionally used to clear Heat — useful for the hot, inflamed, burning presentation.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — "three miles." Four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width outside the shin bone. Traditionally used to strengthen the Spleen's processing, which matters most when stools are loose, urgent, or hard to pass cleanly.
  • SP-6 (Sanyinjiao) — "the meeting of three yin." About four finger-widths above the inner ankle bone, just behind the edge of the shin bone. Traditionally used for the lower digestive and pelvic region — but this point is avoided in pregnancy, along with LI-4.

Do not press on or near the fissure itself. If you are pregnant, or taking blood-thinning medication, check with your clinician first — some points are avoided in pregnancy, and pressure is not appropriate everywhere.

Softening the Exit: Fluids, Fiber, and Food Direction

The single most useful dietary principle for a fissure is unglamorous: keep the stool soft enough to pass without stretching the tear. TCM adds a direction to that principle — moistening where things are dry, cooling where things are hot, and drying where things are damp.

  • Fluids, warm and steady. Small amounts through the day rather than large volumes at once. Warm water is traditionally preferred over iced drinks, which are described as slowing and contracting the digestive system.
  • Soluble fiber, gently. Psyllium, oats, cooked greens, okra, and pear are the usual starting points. Increase slowly — a sudden fiber jump can produce gas and harder stools before it produces softer ones.
  • Moistening foods (for the dry, heat pattern). Pear, prune, honey in warm water, spinach, sesame, and light broths.
  • Drying foods (for the damp, burning pattern). Barley, adzuki beans, mung beans, celery, and tangerine peel (chen pi) in soups.
  • What to pull back during a flare. Very spicy food, alcohol, heavy fried food, and excessive coffee are the common aggravators — not because they cause fissures, but because they can make stools hotter, looser, or more irritating while tissue is trying to settle.
  • Regular meals, regular rhythm. Skipping meals and chaotic eating schedules make bowel habits erratic, and erratic bowels are harder on a healing fissure than predictable ones.

The Bathroom Reset: Five Habits That Give the Tissue a Chance

Mechanical habits matter at least as much as diet here, and they cost nothing.

  1. Do not strain, ever. Pushing increases pressure and reopening risk. If nothing is moving after a couple of minutes, get up and try later.
  2. Use a footstool. Raising the feet so the knees come above the hips (a squat-like position) reduces the effort needed to pass a stool. It is the simplest structural change available.
  3. Keep it short — under five minutes. Extended sitting puts sustained pressure on the area. The phone is the main culprit; leave it outside the bathroom.
  4. Do not delay when the urge comes. Holding on makes the stool drier and harder, which is exactly what you do not want.
  5. Clean gently and dry thoroughly. Pat with water or a plain damp cloth; skip fragranced wipes and soap, and dry with a pat rather than a rub. Moisture and fragrance both keep irritated skin irritated.

Add a long-exhale breathing practice to the routine: slow breathing with an exhale twice as long as the inhale, done for three or four breaths before and during a bowel movement. It sounds trivial, but deliberately unclenching the pelvic floor is one of the few things you can consciously control in this cycle.

What Modern Research Suggests

Nothing here replaces medical assessment. It is included because the overlap with the traditional advice is genuinely notable in this condition.

  • Fiber and fluids reduce recurrence. Adding fiber — with adequate fluid — is a standard first-line measure for fissures, and trials in chronic fissure care suggest that a softer, bulkier stool lowers the chance of the tear reopening after a course of treatment.
  • Warm sitz baths help pain. Warm-water sitz baths are widely recommended and studied, particularly after childbirth and after anal surgery, with reported reductions in pain and improvements in comfort. The mechanism usually cited is relaxation of the sphincter and improved local blood flow.
  • Sphincter spasm and blood supply. The leading modern explanation for chronic fissures is exactly the loop described above: internal sphincter hypertonicity reduces blood flow to the posterior canal, impairing repair. This is the rationale behind topical nitroglycerin and diltiazem — both relax the sphincter and improve perfusion.
  • Pelvic floor tension. Studies of pelvic floor muscle function in people with anal pain report higher resting tension and altered coordination in a subset of patients — a measurable version of the TCM "clenched" pattern, and the reason breathing and relaxation practices are included rather than dismissed.
  • Acupuncture for anal pain. Small trials have examined acupuncture or acupressure for pain after hemorrhoid surgery and for chronic anal pain, with generally favorable but low-quality evidence. Best read as "may support comfort" rather than an established effect.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ See a doctor promptly — and do not manage this at home — if you have rectal bleeding for the first time, bleeding that is dark or mixed with stool, bleeding that continues after the fissure pain settles, a fever, discharge or pus, severe or worsening pain, or a fissure that has not settled after six weeks. A fissure that keeps returning, or one accompanied by changes in bowel habit, weight loss, or a family history of colorectal concerns, needs proper evaluation.

Rectal bleeding always has more than one possible cause, and only a clinician can distinguish them. TCM is a complementary approach and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  • Warm sitz baths, twice daily. 10–15 minutes, plain water, especially after a bowel movement. This is the highest-value habit in this article.
  • Keep the stool soft. Steady warm fluids, slowly increased soluble fiber, and regular meals. Softer stools mean less reopening.
  • Change the mechanics. No straining, a footstool, under five minutes, no phone, no delaying the urge.
  • Match the food direction to your pattern. Moistening foods (pear, prune, spinach) if things feel dry and hot; drying foods (barley, adzuki, mung bean) if things feel damp and burning.
  • Work on pelvic tension deliberately. Long-exhale breathing before a bowel movement, plus BL-57 and LI-4 acupressure for comfort.
  • Get bleeding checked. Not because it is usually serious — because it occasionally is, and only a doctor can tell.
