Uterine Prolapse: A TCM View of Sinking Pelvic Support

Symptom Guide · 9 min read

It shows up slowly, and almost always in the same way: a heavy, dragging sensation low in your pelvis by the end of the day. Something that feels like it has shifted, or is sitting lower than it used to. A fullness you notice most when you have been standing for hours, lifting, or walking further than usual. By evening you want to simply lie down — and lying down, oddly, makes it better.

That last detail is the one that sends many women searching. Rest helps, but the pattern returns. Maybe your doctor has already used the words "pelvic organ prolapse," "uterine descent," or offered a pessary, pelvic floor therapy, or surgery. Perhaps you have been told it is mild and to come back if it gets worse. Either way, the question underneath is the same: why does my body feel like it is losing its grip on something?

In Traditional Chinese Medicine, that question has a specific and surprisingly practical answer. Prolapse is not read as a local mechanical failure alone. It is read as a loss of lift — a system-wide loss of upward support that also shows up in your digestion, your energy, and how your body handles gravity all day long.

Before we dive in: this article focuses specifically on uterine prolapse (and its close neighbours in the pelvic organ family) as a lived experience — the sensations, the self-assessment, and the daily practices. It does not re-explain the underlying TCM concept. For that foundation, see our guides to Sinking Spleen Qi and the TCM view of pelvic health. If your main symptom is leaking rather than descent, our article on urinary incontinence covers that track in depth.

What Uterine Prolapse Actually Is

In conventional medicine, pelvic organ prolapse describes what happens when the muscles, fascia, and ligaments that form the pelvic floor and its supporting web become stretched, weakened, or damaged — and one or more pelvic organs settles downward into, or through, the vaginal canal. When the uterus itself descends, it is called uterine prolapse.

Clinicians usually describe it in grades, roughly:

  • Grade 1 — the uterus sits slightly lower than usual, but is still well inside the vagina. Often nothing is visible; you may feel nothing at all, or only occasional heaviness.
  • Grade 2 — the uterus has descended to the opening of the vagina. Heaviness, dragging, and sometimes a bulge you can feel with a finger.
  • Grade 3 — the uterus protrudes outside the vaginal opening.
  • Grade 4 — the uterus is completely outside the body (procidentia).

Common contributing factors include pregnancy and vaginal birth (especially multiple births, long second-stage labour, or instrumental delivery), chronic constipation and straining, chronic coughing, regularly lifting heavy loads, connective tissue differences, previous pelvic surgery, and the drop in oestrogen after menopause that thins and weakens supporting tissues. Many women present with a combination.

It is also worth naming, gently but clearly: prolapse is common. Studies suggest a substantial proportion of women who have given birth show some degree of pelvic organ descent on examination, and many have no symptoms at all. It is not a personal failing, and it is not something you should quietly endure.

A Different Lens: The Body's Internal Suspension System

TCM approaches the pelvis less as a set of separate parts and more as a structure held up by an active upward force. Think of a suspension bridge: a central tower, cables under tension, and a deck resting on those cables. The cables do not hold anything up by being rigid — they hold it up by being tensioned. Lose the tension and the deck sags, even though nothing has been torn.

In TCM theory, the Spleen is responsible for that tension — the quality of "holding things in their proper place." When this upward-holding function weakens, the classical description is Spleen Qi sinking: a pattern where the body's support structure loses lift and everything at the bottom of the body (the pelvic floor, the organs above it) begins to feel heavy and low. This is not a metaphor TCM invented for prolapse — it is a general pattern that also shows up as tired digestion, a tendency to feel worse after standing, haemorrhoids, and varicose veins. Our companion guide explains how sinking Qi develops and feels; here, we focus on what it means specifically for a uterus that has descended.

Two other organ systems complete the picture, and the three together are what make the TCM map useful:

  • The Kidney is the deep foundation — the anchor, and the source of the "grasping" force that holds the lower burner closed and stable.
  • The Spleen is the lift — the active tension that keeps organs in position against gravity.
  • Damp-Heat is what happens when fluid that should be moving downward and out instead pools and irritates, adding weight and inflammation to an already low, tired area.

Lifting alone does not describe every case, and a foundation problem alone does not either. Which of these three dominates changes what will actually help you day to day — so the next two sections are designed to help you recognise your own version.

Reading Your Own Symptoms: Two Useful Lenses

Before any self-care makes sense, two questions matter more than the rest. The first is how low the descent is. The second is what kind of low — the quality of the sensation. Most women can answer both without any equipment.

Lens one: how far, and how much does it interfere?

You do not need to grade yourself — that is what a clinician's examination is for. But the practical question is how much the prolapse is limiting your life, because that is what guides urgency:

  • Descent with no symptoms. Often found incidentally. Monitoring, plus habits that reduce strain, is usually the focus.
  • Descent with daily heaviness and dragging. Usually worse by late afternoon, better lying down. The most common presentation, and the one where daily management pays off most.
  • Descent that interferes with urination or bowel movements, causes a sensation of a lump outside the body, or makes you avoid activities you used to do. This is the point to have a proper assessment rather than manage alone.

Three Patterns, Up Close

Pattern 1: Spleen Qi Sinking — The Lost Lift

What it feels like: a dragging, downward sensation in the lower belly that builds through the day and eases when you lie down. A feeling that something is sitting lower than it should, and that your whole body has simply run out of upward energy by mid-afternoon.

Often accompanied by: tired digestion — bloating after meals, soft or loose stools, a sensation of incomplete bowel movements; low-grade fatigue; a tendency to feel worse after standing for long periods or after eating heavily; sometimes haemorrhoids or heavy, aching legs.

Why it happens: the Spleen's holding function runs on the energy it derives from digestion. When that energy is thin — from poor food quality, irregular meals, chronic overwork, or long-term worry — the body's tension system slackens. The uterus, like the pelvic floor and the veins in the legs, is simply the structure that shows the loss most clearly.

What traditionally supports it: warm, cooked meals eaten on a schedule; raising the body's lift with DU-20 (Baihui, on the crown of the head) — traditionally used for sinking Qi patterns; avoiding breath-holding during exertion; reducing prolonged standing; and a supportive, non-straining bowel routine.

Pattern 2: Kidney Qi Deficiency — The Loosened Foundation

What it feels like: a deep, dull ache low in the back and pelvis rather than a surface heaviness. Worse when you are tired, better after proper rest — not just a nap, but sustained recovery. A sense that your reserves are genuinely low.

Often accompanied by: frequent urination, especially at night; cold knees or feet; lower back weakness; feeling older than your years after periods of stress, poor sleep, or successive pregnancies; sometimes hair thinning or a sense of being "depleted."

Why it happens: in TCM theory, the Kidney governs the lower burner, the pelvic region, the bones, and the capacity to hold and retain. Where the Spleen provides the lift, the Kidney provides the anchor — the deep foundation the whole suspension system is bolted into. Repeated births, chronic sleep debt, long-term stress, and the natural decline of reserves with age all thin out that foundation.

What traditionally supports it: protecting sleep and recovery first; warming practices for the lower body (moxibustion is traditionally used for cold, weak, sinking pictures — see our guide to moxibustion for pelvic support); nourishing, slow-cooked food; and acupressure at CV-4 (Guanyuan) and KI-3 (Taixi).

Pattern 3: Damp-Heat Pouring Downward — The Heavy Swamp

What it feels like: weight and downward pressure combined with irritation — discharge, odour, a raw or hot sensation, sometimes itching or a feeling of stickiness in the area. The downward sensation is present, but so is a sense of heat or congestion.

Often accompanied by: symptoms that flare clearly after alcohol, spicy food, fried food, or sugar; sticky or sluggish digestion; a heavy feeling in the body in humid weather; darker urine or a burning sensation when urinating.

Why it happens: Damp-Heat is the TCM pattern for fluid that has become thick and hot instead of clear and mobile. Because heat and heaviness both tend to move downward, the pelvis is where this pattern collects. In this case the problem is not a lack of lift alone — it is an added burden of congested, irritated tissue sitting on an already tired support structure.

What traditionally supports it: cooling and draining foods (celery, cucumber, barley, mung bean, leafy greens); reducing alcohol, fried food, and heavy spice; keeping the area dry with breathable cotton and changing out of damp clothing promptly; and avoiding the instinct to "push through" with more exercise and stimulants. This pattern is also the one that most warrants a clinical assessment, because discharge and irritation can have other causes that need to be ruled out.

Note on pregnancy and postpartum: gentle acupressure is generally considered safe in the postpartum period, but some points — notably SP-6 (Sanyinjiao) and LI-4 (Hegu) — are traditionally avoided during pregnancy because they are considered strongly moving. If you are pregnant, skip those two points and check with your healthcare provider before starting any point work. If you are newly postpartum, wait until you have been cleared for activity.

Supportive Practices: Working With Gravity Instead of Against It

Every practice below is designed around one idea: reduce the downward load, restore upward support, and stop adding strain to a structure that is already working hard. In TCM terms this is the sequence — remove the weight, then rebuild the lift.

1. Breathing and pressure management

This is the highest-yield habit, and it costs nothing. The fastest way to push your pelvic organs downward is to hold your breath while straining — lifting something, pushing a heavy door, or bearing down on the toilet. Instead, breathe out through the effort: exhale as you lift, exhale as you rise from a chair, exhale instead of holding.

Pair it with a simple daily practice: sitting or lying comfortably, breathe in gently and feel the pelvic floor soften and lengthen; breathe out slowly and feel a light, natural lift — never a hard clench. Two or three minutes a day. In TCM terms, this is movement of Qi downward and upward in balance. In Western terms, it is training coordinated pressure management with the diaphragm rather than against it.

2. Bathroom mechanics and lifting habits

  • Respond to the urge promptly. Delaying creates harder stools, and straining is one of the most direct ways to push downward.
  • Do not camp on the toilet. Leave the phone outside. Long sitting with gravity acting on an open pelvic floor is time spent in exactly the position you are trying to reduce.
  • Raise your feet. A small stool so the knees sit slightly above the hips creates a more squat-like angle, which some studies suggest reduces the effort needed.
  • Change how you lift. Bend the knees, keep the load close, exhale through the lift, and split heavy loads into two trips. For a while, delegate what you can.
  • Break up standing. If your work is on your feet, take a seated or lying pause every hour. Women with a sinking-Qi picture often notice the difference within a week.

3. Acupressure for a sense of lift

Gentle pressure — one to two minutes per point, firm but never painful — is traditionally used to support the lifting and anchoring functions. Do not press on a full bladder, and skip point work during pregnancy as noted above.

  • DU-20 (Baihui) — crown of the head, midpoint between the ear tops. The classical point for sinking Qi: it is believed to raise the body's lift.
  • CV-6 (Qihai) — about two finger-widths below the navel. Traditionally used to strengthen Qi in the lower burner.
  • CV-4 (Guanyuan) — about four finger-widths below the navel. The classical point for the deep foundation; traditionally used for pelvic support.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — below the knee, one finger-width outside the shin bone. The digestive workhorse that supports Spleen Qi.
  • KI-3 (Taixi) — the hollow between the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon. The Kidney source point, for the anchor.

4. Food and drink: warming the middle, lightening the load

For a sinking-Qi picture, the traditional emphasis is warm, cooked, easily digested food eaten at regular times — soups, stews, congee, well-cooked root vegetables, oats, squash, and modest amounts of good-quality protein. Cold drinks, raw salads as a main meal, and long gaps between meals all ask more of a digestion that is already working hard to produce the energy you need for support.

If Damp-Heat features, shift toward lighter and more draining choices: barley, celery, cucumber, leafy greens, green tea; and reduce alcohol, fried food, sugar, and heavy spice for a few weeks to see whether the irritation settles.

Whichever pattern dominates, fibre and adequate fluid matter — not from a TCM angle but from a plain mechanical one, because soft stools mean less straining, and less straining means less downward pressure on the pelvic floor.

5. Movement: train the cables, and support the tower

Pelvic floor muscle training remains the most studied non-surgical approach for prolapse symptoms, and it deserves its reputation. But we would add one TCM-informed nuance: tightening the cables will only get you so far if the tower itself is depleted.

  • Pelvic floor training — ideally with a pelvic health physiotherapist who can check you are contracting effectively rather than bearing down. A gentle daily practice beats an aggressive weekly one.
  • Gentle, whole-body movement — walking, unhurried qigong-style breathing and movement, or a slow yoga practice keeps Qi moving without loading the pelvis heavily.
  • What to pause — heavy loaded squats, breath-held abdominal work, and high-impact jumping are usually not the right starting point while symptoms are active. Ask a clinician before returning to them.

What Research Suggests

Modern research does not map perfectly onto TCM patterns, but a few findings are worth knowing because they line up surprisingly well with the traditional picture:

  • Pelvic floor muscle training. Systematic reviews and Cochrane analyses suggest supervised pelvic floor muscle training may improve prolapse symptoms and related quality-of-life measures, especially for mild to moderate descent. This matches the TCM emphasis on restoring holding function rather than only addressing the visible symptom.
  • Pessaries. A review of observational studies suggests vaginal pessaries may reduce prolapse symptoms for many women, with ring and Gellhorn pessaries being common choices. In TCM framing, a pessary is external support for a structural problem — useful, and complementary to work on the underlying lift.
  • Acupuncture and moxibustion. Small clinical studies and reviews have explored acupuncture and moxibustion for pelvic organ prolapse and related pelvic floor weakness, with some reports of symptom improvement. Most trials are small and of limited quality, so these findings are best read as preliminary signals rather than proof.
  • Straining and pelvic floor load. Observational work links chronic straining from constipation and repeated heavy lifting with pelvic floor disorders, which supports the very practical TCM advice to reduce downward strain rather than simply push through it.
  • Traditional herbal formulas. Formulas such as Bu Zhong Yi Qi Tang ("Tonify the Middle and Augment the Qi Decoction") have been traditionally used for sinking-Qi patterns, including organ prolapse, for centuries. Clinical evidence is limited, and herbal formulas should only be used under the guidance of a qualified practitioner — especially alongside other medications.

When to See a Doctor

Please seek medical attention if you experience any of the following:

  • Tissue you can see or feel outside the vaginal opening, or a bulge that cannot be gently pushed back
  • Sores, ulceration, bleeding, or persistent raw discomfort in the prolapsed tissue
  • Difficulty passing urine, a weak or interrupted stream, or a feeling that your bladder does not empty
  • Difficulty passing a bowel movement, or needing to press on the vaginal wall to complete one
  • Fever, foul-smelling discharge, or pelvic pain that is new, severe, or worsening
  • Sudden worsening of symptoms, or symptoms that interfere with walking, working, or everyday activity

Prolapse is a structural issue that deserves a proper assessment — examination, and where appropriate, pelvic floor physiotherapy, a pessary, or discussion of surgical options. TCM is a complementary perspective: it may support comfort, digestion, energy, and daily function alongside your medical care, but it does not replace evaluation, and it is not a substitute for emergency care.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  • Name your pattern. Dragging heaviness points to the lost lift; deep back-and-pelvic ache with low reserves points to a loosened foundation; weight with irritation or discharge points to Damp-Heat. Start with whichever is loudest.
  • Never hold your breath under load. Exhale through every lift, push, and rise. This single habit reduces the most common daily downward pressure.
  • Rebuild the middle. Warm, cooked meals on a regular schedule, eaten unhurriedly — plus fibre and enough fluid so that stools stay soft and straining stops.
  • Work the lift and the anchor. One to two minutes each on DU-20, CV-6, CV-4, ST-36, and KI-3 (skip SP-6 and LI-4 if pregnant).
  • Train with a professional, not alone. Pelvic floor muscle training works best when someone checks that your effort is lifting rather than bearing down.
  • Rest is part of the programme, not a failure of it. Lying down for ten minutes mid-afternoon is not giving in — it is removing the load so the support can recover.

Prolapse can feel like a quiet betrayal of a body you used to be able to trust. But a sagging structure is not a broken one — it is one that has lost tension, and tension can be rebuilt. Understanding which force has weakened in your case turns a vague, frightening sensation into a set of small, manageable daily decisions.
