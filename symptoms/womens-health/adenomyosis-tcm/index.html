Adenomyosis from a TCM Perspective: Heavy Periods and Blood Stasis

Symptom Guide · 7 min read

You know the drill. Day two of your period, and you're changing a super-plus tampon plus a pad every couple of hours. You've bought dark sheets so the stains don't show. The cramps aren't the kind that pass with a heating pad — they're deep, heavy, and they radiate down your thighs and into your lower back. Your gynecologist says your ultrasound shows an "enlarged uterus" but no fibroids. "It's probably just adenomyosis," they say. Then they hand you a hormonal prescription and a "let's wait and see."

If that scene feels familiar, you're not alone. Adenomyosis affects an estimated 1 in 5 women of reproductive age, yet it's one of the most under-diagnosed conditions in gynecology — partly because its symptoms (heavy bleeding, painful cramps) overlap with ordinary period problems, and partly because it only shows clearly on MRI or specialized ultrasound. Many women live with it for years before getting a name for what's happening.

Here's the thing TCM has to offer: a framework that takes the heavy bleeding seriously as its own signal — not just "bad cramps." In TCM theory, the hallmark of adenomyosis is a specific kind of stagnation lodged in the wall of the uterus itself, and that changes how you think about support. This article explains the TCM view, the patterns most commonly associated with adenomyosis, and the traditional self-care approaches you can discuss with a licensed practitioner.

What Is Adenomyosis? A Quick Western Overview

In Western medicine, adenomyosis is defined by location: the tissue that normally lines the inside of the uterus (the endometrium) starts growing into the muscular wall of the uterus (the myometrium). Over time, this embedded tissue thickens the uterine wall, making the uterus enlarged, tender, and less elastic — sometimes described on scans as a "boggy" uterus.

The two classic symptoms follow directly from that anatomy:

  • Heavy, prolonged bleeding — because the thickened, stiffer wall can't contract as effectively during menstruation, the uterus struggles to shed the lining and clamp down on the blood vessels the way it normally would.
  • Deep, cramping pelvic pain — often worst right before and during the period, sometimes radiating to the lower back and thighs. Pain during intercourse and a feeling of fullness or heaviness in the pelvis are also reported.

Diagnosis typically requires transvaginal ultrasound or MRI, where doctors look for a thickened "junctional zone" — the border between the lining and the muscle wall. There's no single blood test for it, which is one reason it's so often missed or delayed.

The TCM View: Blood Stasis Lodged in the Uterine Wall

Traditional Chinese Medicine has no historical term for "adenomyosis" — the diagnosis didn't exist until modern imaging. But TCM has a very well-developed framework for what adenomyosis looks like from the inside: Blood Stasis (Xue Yu, 血瘀) lodged in the Uterus (Zi Gong, 子宫).

New to Blood Stasis? For a broader overview of what Blood Stasis is, how to recognize it, and what research says about it, see our TCM Basics article on Blood Stasis in TCM. This article focuses specifically on how that concept plays out in the uterine wall with adenomyosis — the patterns, the self-care, and the heavy-bleeding angle.

Think of the uterus as a house. The endometrium is the wallpaper — a layer that grows, thickens, and sheds each cycle. In adenomyosis, the wallpaper starts growing into the walls themselves, burrowing into the brick and mortar. The wall thickens, stiffens, and can't flex the way it should. In TCM terms, that wall is now holding stagnant Blood — tissue that has lost its normal rhythm of growth and release.

Why does this matter? Because Blood Stasis in the uterine wall explains both of adenomyosis's signature symptoms at once:

  • The heavy bleeding: a wall stiffened by stagnation can't squeeze properly. In TCM language, the Qi (energy) and Blood of the Uterus have lost their smooth, coordinated movement — so the monthly "letting go" becomes a flood instead of a controlled release.
  • The deep pain: stagnant Blood is classically associated with fixed, sharp, stabbing pain in one location — exactly the quality of adenomyosis cramps, which stay put in the lower abdomen rather than moving around.

The key TCM insight is that Blood Stasis is rarely a random event. Something creates the stagnation in the first place — and in adenomyosis, the usual suspects are the same four patterns you'll meet below.

Adenomyosis vs Endometriosis: Same Family, Different Address

Adenomyosis and endometriosis are often described as sisters — sometimes "the evil twins" — because they share the same core mechanism: endometrial-like tissue growing where it shouldn't. The difference is the address.

  • Endometriosis: tissue grows outside the uterus — on the ovaries, fallopian tubes, and the pelvic lining. In our house analogy, the wallpaper spreads into the garden and the neighbor's yard.
  • Adenomyosis: tissue grows inside the uterine wall itself. The wallpaper grows into the bricks of the house — no farther, but deeply embedded.

The two conditions often co-exist (studies suggest roughly a third to half of women with adenomyosis also have endometriosis), and in TCM both are rooted in Blood Stasis. But the symptom profiles differ in a way that matters for self-care: endometriosis leans toward pain and fertility challenges, while adenomyosis leans toward heavy, prolonged bleeding — because the affected tissue is right in the muscle that controls the flow.

Key TCM Patterns Associated with Adenomyosis

In TCM, the same diagnosis can look different in different bodies. A practitioner looks at the combination of your symptoms — the quality of your bleeding, your pain, your digestion, your energy, your temperature preferences — to identify which pattern (or mix of patterns) is running the show. These are the ones most commonly associated with adenomyosis:

1. Blood Stasis in the Uterine Wall (子宫血瘀)

Signs: Heavy bleeding with dark, clotted blood — the clots may be large. Pain that is fixed in one spot, sharp and stabbing in quality, and worse during the period. A feeling of pressure or fullness in the lower abdomen. Some women notice the pain is worse at night or with rest. The tongue may look purple or have small dark spots, and the pulse may feel choppy or wiry.

TCM view: This is the core pattern of adenomyosis — Blood that should move smoothly through the Uterus has become stuck in the wall. The embedded tissue is both a product and a source of the stagnation: it blocks normal flow, and the blocked flow feeds the tissue. The body's monthly attempt to shed the lining meets resistance, producing the classic combination of flooding and fixed pain.

Traditional approach: Move Blood and resolve Stasis — gently, because the bleeding is already heavy. Herbal formulas in the Gui Zhi Fu Ling Wan (桂枝茯苓丸, "Cinnamon Twig and Poria Pill") family are classically associated with stagnant Blood in the pelvic region and have a long history of use for uterine masses and congestion. Acupuncture and acupressure points that move Blood, like SP-10 (Xuehai, "Sea of Blood") and SP-6 (Sanyinjiao), are commonly included.

2. Qi Deficiency Failing to Hold Blood (气虚不摄)

Signs: Bleeding that is heavy and prolonged — sometimes dragging on for 8, 10, or more days. The blood may be lighter in color with smaller clots. Overwhelming fatigue before, during, and after the period. A pale face, poor appetite, bloating, and loose stools. You feel like the period "takes everything out of you" — and it does, because heavy bleeding drains Blood, and Blood is what carries energy.

TCM view: In TCM theory, Qi holds Blood in its vessels the way a well-stretched net holds water. When Spleen Qi — the body's digestive engine that produces Qi and Blood — is deficient, the "net" loses tension. The uterine wall can't grip and contract, and the result is bleeding that doesn't know when to stop. This pattern is especially common after months or years of heavy periods: the chronic blood loss creates the very deficiency that makes the next period heavier.

Traditional approach: Build Qi and Blood while gently moving any stasis. This is a "tonify first" pattern — formulas in the Bu Zhong Yi Qi Tang (补中益气汤, "Tonify the Middle and Augment Qi Decoction") or Gui Pi Tang (归脾汤, "Restore the Spleen Decoction") families are traditionally associated with Spleen support. Food therapy is central: warm, cooked, easily digestible meals; bone broths; cooked dark leafy greens; and adequate protein.

3. Cold Congealing Blood (寒凝血瘀)

Signs: Severe cramping pain that feels better with heat and worse with cold exposure. Dark blood with clots, sometimes described as "thick" or "tar-like." Cold hands and feet, a strong preference for warm drinks, a lower abdomen that feels cold to the touch during the period. Symptoms flare after swimming in cold water, wearing thin clothing in winter, or a season of iced drinks.

TCM view: Cold contracts and congeals — think of how cold thickens oil or slows a stream. When Cold has settled in the pelvic region, it makes an already-stagnant uterine wall even stiffer. The cramps are the body trying to push Blood through a channel that cold has narrowed. This pattern is a common overlay on top of the core Blood Stasis of adenomyosis.

Traditional approach: Warm the Uterus and dispel Cold while moving Blood. Heat is the classic ally here: warm compresses, hot water bottles, moxibustion (burning dried mugwort near the lower abdomen — a technique for a trained practitioner), and warming foods like ginger, cinnamon, and black pepper. Herbal formulas in the Wen Jing Tang (温经汤, "Warm the Menses Decoction") family are traditionally used for Cold-related pelvic pain.

4. Liver Qi Stagnation with Blood Stasis (气滞血瘀)

Signs: Pain and bleeding that flare with stress. Premenstrual worsening — breast tenderness, irritability, a "lump in the throat" feeling, sighing frequently. The pelvic pain may feel distending or pressing rather than purely stabbing. Symptoms noticeably worse in high-stress months, and somewhat better when flow begins.

TCM view: The Liver governs the smooth, free movement of Qi throughout the body. When stress, frustration, or repressed emotion blocks that movement, Qi stalls — and since Qi is the force that moves Blood, stalled Qi leads directly to stalled Blood. In adenomyosis, stress acts as a kind of traffic jam on top of an already-congested route: it tightens the uterine wall further and makes both pain and flow worse.

Traditional approach: Move Qi and soothe the Liver alongside addressing Blood Stasis. LV-3 (Taichong) — the classic Liver point on the foot — is a staple. Formulas in the Xiao Yao San (逍遥散, "Free and Easy Wanderer") family, sometimes combined with Blood-moving herbs, are traditionally associated with stress-driven menstrual discomfort. Daily movement, breathwork, and anything that gives stress a release valve are considered part of the prescription.

Why the Bleeding Is So Heavy: The Wall That Can't Squeeze

If you've ever been told "heavy periods are just how you are," it's worth understanding the mechanics — because it explains why TCM keeps coming back to the uterine wall.

Normally, when your period starts, the uterine muscle contracts in waves to shed the lining, then clamps down on the small blood vessels to control the bleeding. That clamping is the "squeeze." In adenomyosis, the wall is thicker, stiffer, and scarred by embedded tissue — it can't squeeze as well. The blood vessels stay open longer, and the result is the flood: heavy flow, big clots, and periods that overstay their welcome.

Western research points to related mechanisms — elevated prostaglandins (compounds that drive uterine contractions and inflammation) and altered contractility of the myometrium — both of which map neatly onto the TCM picture. A wall that can't contract = Qi failing to move and hold. A wall that's thickened and congested = Blood Stasis. The two descriptions are different languages for the same physiology.

Why this matters for support: If the problem is a stiff, stagnant wall, then support that warms, moves, and relaxes the pelvic region — heat, gentle movement, acupressure, stress reduction — addresses the mechanism, not just the symptom. And support that builds Qi and Blood helps the body recover from the monthly drain, so the next cycle starts from a stronger place.

Self-Care Approaches from a TCM Perspective

These are traditional self-care approaches — not medical recommendations — that some women explore alongside conventional care. Always check with your doctor before starting anything new, especially if you're taking medication or have other health conditions.

Acupressure You Can Do at Home

Acupressure applies the same points as acupuncture, using firm finger pressure instead of needles. Two to three minutes of small, slow circles per point, once or twice a day, is a typical home practice:

  • SP-10 (Xuehai, "Sea of Blood") — on the inner thigh, about two thumb-widths above the top of the kneecap. The classic "move Blood" point, traditionally used for menstrual pain, heavy bleeding, and skin conditions.
  • SP-6 (Sanyinjiao) — four finger-widths above the inner ankle bone, just behind the shinbone. The meeting point of the Spleen, Liver, and Kidney channels — a cornerstone of women's health. Skip this point during pregnancy.
  • LV-3 (Taichong) — on the top of the foot, in the hollow between the first and second toe bones. Moves Liver Qi and eases stress-related flare-ups.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli) — four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width to the outside of the shinbone. The classic Qi-building point, helpful for the fatigue that follows heavy bleeding.
  • CV-4 (Guanyuan) — on the midline, about three finger-widths below the navel. Traditionally used to warm and support the lower abdomen. Use gentle palm pressure or a warm compress here rather than hard pressing.
Important: If you are pregnant or trying to conceive, some acupoints — especially SP-6 and LI-4 — are traditionally avoided during pregnancy. Always tell your practitioner about any pregnancy plans, and have a qualified TCM practitioner show you the points before relying on self-applied acupressure.

Heat: The Stagnant Wall's Best Friend

For the Cold and Stasis patterns, warmth is medicine. A warm compress or hot water bottle on the lower abdomen during your period helps relax the uterine muscle — the "squeeze" works better when the wall isn't tight with cold. A warm bath or a short warm foot soak can also help. Moxibustion (a trained practitioner's technique of warming acupoints with burning mugwort) is a traditional option for cold-type pelvic pain, but it's not a DIY first step.

Food as Support

TCM food therapy for adenomyosis-style patterns centers on two jobs: warming and moving, and rebuilding what's lost.

  • Warm and cook your food. Soups, stews, cooked grains, and room-temperature water support a wall that's stiff from Cold and Stasis. Raw salads, iced drinks, and frozen desserts are minimized, especially in the week before and during your period.
  • Move with spices. Ginger, turmeric, cinnamon, and black pepper are traditionally used to promote circulation and warmth. A ginger tea during your period is both warming and soothing.
  • Rebuild Blood. Because heavy periods drain Blood, TCM emphasizes Blood-nourishing foods: cooked dark leafy greens (spinach, kale), beets, black beans, goji berries, bone broth, and adequate protein. These support the body's reserves between cycles.
  • Lighten the inflammatory load. While not a traditional TCM concept, many modern practitioners note that reducing refined sugar, processed foods, and excessive dairy supports the whole picture.

Movement That Doesn't Fight the Wall

Gentle, rhythmic movement — walking, easy stretching, qigong, tai chi — helps Qi and Blood keep flowing through the pelvic region. During heavy flow days, the goal is circulation, not exertion: a slow walk beats an intense workout. Movement also gives stress (the Liver Qi pattern) a release valve, which may translate into fewer stress-driven flares.

What the Research Says

Research on adenomyosis has grown rapidly as imaging has improved, but research on TCM for adenomyosis specifically is still early-stage. Here's a fair summary:

  • Diagnosis and prevalence: MRI studies identify adenomyosis in roughly 20% of women having hysterectomies, and a thickened junctional zone (often defined as 12 mm or more on MRI) is the standard diagnostic criterion. This is why the condition is so much better understood now than a generation ago.
  • Acupuncture for period pain: A Cochrane review of acupuncture for primary dysmenorrhea found that it may reduce period pain compared with no intervention, though the evidence quality was limited. For adenomyosis specifically, studies are smaller, but some clinical trials report reduced pain scores and improved quality of life with acupuncture — encouraging but preliminary.
  • Gui Zhi Fu Ling Wan: This classical formula for pelvic Blood Stasis has been the subject of clinical studies in adenomyosis and related conditions. Some trials report improvements in pain and heavy bleeding, but most are small, and the Cochrane review of Chinese herbal medicine for endometriosis-like conditions noted that methodological limitations make firm conclusions difficult.
  • Herbal medicine for heavy menstrual bleeding: Systematic reviews of Chinese herbal medicine for menorrhagia generally report symptom improvement compared with conventional care, while consistently flagging the need for larger, higher-quality trials.
  • Prostaglandins and the myometrium: Research shows elevated prostaglandin levels and altered uterine contractility in adenomyosis — a physiological echo of the TCM picture of a wall that's congested and can't squeeze properly.

None of this is a substitute for gynecological care. TCM approaches are best used as complementary support alongside proper medical management — including monitoring for iron deficiency, which heavy bleeding frequently causes.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:
  1. Get a proper diagnosis first. If your periods are consistently heavy or painful, push for transvaginal ultrasound or MRI — adenomyosis is frequently missed. Rule out fibroids, endometriosis, and other structural causes with your gynecologist before assuming anything.
  2. Check your iron. Heavy bleeding commonly causes iron deficiency and anemia. A simple blood test tells you whether you need supplementation — discuss this with your doctor, because anemia worsens fatigue and can make everything feel heavier than it already is.
  3. Track the pattern, not just the pain. Note the color and size of clots, the number of heavy days, your energy, your stress level, and what makes things better or worse. This is gold for both your gynecologist and any TCM practitioner.
  4. If you see a TCM practitioner, bring the imaging. A good practitioner will want your ultrasound or MRI report and should work in collaboration with your gynecologist — not in place of them.
  5. Think in cycles, not single fixes. In TCM, shifting a long-standing pattern like Blood Stasis in the uterine wall takes time — typically several months of consistent support, with special attention to the week before and during your period.

When to See a Doctor

⚠️ Adenomyosis requires proper medical diagnosis and monitoring, and heavy bleeding can become a medical emergency. Seek care immediately if you experience any of the following:

  • Soaking through a pad or tampon every hour for two or more consecutive hours
  • Bleeding that lasts longer than 7 days, or a period so heavy you can't do your normal activities
  • Passing clots larger than a quarter repeatedly, or sudden gushes of blood
  • Signs of anemia: severe fatigue, dizziness, shortness of breath, pale skin, or a racing heart
  • Sudden, severe pelvic or abdominal pain
  • Pain with fever, chills, nausea, or vomiting
  • Pain during intercourse that is new or worsening
  • Difficulty becoming pregnant after 12 months of trying (or 6 months if you're over 35)

Heavy bleeding is not "just how you are." It's worth a proper evaluation — both for your quality of life and for your long-term health. TCM is a complementary approach that may support the picture, but it does not replace gynecological care, hormonal management when indicated, iron replacement, or surgical evaluation when needed.
