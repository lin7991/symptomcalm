Moxibustion for Respiratory Health: Warming the Lungs Through Cold Season

Treatment Guide · 7 min read

You know the rhythm: the first cold snap of the year, and within a week your chest feels tight, your nose runs when you step outside, and the cough that "just needs a few days" stretches into the whole season. You've tried the humidifier, the vitamin C, the extra layers — and each helps a little. But every winter, the same pattern returns.

In TCM, this seasonal vulnerability has a specific story to tell. The Lungs are the body's most exposed organ — the first structure the outside air touches. And in TCM theory, cold weather is precisely what weakens the system that guards them. This is where moxibustion enters: a traditional practice of warming specific points on the body with a smoldering mugwort stick. Its role in respiratory care is not about fighting an active infection — it's about warming the Lungs' defenses before and during the cold season, so the system doesn't go into winter underpowered.

Before we begin: This article explains the TCM perspective on moxibustion for respiratory wellness. It is educational content, not medical advice. Moxibustion is not a replacement for standard respiratory care — including medications prescribed for asthma, COPD, or other diagnosed conditions. If you have a diagnosed respiratory condition or are experiencing difficulty breathing, chest pain, or a high fever, see a healthcare provider first.

Why Moxibustion for Respiratory Health? The Warming Logic

Here's the counterintuitive part: most people associate respiratory problems with heat — fever, inflammation, a burning chest. So why would anyone add more warmth?

In TCM thinking, the issue is not heat inside the chest — it's cold at the perimeter. Think of the body as a house. The Lungs are the front door and the air-filter system combined: every breath brings the outside world directly into contact with your internal environment. The TCM concept of Wei Qi (protective Qi) is the security detail stationed at that door — a defensive layer that patrols the surface of the body, especially where the body meets cold air first: the nose, the throat, the upper back, and the skin.

Western bridge: There is a physiological parallel. Cold air constricts blood vessels in the nasal passages and upper airways, which can slow the movement of mucus and the activity of the immune cells that patrol those surfaces — one reason respiratory complaints cluster in cold months. Warming the surface of the body — especially the upper back and chest — is a traditional strategy for supporting the body's ability to stay warm and keep those defenses active.

When Wei Qi is strong, cold air arrives at a well-defended door. When Wei Qi is weak — after a poor night's sleep, a stressful month, or a pattern of getting chilled repeatedly — the door is left ajar. Moxibustion's role in TCM is to recharge the security system: warming specific points that are traditionally understood to strengthen Lung Qi and Wei Qi, so the body enters the cold season with its defenses at full strength.

The TCM Logic: Lungs, Wei Qi, and the Cold Season

Three TCM concepts explain why respiratory health dips in winter — and why warming is the traditional response:

  • The Lungs are the most exposed organ. In TCM, the Lung is called the "tender organ" — it sits at the top of the body, receives the first breath of every environment you enter, and governs the surface (skin and body hair) as part of its defensive role. No other organ interfaces with the outside world as directly. For a deeper look, see our guide to the Lungs in TCM.
  • Wei Qi patrols the perimeter. Wei Qi is the body's protective layer — the "defensive Qi" that circulates on the surface, guarding against external influences like cold and wind. It is closely tied to the Lungs: the Lung governs the surface, and Wei Qi is its field force. When Lung Qi is strong, Wei Qi patrols thoroughly; when Lung Qi is depleted, the perimeter thins out. Our TCM Basics article on Wei Qi covers this concept in full.
  • Cold contracts and slows. In TCM theory, Cold is a contracting influence — it tightens, slows, and blocks. When cold enters the respiratory system, it can tighten the chest, slow the movement of fluids (making phlegm heavier and stickier), and reduce the circulation of Qi at the surface. The traditional remedy is the opposite quality: warmth, which moves, opens, and circulates.

This is why the same person can sail through a mild autumn and then collect every bug of winter: the external challenge (cold) intensifies exactly when the internal defense (Lung Qi and Wei Qi) is most taxed. Moxibustion addresses the second half of that equation — the strength of the defense itself.

Three Respiratory Patterns Moxibustion May Fit (and the One It Doesn't)

Moxibustion for respiratory health is not one-size-fits-all. In TCM thinking it suits cold, depleted, under-defended patterns — and can aggravate hot, inflamed ones. Here's how to tell which is which.

Lung Qi Deficiency — The Low-Defense Pattern

  • What it feels like: You catch every cold that goes around; your voice is quiet or easily tired; you feel short of breath after mild exertion (climbing stairs, carrying groceries); a low-level cough that lingers for weeks after an illness; pale complexion; you feel the cold easily and dislike drafts.
  • Why moxibustion fits: This is the underpowered security system. Lung Qi is the fuel for the body's respiratory defenses, and warming the points associated with Lung Qi is traditionally used to replenish that fuel. In TCM terms, moxibustion here supports the "gathering Qi" — the energy that fills the chest and powers steady breathing.
  • Where the warmth goes: LU-7 and BL-13 (both detailed below) — the Lung channel's key points for strengthening the system from within.

Cold-Phlegm — The Fogged-Pipes Pattern

  • What it feels like: A chest that feels heavy or "stuffed" rather than sharp or burning; phlegm that is clear, white, or foamy; symptoms that feel worse in cold weather and better with warmth; a wet-sounding cough; cold hands and feet accompanying the congestion.
  • Why moxibustion fits: In TCM, this is Cold congealing fluids — like a pipe system where cold weather makes the contents thicker and slower-moving. Warmth is the traditional counter: it thins and moves the fluids, helping the chest "clear out" rather than staying clogged. Moxibustion at the chest and upper back points is the classic warming strategy for this pattern.
  • Where the warmth goes: CV-17 on the center of the chest (the "Sea of Qi" point) and BL-13 on the upper back.

Kidney Yang with Lung Qi — The Deep-Winter Stamina Pattern

  • What it feels like: The classic "deep winter" profile: cold lower back, cold feet, frequent nighttime urination, low energy through the afternoon, and a breathing pattern that feels shallow or unsupported — as if each breath doesn't quite reach the bottom of the lungs.
  • Why moxibustion fits: In TCM theory, the Kidney "grasps the Qi" — it anchors the breath at the bottom, so breathing isn't just a chest action but a full-body one. When Kidney Yang runs low, the body loses its internal furnace, and the breath loses its anchor. Warming the lower back and lower abdomen is traditionally used to support this foundation — a long-term strategy for winter resilience rather than a quick fix.
  • Where the warmth goes: CV-4 on the lower abdomen and the lower back region around BL-23 (Kidney Back-Shu), often with ST-36 on the legs as a supporting point.

The Pattern Moxibustion Does NOT Fit — Heat and Active Infection

  • What it looks like: A fever; yellow, green, or dark phlegm; a sore throat that is hot and swollen; a cough that produces thick colored mucus; night sweats; a red tongue or feeling constantly warm. Also — and this is critical — any active respiratory infection with a fever.
  • Why moxibustion is a poor fit: In TCM terms, these are Heat patterns. Adding warmth to an already-hot system is traditionally described as "adding fuel to a fire" — it can worsen the inflammation rather than support the body. If your symptoms are hot, acute, or colored, moxibustion is not the right tool right now.
  • What to do instead: Rest, fluids, and standard medical care for active infections. For non-infectious Heat-type congestion, a cooling approach (like cupping for respiratory support) may be a better fit — or see a qualified practitioner for guidance.

Where the Warmth Goes: Respiratory-Specific Points

These are the points most commonly recommended for warming moxibustion in a respiratory context. Each has a specific role in the respiratory-support chain.

LU-7 (Lieque) — The Master Point of the Lung Channel

Location: On the inside of the forearm, about one and a half thumb-widths above the wrist crease, in the hollow just above the styloid process of the radius.

Why it matters for respiratory health: LU-7 is traditionally considered a key point for the Lung channel's dispersing and descending functions — the movements that spread protective Qi to the surface and keep breath flowing downward rather than "rebelling" upward into coughing. In TCM terms, warming LU-7 supports the Lung's ability to do its daily job of moving Qi and guarding the surface.

How to use: Hold a lit moxa stick 2–3 cm from the point, moving in small circles, for 5–10 minutes per side. The sensation should be warm and comfortable — never burning.

BL-13 (Feishu) — The Lung Back-Shu Point

Location: On the upper back, one and a half finger-widths lateral to the lower edge of the spinous process of the third thoracic vertebra (approximately at the level of the top of the shoulder blades).

Why it matters: Back-Shu points are the "back ports" of each organ — the places where a practitioner traditionally accesses the organ's Qi from behind. BL-13 is the Lung's back port, and it is the point most commonly warmed in respiratory-focused moxibustion. Warming the upper back here is like opening a service hatch on the ventilation system: it lets warmth reach the Lungs directly, from the side of the body that faces the cold air first.

How to use: Because this point sits on your own back, it's easiest to warm with a moxa box or with a partner holding a moxa stick. A moxa box placed over the upper back for 15–20 minutes is a classic home setup — see our moxibustion home guide for equipment details and safety.

CV-17 (Danzhong) — The Sea of Qi

Location: On the center of the sternum, level with the nipples — roughly the midpoint of the breastbone.

Why it matters: CV-17 is traditionally called the "Sea of Qi" — the meeting point of the respiratory energies in the chest. In TCM thinking, this is where the body's gathering Qi collects, and where chest tightness from constrained breathing often registers. Warming CV-17 is the traditional response to a chest that feels heavy, tight, or "not quite full" — the point works like clearing the central plaza so traffic can move again.

How to use: Moxa stick, 5–10 minutes, gentle circles over the sternum. Keep the moxa moving and the sensation comfortably warm.

ST-36 (Zusanli) — The Foundation Point

Location: Four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width outward from the shin bone (tibia), in the depression of the tibialis anterior muscle.

Why it matters (shared anchor): ST-36 is the workhorse of warming therapies — the point most often used to build overall Qi and Blood. In a respiratory context it plays a supporting role: in TCM theory, the Spleen (which ST-36 strengthens) produces the "Food Qi" that the Lungs combine with air to create the body's gathering Qi. Warming ST-36 is like ensuring the fuel supply line is full, so the Lungs have raw material to work with all winter.

How to use: Moxa stick, 5–10 minutes per leg. This is also the safest point for beginners to practice on — it's easy to locate and far from the face and chest.

A Cold-Season Moxibustion Protocol

Here's a practical weekly rhythm for using moxibustion as seasonal respiratory support — not for acute illness, but for the weeks leading into and through the cold season.

  • Weeks 1–2 (Foundation): ST-36 on both legs + LU-7 on both forearms, 3–4 sessions per week, morning or early afternoon. This builds the baseline: strengthening Lung Qi and the fuel supply.
  • Weeks 3–4 (Add the back): Add a moxa box over the upper back (BL-13 region) for 15–20 minutes, 2–3 times per week. This is the direct-access step — warming the Lungs from behind, where cold air hits first.
  • Ongoing maintenance: 1–2 sessions per week through the cold season, rotating: one session on ST-36 + LU-7, one session with the upper back moxa box. If your profile matches the deep-winter pattern above, add CV-4 on the lower abdomen or a lower-back moxa box (BL-23 region) in the maintenance phase.
  • Timing: Morning and early afternoon are considered "Yang hours" in TCM — the natural window for warming therapies. Avoid moxibustion late in the evening, when the body is shifting toward rest.
  • Aftercare: Drink warm water or ginger tea after a session; avoid cold drinks and cold baths for an hour; rest quietly for 10–15 minutes; make sure the moxa is fully extinguished (water, then a sealed metal container) before disposal.

Listen to your body: A warming adjustment period — feeling tired or slightly warm after early sessions — can be normal. But if a session makes you feel overheated, restless, or noticeably worse, reduce the frequency and duration. "More" is not better with moxibustion; the goal is a comfortable, steady warmth.

What the Research Says

Research on moxibustion for respiratory health is growing, though the evidence base is still preliminary and much of it comes from China and other parts of Asia:

  • Moxibustion and COPD: Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have examined moxibustion as an add-on to conventional care for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. A 2019 meta-analysis in Medicine pooled randomized trials and found that moxibustion combined with standard treatment was associated with improved lung function measures (including FEV1) and exercise capacity compared to standard treatment alone. The authors noted that most trials were small and called for larger, higher-quality studies.
  • Moxibustion for asthma: A 2017 systematic review in Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine examined moxibustion for asthma and found that several small trials reported improvements in symptom scores and lung function when moxibustion was added to conventional treatment. The review emphasized the preliminary nature of the evidence.
  • Warming therapies and the upper back: Studies on moxibustion at back points (including BL-13) report that the approach is generally well tolerated, with the main adverse events being local skin irritation or mild burns from improper technique — highlighting why technique and temperature control matter.
  • Cold and the airways: Research on cold air and respiratory physiology supports the TCM premise that cold challenges the airways: cold air can reduce nasal mucosal temperature, slow mucociliary clearance, and increase the susceptibility of the upper airways to viral infection. This is the physiological backdrop for a warming strategy.

Research note: These findings are promising but preliminary. Moxibustion studies often have small sample sizes, variable methodology, and are concentrated in specific regions. Think of moxibustion as one layer of a respiratory-support strategy — alongside sleep, nutrition, and standard medical care — not as a substitute for any of them.

Using Moxibustion Safely

  • Never during a fever or acute infection. If you have an active respiratory infection with fever, moxibustion is not appropriate — it may worsen the condition. Wait until you are fully recovered.
  • Skip it for Heat patterns. Yellow or green phlegm, hot sore throat, night sweats, feeling constantly warm — these suggest a Heat pattern where additional warmth is traditionally considered counterproductive.
  • Keep it comfortable. The sensation should be warm, never burning. Keep the moxa stick 2–3 cm from the skin and keep it moving. A moxa box should be checked periodically for temperature.
  • Protect the skin. Moxibustion should never be used on open wounds, over inflamed or infected skin, or on areas with reduced sensation (where you might not feel a burn developing). People with diabetes affecting sensation, or anyone with reduced skin sensitivity, should consult a healthcare provider before attempting moxibustion at home.
  • Pregnancy: If you are pregnant, consult your healthcare provider before using moxibustion, and avoid the lower abdomen and lower back.
  • Heat-sensitive constitutions: If you feel hot easily, tend toward constipation, or have a red tongue, use moxibustion sparingly or consult a qualified practitioner first.
  • Follow home-safety basics: Never leave lit moxa unattended; use a fire-safe holder; extinguish thoroughly (water, then sealed metal container); keep away from flammable materials and children or pets. Our home moxibustion guide covers equipment and safety in detail.

When to See a Doctor

Seek medical care promptly if you experience: difficulty breathing or shortness of breath at rest; chest pain or pressure; a fever above 101°F (38.3°C) that persists; coughing up blood; phlegm that is yellow, green, or dark and worsening; or symptoms that do not improve after a week or that keep coming back. Moxibustion is a complementary wellness practice — it does not replace medical evaluation for respiratory symptoms, and any diagnosed respiratory condition (asthma, COPD, and others) should be managed by your healthcare provider.

Practical Takeaways

  • Moxibustion for respiratory health is a seasonal strategy, not an acute remedy. Its traditional role is warming Lung Qi and Wei Qi in the weeks before and during the cold season — not treating an active infection.
  • Match the warmth to the pattern. It fits cold, depleted profiles: catching every cold, clear or white phlegm, feeling cold easily, shallow breath, low stamina. It does not fit Heat: fever, yellow or green phlegm, hot sore throat, or any acute infection.
  • Use the respiratory point set. LU-7 and BL-13 for the Lungs directly, CV-17 for chest tightness, ST-36 for the foundation, and CV-4 or the lower back for the deep-winter stamina pattern.
  • Follow the seasonal rhythm. Foundation weeks on ST-36 and LU-7, then add the upper-back moxa box, then 1–2 maintenance sessions weekly. Morning and early afternoon are the traditional windows.
  • Safety is non-negotiable. No moxibustion during fever or active infection; comfortable warmth only; protected skin; thorough extinguishing; and professional consultation for pregnancy, diabetes, or heat-sensitive constitutions.
