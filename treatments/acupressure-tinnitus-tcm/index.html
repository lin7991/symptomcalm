Acupressure for Tinnitus: Points for Ringing Ears

Treatment Guide · 8 min read

It is quiet in the room, and that is exactly when you hear it. A thin high tone in one ear. A hiss like escaping steam. A ring that rises the moment the world goes silent and somehow fades when you are busy — which makes you wonder whether the sound is really in your ear or in your attention. Roughly one in six adults lives with some form of tinnitus, and for a smaller group it is loud enough to interfere with sleep, concentration, and mood.

Western medicine has precise explanations for the sound: damaged hair cells in the cochlea, age-related hearing loss, the auditory cortex turning up its internal gain to compensate for missing input, and the brain's alarm system deciding that the noise is a threat worth monitoring. TCM does not dispute any of that. It asks a slightly different question — why is this particular ear producing that particular sound right now? — and offers hands-on tools that can sit alongside medical care. Acupressure is the most accessible of them: no needles, no prescription, just steady thumb pressure on specific points while you breathe.

Before we dive in: This guide focuses specifically on the acupressure protocol itself — which points, in what order, how to press them, and how to choose between them. Why Pressing Your Hand Can Affect Your Ear

Think of the body's channel network as a fiber-optic system. Every point on the network is a terminal, and every terminal is wired back to something bigger. The ear happens to sit at an unusually busy junction: three major channels — Small Intestine, Gallbladder, and Triple Burner — travel around and through the region, and in TCM theory the Kidney system is said to "open into the ears," meaning the ear is treated as the Kidney's sensory window.

That gives you two very different ways to reach the ringing, and they explain why the protocol below uses points on your hand, foot, and the base of your skull as well as points around the ear itself.

Doorway points (local)

The points right around the ear are like the door hinge of a noisy room. They do not settle the whole house, but they can quiet the immediate area — releasing the tightness and congestion that keep the sound amplified. They are also the fastest to notice, which is why they open every sequence.

Supply-line points (distal)

Points on the wrist, foot, and ankle sit on the same channels as the doorway points, far upstream. In TCM thinking, a kink near the source shows up as noise at the far end — like a plumbing restriction that makes a tap rattle two rooms away. Working upstream may change the pressure the far end receives.

Western researchers describe something adjacent to this when they study how stimulation at the wrist or ankle can influence attention and descending gating of unpleasant signals. The two frameworks are not the same thing, and it is fair to say the mechanism question is genuinely open. What is not open is that pressing these points is low-risk for most people and takes ninety seconds.

Which Kind of Ringing Do You Have? A 60-Second Self-Check

TCM does not have one answer for tinnitus; it has several patterns, and the point selection changes with them. Read the four rows below and see which sounds most like your ear. Most people recognize themselves in one, sometimes two.

Kidney type: the dimming speaker

Imagine a speaker that fades into a hum when the power supply sags. In TCM theory, the Kidneys hold your deepest reserve, and the ears are their sensory window — so a slow decline in that reserve is associated with a high, steady tone that is worst when there is nothing else to listen to. The lever here is not force; it is refilling. Sleep, warmth, adequate protein, and time in bed earlier than you think you need.

Liver Yang type: pressure in the pipes

When stress keeps the system pressurized, the pressure has to go somewhere, and the head is the top floor. This pattern tends to arrive suddenly, often one-sided, and it follows your stress curve rather than your sleep curve — loud in a tense meeting, quiet on vacation. The lever is downward movement: walk, exhale longer than you inhale, unclench the jaw, and press points that move stagnation.

Phlegm-Heat type: a blocked vent

A vent that is both clogged and warm does not move air well, and everything in the room feels heavy. This pattern shows up with a plugged, cotton-wool sensation, and it usually tracks digestion — heavier after rich food, lighter after simple meals. The lever is less input and better drainage: lighter meals, less alcohol, more movement after eating.

The Local Points: Around the Ear

These four points sit within a two-inch circle of your ear. Their location trick is the same: you are looking for a soft hollow, never a hard bone. Pressing them open each sequence because they are the fastest to register.

SI-19 (Tinggong — "Palace of Hearing")

Where: directly in front of the ear canal. Open your mouth slightly — a hollow appears right there. How: index fingers in both hollows, gentle circular pressure for 30 seconds. This is the classic local point for the ear in TCM literature.

GB-2 (Tinghui — "Meeting of Hearing")

Where: just below SI-19, in the depression between the ear canal and the jaw joint. Open and close your mouth to feel the soft spot move. How: press with fingertips angled slightly up and inward for 30 seconds. Useful when jaw tension accompanies the ringing.

TW-17 (Yifeng — "Wind Screen")

Where: behind the earlobe, in the hollow between the jawbone and the mastoid bone. How: press inward and slightly upward with your index fingertips for 20–30 seconds. Traditionally used for ear-related complaints in the Triple Burner channel that circles the ear.

GB-12 / the earlobe itself

Where: the shallow hollow behind the ear where the skull meets the neck (GB-12), plus the earlobe. How: 20 seconds of pressure at GB-12, then massage the earlobe between thumb and index finger for 30 seconds. In TCM the ear is treated as a microsystem of the whole body, so general ear massage is a reasonable warm-up before point work.

The Distal Points: Hands, Feet, and the Base of the Skull

These are the supply-line points. They take longer to learn to find, but they are where the pattern-specific work happens — which is why they are worth two minutes of your routine.

GB-43 (Xiaxi — "Pinched Stream")

Where: on the top of the foot, in the groove between the fourth and fifth toes, just behind the web. How: press firmly with your thumb for 30–60 seconds per side. This is the point most often cited in classical TCM texts for ringing in the ears, and it belongs to the channel that circles the ear. A strong candidate if your ringing is one-sided or stress-linked.

TW-5 (Waiguan — "Outer Gate")

Where: two finger-widths above the wrist crease on the back of the forearm, in the channel between the two forearm bones. How: press toward the bone with your opposite thumb for 30 seconds per arm. Traditionally described as releasing the region between the ears and the shoulders.

LI-4 (Hegu — "Joining Valley")

Where: the fleshy web between thumb and index finger, on the highest point of the muscle when the thumb is drawn in. How: firm pressure with the opposite thumb for 30 seconds, both hands. The general-purpose point for the head and face. Skip it if you are pregnant — see the caution below.

KI-3 (Taixi — "Great Stream")

Where: the hollow between the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon. How: press firmly for 1–2 minutes per side while breathing slowly out. In TCM, source points are where an organ system's original Qi pools — KI-3 is the source point of the Kidney channel, making it the anchor if your pattern is the depletion type.

LV-3 (Taichong — "Great Surge")

Where: on top of the foot, in the depression between the first and second toes, about two finger-widths back from the web. How: 1 minute per side, pressing in a direction that feels like a slow release rather than a stab. Best used when your ringing follows your stress level.

GB-20 (Fengchi — "Wind Pool")

Where: at the base of the skull, in the two hollows between the neck muscles. How: thumbs in the hollows, press upward into the skull base for 30 seconds. This is the point that connects neck tension to head and ear symptoms, and it is nearly always the first point in the sequence that follows.

Two more worth knowing: ST-40 (Fenglong), halfway between the knee and the ankle, two finger-widths outside the shin bone — the classical point associated with Phlegm; and PC-6 (Neiguan), two finger-widths above the inner wrist crease between the two tendons — a calming point for the throat and chest that also pairs well with the muffled, plugged sensation.

Pregnancy caution: LI-4 and SP-6 are traditionally avoided during pregnancy, and strong pressure on either is generally discouraged. If you are pregnant, skip both and ask your healthcare provider before starting any acupressure routine. The same applies if you have a bleeding disorder, are on blood-thinning medication, or have a skin condition near a point.

Two Protocols: The 90-Second Reset and the Daily Build

Acupressure works on two timescales, and mixing them up is why people give up. One is a reset you use when the sound spikes. The other is a build you repeat for weeks. They feel similar; they are doing different jobs.

Protocol 1: The 90-second reset

Use this when the ringing suddenly gets louder, when you are lying awake at 3 a.m., or when you notice you are clenching your jaw against the noise. Work top to bottom — the idea is to release the top of the channel, open the doorway, then pull the signal down toward the hands and feet.

  1. GB-20 (base of skull) — 20 seconds per side. Thumbs in the hollows, gentle upward pressure. Exhale slowly as you press.
  2. SI-19 + GB-2 (in front of the ear) — 30 seconds total. Mouth slightly open. Small circles. Keep the pressure mild — 4 out of 10, not 8.
  3. TW-5 (outer forearm) — 20 seconds per side. Press toward the bone, breathing out.
  4. KI-3 or LV-3 (ankle or foot) — 30 seconds per side. Choose KI-3 if your ringing is a steady high tone; LV-3 if it tracks your stress.
  5. Finish with six slow breaths — in through the nose for four counts, out for six. The exhale is the part that matters.

Protocol 2: The daily build

One to two sessions a day, five to eight minutes each, for at least two to four weeks. Steady and unspectacular beats intense and occasional — the useful analogy is a savings account, not a lottery ticket. A simple structure:

  • Evening routine (6 points, 5 minutes): GB-20 → SI-19 → GB-2 → TW-5 → KI-3 → GB-43. This order suits the depletion type, whose ringing is loudest at night.
  • Stress-linked variation: swap KI-3 for LV-3 and add ST-40 if you also feel foggy or congested. Use it after tense periods and before bed.
  • Kitchen-sink version for plugged ears: add PC-6 and LI-4, and keep meals lighter for a week to see whether the fullness tracks your digestion.
  • Pair it with sound: many practitioners of both traditions agree that a low-level background sound at night — rain, a fan, soft pink noise — takes the edge off the silence that makes tinnitus feel loudest.

What to expect: people commonly notice the ringing feels less intrusive, or their sleep improves, before they notice any change in the sound itself. That is a real result, not a consolation prize — the distress caused by tinnitus and the volume of tinnitus are not the same measurement. Do not measure success by whether the tone disappears; measure it by whether your evenings and nights get easier.

If You Want to Use the Ear's Own Map

In auricular (ear) acupressure, the whole body is mapped onto the ear like a control panel, and four points are commonly used in tinnitus routines: Shen Men (for settling the mind's alarm response), Kidney (reflecting the reserve system), Inner Ear (the local area), and Subcortex (used in some protocols for habituating to persistent sensation). You can press them with a fingertip, or apply tiny seeds or magnets that hold a light pressure for several days. The placement and application method are covered in detail in our Ear Seed Acupressure guide. Keep the pressure light and skip ear work entirely if the ear is sore, infected, or has recently had surgery.

Getting the Press Right: Six Rules

  1. Hunt for the hollow, not the bone. If you are pressing on something hard, you are close but not on the point. Move a few millimeters in each direction until you find the soft dip.
  2. Calibrate to a 4 to 6 out of 10. A good point feels like a deep, dull, slightly spreading sensation — not sharp pain. Sharp means back off.
  3. 30 to 60 seconds per point, with your breathing involved. Long, slow exhales while pressing are what shift a routine from mechanical to regulating.
  4. Steady pressure and small circles are both fine; grinding is not. Pick whichever lets you keep the pressure consistent.
  5. Never press into the ear canal, and never work over a sore, swollen, or discharging ear. Local points sit in front of and behind the ear for a reason.
  6. Do both sides even when only one ear rings — you are working with channels, and channels are bilateral. Keep fingernails short and remove hearing aids first so you are not pressing hardware against skin.

When to See a Doctor

Acupressure is a self-care practice, not a diagnostic tool. Please do not let it delay an evaluation. Seek medical care promptly — and in some cases urgently — if any of the following applies:

  • Sudden ringing in one ear with hearing loss. This can indicate sudden sensorineural hearing loss, which is time-sensitive. Contact a healthcare provider or urgent care right away — the sooner it is assessed, the better.
  • Pulsatile tinnitus — a sound that beats in time with your pulse. This pattern has different causes and typically needs a medical workup, including vascular assessment.
  • Tinnitus with vertigo, ear pain, discharge, or fever — possible infection or inner-ear involvement.
  • Any tinnitus after a head injury, or with one-sided weakness or numbness, difficulty speaking, confusion, or vision changes. These are emergency symptoms — call emergency services.
  • Ringing that began or worsened after starting a medication. Some antibiotics, high-dose aspirin, certain diuretics, and some cancer therapies are associated with tinnitus. Speak to your prescriber — do not stop a prescribed medication on your own.
  • Ringing that is driving anxiety, depression, or sleeplessness. This is worth telling a clinician about; tinnitus-focused cognitive behavioral therapy and sound-based approaches have the strongest evidence base for reducing the distress it causes.
  • Ringing in one ear only, or with a noticeable hearing difference between ears, especially if it is persistent — this deserves an audiology or ENT assessment rather than watchful waiting.

What Research Suggests

Honesty matters more than enthusiasm here, so let's separate what is reasonably well supported from what is still preliminary.

  • Acupuncture for tinnitus: a Cochrane systematic review of acupuncture for tinnitus found limited, low-quality evidence and could not reach a firm conclusion about benefit. That means acupuncture and acupressure for tinnitus should be understood as low-risk additions to standard care, not substitutes for it.
  • Auricular acupressure: some small trials suggest that ear acupressure may be associated with improved sleep quality and reduced tinnitus-related distress in people with persistent tinnitus. Sample sizes are modest, and results have not been consistent across studies.
  • Auricular vagus nerve stimulation: researchers have investigated stimulating the auricular branch of the vagus nerve — the cymba conchae of the ear — for tinnitus, with preliminary and mixed results. It is a striking overlap with the TCM idea that the ear is a control surface for the whole system, though the two are not the same claim.
  • Guideline direction: major ENT clinical guidelines for persistent tinnitus advise against routine drug or supplement approaches and instead emphasize education, sound therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, and correcting any hearing loss — hearing aids can reduce perceived tinnitus in people who have hearing loss. Acupressure fits comfortably alongside those measures.
  • The amplification loop: research on tinnitus distress highlights how the limbic system and attention networks amplify a sound that the auditory system has already flagged. Anything that reliably downshifts arousal — slow breathing, massage, a calmer nervous system — may change how loud the same signal feels.

Practical Takeaways

  • Start with four points, not twelve. GB-20, SI-19, GB-2, and either KI-3 (steady high tone) or LV-3 (stress-linked) cover most people's first two weeks.
  • Use the 90-second reset when it spikes — top to bottom, ending with six long exhales. Use the 5-minute daily build for the slow cumulative work.
  • Match your pattern. Depletion ringing gets KI-3 and earlier nights; stress ringing gets LV-3 and movement; plugged, foggy ringing gets ST-40, PC-6, and lighter meals.
  • Skip LI-4 and SP-6 during pregnancy, and never press into the ear canal or over a sore or infected ear.
  • Two to four weeks before you judge it, and measure your nights and your stress, not just the volume of the tone.
  • Book the medical visit first. Sudden one-sided tinnitus with hearing loss, pulsatile tinnitus, or tinnitus after a head injury needs a clinician, not a protocol.
