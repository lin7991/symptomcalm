Tui Na for Neck Pain: Hands-On Relief for Stiff Necks

Treatment Guide · 8 min read

Introduction: The Neck That Won't Unclench

You turn your head to check a blind spot while driving — and a sharp tug reminds you your neck is tight. Or you spend the workday with your shoulders creeping toward your ears, and by evening you can barely rotate your head past a 45-degree angle. Neck pain is one of the most common musculoskeletal complaints worldwide, affecting an estimated one in three adults annually.

A painkiller may take the edge off, but it doesn't address the underlying tension. Stretching helps, but the tightness often returns by the next morning. This is where Tui Na — a hands-on bodywork therapy within Traditional Chinese Medicine — offers a different approach. Rather than trying to relax muscles, Tui Na works with the body's channel system to release trapped Qi and Blood in the neck.

Think of it this way: neck tension is like a garden hose with a kink. The water (Qi) builds up behind the kink, and the hose becomes rigid and uncomfortable. Tui Na doesn't just squeeze the nozzle — it finds and releases the kink itself.

What Is Tui Na?

Tui Na (pronounced "tway nah") translates to "push-grasp" — the two foundational hand techniques in this therapy. Unlike Swedish massage, which focuses on soft-tissue relaxation, Tui Na specifically targets the body's meridian channels and acupressure points, using techniques that range from gentle pressing to deeper rhythmic manipulation.

The key difference between Tui Na and general massage:

  • Targeted channels: Tui Na works along specific meridian pathways, not just broad muscle groups
  • Acupoint activation: Pressing and holding key points to move Qi through stubborn areas
  • Directional manipulation: Techniques follow the direction of Qi flow (not just the direction of muscle fibers)
  • Diagnostic component: The practitioner assesses tenderness and tissue texture along channels to identify which pattern is causing the neck pain

For a broader overview of Tui Na techniques and principles, see our Tui Na Therapy Guide. This article focuses specifically on applying Tui Na to neck pain.

How Neck Pain Looks Through a TCM Lens

In TCM theory, neck pain is rarely just about the neck muscles. The neck is a critical junction where multiple channels converge, including:

  • Gallbladder Channel (GB): Runs along the side of the neck — commonly involved in tension headaches that wrap around the sides of the head
  • Bladder Channel (BL): Runs along the back of the neck and spine — involved in neck stiffness with headache at the back of the head
  • Small Intestine Channel (SI): Runs along the side of the neck to the shoulder — involved in pain radiating down the arm
  • San Jiao (Triple Burner) Channel (SJ/TB): Runs behind the ear down the neck to the shoulder — involved in neck tension that accompanies jaw clenching or ear fullness
  • Governing Vessel (DU): Runs along the spine to the top of the head — involved in whole-spine stiffness

Most common neck pain patterns fall into three TCM categories:

Pattern 1: Qi and Blood Stagnation from Posture

The most common pattern in modern life. Prolonged desk work, phone use, and forward-head posture create a chronic kink in the channels. The neck feels stiff, achy, and worse with sustained positioning. The muscles may feel "ropey" or tight to the touch. Improving with movement — after a warm shower or a walk, the stiffness eases temporarily.

Pattern 2: Wind-Cold Invasion

Exposure to drafts, cold weather, or air conditioning creates a "frozen" sensation in the neck. Onset is often acute — you slept with the window open or sat under a vent, and woke up unable to turn your head. The area feels cold to the touch, and warmth brings noticeable relief.

Pattern 3: Liver Qi Stagnation with Neck Tension

When accumulated stress has nowhere to go, it settles in the neck and shoulders. The tension is chronic, worse with emotional stress, and often accompanied by jaw clenching, shoulder tightness, and a sensation of a lump in the throat (called "Plum Pit Qi" in TCM). The neck feels tight rather than painful, and relaxation techniques only provide temporary relief.

6 Key Acupressure Points for Neck Pain

Tui Na for neck pain typically targets points along the affected channels. These points serve as "release valves" — pressing and holding them sends a signal through the channel to release tension upstream and downstream:

GB20 (Fengchi) — Wind Pool

Location: In the hollow at the base of the skull, between the two large vertical neck muscles

TCM action: Expels Wind-Cold, releases the neck and upper shoulders, clears head congestion — one of the most important points for neck stiffness

Technique: Press firmly with both thumbs simultaneously, angling slightly upward toward the skull. Hold for 30-60 seconds while breathing deeply. You should feel a distinct ache or fullness.

GB21 (Jianjing) — Shoulder Well

Location: At the highest point of the shoulder muscle, midway between the base of the neck and the tip of the shoulder

TCM action: Moves Qi in the neck and shoulder region, releases stubborn tension that feels "stuck"

Technique: Use your thumb or middle finger to press deeply into the muscle belly. The sensation should be strong but not sharp. Hold 30-60 seconds, then slowly release.

BL10 (Tianzhu) — Celestial Pillar

Location: On the back of the neck, about half an inch lateral to the spine at the hairline

TCM action: Releases the Bladder channel along the back of the neck, useful for headache at the back of the head with neck stiffness

Technique: Press with thumbs on both sides simultaneously, working from top to bottom in slow increments. Excellent for the "stiff neck that won't turn."

SI3 (Houxi) — Back Stream

Location: On the outer edge of the hand, at the end of the small finger's crease when making a loose fist

TCM action: A master point for the Governing Vessel — relieves neck pain and stiffness that runs along the center of the spine

Technique: Press the point against the edge of a table or with the opposite thumb. Roll the hand slightly to find the tender spot.

SJ15 (Tianliao) — Heavenly Bone

Location: Above the shoulder blade, about midway between the neck and shoulder tip

TCM action: Releases San Jiao channel tension in the neck-shoulder transition zone — excellent for neck tension that accompanies shoulder tightness

Technique: Have a partner press firmly into the muscle above the shoulder blade. The sensation radiates comfortably toward the neck.

LI4 (Hegu) — Union Valley

Location: In the web between the thumb and index finger, at the highest point of the muscle when the thumb is adducted

TCM action: A powerful point for moving Qi generally — useful for any type of stagnation, including neck pain from stress

Technique: Squeeze between thumb and index finger of the opposite hand, pressing toward the bone of the index finger. Hold 30 seconds.

Self-Tui Na Routine for Neck Pain (5 Minutes)

This sequence follows channel logic — releasing tension from the top of the neck downward, then addressing the shoulder connection:

  1. Warm Up (30 seconds): Gently rub the back of your neck with your palm until you feel warmth. This activates local circulation and prepares the tissues.
  2. GB20 press (60 seconds): Both thumbs into the hollows at the base of the skull. Breathe slowly. The tissue should soften as you hold.
  3. BL10 tracing (30 seconds): Thumb-walk down both sides of the upper neck, pressing and releasing in one-inch increments. Stop and hold at any spot that feels particularly tight.
  4. GB21 squeeze (60 seconds each side): Deep press into the shoulder muscle on one side, then the other. Combine with slow head rotation for a more thorough release.
  5. SI3 press (30 seconds each hand): Press into the outer edge of each hand. This may feel surprising — a neck-release point on the hand — but the Governing Vessel connection makes it effective.
  6. Neck mobilization (30 seconds): With one hand holding GB20 and the other hand gently guiding the head through a slow rotation, let the Tui Na release integrate into movement.

What Research Says

While research on Tui Na specifically for neck pain is still developing, several lines of evidence support the approach:

  • Tui Na for chronic neck pain: A 2017 systematic review in Medicine found that Tui Na, alone or combined with other therapies, was associated with improvements in chronic neck pain compared to routine care, though study quality varied.
  • Acupressure for neck pain: A 2021 meta-analysis in Clinical Rehabilitation (8 RCTs, 610 participants) found that acupressure — which shares key techniques with Tui Na — reduced neck pain intensity and improved range of motion compared to sham or waiting-list controls.
  • GB20 and neck muscle tension: A small 2020 study in EBCAM used infrared thermography to show that GB20 stimulation increased local blood flow in the neck, suggesting a measurable physiological effect on tissue circulation.
  • Manual therapy and neck pain guidelines: The American College of Physicians (2017) recommends non-pharmacologic approaches — including manual therapy — as first-line treatment for acute and chronic neck pain before medications.

When to See a Doctor

While Tui Na is generally gentle and safe, certain neck pain symptoms require professional medical evaluation:

  • Neck pain following a fall, accident, or direct trauma
  • Pain that radiates down both arms or is accompanied by numbness or weakness
  • Neck stiffness with fever, headache, and light sensitivity
  • Unexplained weight loss accompanying neck pain
  • Pain that worsens progressively over several weeks
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control with severe neck pain

Practical Takeaways

For stubborn neck tension that returns day after day:

  • Start at the base of the skull: GB20 is the first release point for most neck patterns — press and hold before trying to stretch
  • Match technique to pattern: Cold/frozen neck = warming Tui Na (rubbing, kneading); Stress-related tension = holding pressure at GB21 and GB20
  • Use SI3 for spine-line stiffness: The Governing Vessel point on your hand is surprisingly effective when neck pain runs along the center of the spine
  • Address posture at the source: Tui Na releases the Channel obstruction, but sustained improvement requires adjusting the desk setup that creates the kink
  • Consider professional treatment: Self-Tui Na is like checking your own oil — effective for maintenance, but a professional Tui Na practitioner can address deeper channel patterns
