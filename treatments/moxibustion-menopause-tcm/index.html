Moxibustion for Menopause: Warming Practices for the Transition Years

Treatment Guide · 7 min read

Wait — Moxibustion for Menopause? Isn't It All About Heat?

Every article about menopause seems to be about heat. Cooling teas. Cooling foods. Cool sheets. Cool nights. And if hot flashes are your main event, that advice makes sense.

But there is another menopause that almost no one talks about — the one where you're freezing.

Your hands stay cold in a warm room. Your lower back aches in a way that feels deep and old, like the warmth has drained out of it. You wake at 4 a.m. and your feet are ice cubes. You mention it to friends and they look at you sideways: "But aren't you having hot flashes?"

Sometimes — and this is the part that really throws people — you have both. A flash that floods your face and chest with heat while your feet stay freezing. You are, in a single moment, a woman who is somehow too hot and too cold at the same time.

That is not a malfunction. In TCM theory, it is a pattern with a name — and it changes everything about whether a warming practice like moxibustion fits you. Let's unpack it.

Key idea: Moxibustion (pronounced mox-ee-BUS-chun) is the ancient TCM practice of burning dried mugwort — moxa, or Ai Ye — near specific acupoints to deliver deep, penetrating warmth. For the transition years, it is traditionally used as a way to warm the ground floor of the body: the lower abdomen, the lower back, and the legs — rather than adding heat where heat is already pooling.

The Two Climate Systems of the Transition Years

For a full map of the transition, see our guide to menopause from a TCM perspective and the companion deep-dive on hot flashes. Here's the one-sentence version you need for this article:

In TCM, the Kidneys store the body's deepest reserves, and they hold two complementary fuels. Yin is the cooling, moistening, anchoring fuel — think of it as the coolant line. Yang is the warming, activating fuel — the heating line. Across the transition years, both supplies decline. Which shortage you feel first shapes your entire experience:

  • If Yin declines faster, Empty Heat rises: hot flashes, night sweats, dryness, irritability. The attic heats up.
  • If Yang declines faster, the warmth supply drops: cold hands and feet, a deep low-back ache, needing to urinate more often, fatigue, feeling cold when everyone else is warm. The basement gets cold.
  • If both decline together, you get the mixed picture: flushing in the face and chest while the feet, hands, or lower back stay cold — a stuffy attic and a freezing basement in the same house.

Moxibustion is a warming therapy. It is not aimed at the first group. It may have a genuine, traditional role for the second and third.

Here's the mental image that makes this click: think of your body as a house with two separate climate systems — central air for cooling, central heating for warming — both drawing on one shared power budget. Around menopause, that budget gets recalibrated. Most of the conversation focuses on the cooling system, and rightly so: when it sputters, you get surges of heat. But the heating system is on the same budget, and it can sputter too. And because heat rises, a house can end up stuffy upstairs and freezing downstairs at the same time. The fix is not to turn off all the heat — that only makes the basement colder. The traditional approach is to rebalance the zones: warm the ground floor, and let the attic vent.

The Menopause Patterns Moxibustion Fits (and the One It Doesn't)

Moxibustion is not a universal menopause remedy. In TCM thinking, it suits cold and mixed-cold patterns — and can add fuel to the fire of pure heat ones. Here's how to tell which one you're dealing with.

Kidney Yang Deficiency — The Freezing-Basement Pattern

  • What it feels like: Hands and feet that stay cold most days, even indoors; a deep ache in the lower back; needing to urinate more often, especially at night; fatigue that warmth improves; feeling cold when others feel fine; a pale complexion; symptoms that worsen in cold weather.
  • Why moxibustion fits: This is the classic warming pattern. The heating line is sputtering — the fuel exists, but there isn't enough heat to circulate it. Moxibustion adds warmth directly to the system, like relighting a pilot light rather than demanding more output from a cold machine.
  • Where the warmth goes: Lower abdomen (CV-4), lower back (BL-23 area), and ST-36 on the legs — all detailed below.

The Mixed Pattern — Stuffy Attic, Freezing Basement

  • What it feels like: Hot flashes and cold feet or hands — sometimes in the same moment. A flush that rises to your face and chest while your legs stay cold. Night sweats paired with icy feet. A lower back that feels cold to the touch. You can't win: too hot and too cold at once.
  • Why moxibustion fits: This is the pattern TCM understands best. Heat has pooled upward while the lower half runs cold. Warming the lower half — the feet, the legs, the lower abdomen — is traditionally used to draw warmth downward and rebalance the zones. The key is that the warmth stays below the waist, never on the upper back, neck, or chest.
  • Where the warmth goes: KI-1 on the soles, ST-36 on the legs, CV-4 on the lower abdomen — the "ground floor" points. Nothing above the navel.

Spleen Qi Deficiency — The Low-Fuel Pattern

  • What it feels like: Menopause fatigue with a digestion flavor: an energy slump an hour or two after meals, heavy limbs, bloating, weight that creeps up despite unchanged habits, loose stools, cold hands, sweet cravings.
  • Why moxibustion fits: The Spleen in TCM is the conversion plant — it turns food into usable energy. When it runs weakly, you're like a laptop plugged in but not charging: food goes in, output stays low. Warming moxibustion at ST-36 and over the abdomen is traditionally used to support that conversion.
  • Where the warmth goes: ST-36 on both legs, plus gentle warmth over the upper abdomen.

When Moxibustion Is NOT the Fit — Pure Heat and Empty Heat

  • What it feels like: Frequent, intense hot flashes; night sweats that drench the sheets; a red tongue; dry mouth; irritability; feeling warm all over most of the time; heat sensitivity that makes you avoid warm rooms, warm drinks, even warm weather.
  • Why moxibustion is not the first choice: This is a system already running hot. Adding more warmth is like firing up the furnace when the attic is already stuffy — it can intensify flushing and discomfort.
  • What to do instead: Cooling, calming approaches fit this pattern better. Our guide to acupressure for menopause covers point-based techniques suited to heat-dominant experiences, and the hot flashes guide has a full cooling toolkit.

Not sure which pattern you are? Try the three-question test. (1) Are your hands or feet cold more days than not, even in a warm room? (2) Do you feel better after warmth — a hot shower, a warm drink, a heating pad? (3) Do you get hot flashes while your feet, hands, or lower back stay cold? Three yeses — or yes to 1 and 2 — point toward the cold or mixed patterns where moxibustion fits. If question 3 is your only yes and your flashes are intense and drenching, use only the lower-body protocol below. If you answered no to all three and you run hot, warming practices are probably not your starting point.

Key Points for the Transition: Where the Warmth Goes

These points form the core of a menopause-focused moxibustion practice. All are workable at home when the basic rules are followed (see the safety section below).

CV-4 (Guanyuan) — Conception Vessel 4: The Transition Reserve

Location: On the midline of the lower abdomen, about four finger-widths below the navel.

Why for menopause: CV-4 is called the Gate of Origin — the point traditionally associated with the body's foundational warmth, the Kidney Yang that governs the transition years. In moxibustion practice it is the classic first stop for a cold lower abdomen, a weak lower back, and needing to urinate often.

How to use: 10–15 minutes with a moxa stick held 2–3 cm away in small circles, or a moxa box placed over the lower abdomen for 15–20 minutes of broader, gentler heat.

ST-36 (Zusanli) — Stomach 36: The Stamina Conversion Point

Location: Four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width to the outside of the shin bone, in the muscle belly.

Why for menopause: ST-36 is the classic point for rebuilding Qi and Blood from food — the conversion plant access panel. It's the point most relevant to the low-fuel fatigue pattern, and it doubles as the natural anchor for midlife stamina: the same point most frequently used across moxibustion trials.

How to use: 10–15 minutes per leg, small circles, pleasantly warm — never sharp or burning.

KI-1 (Yongquan) — Kidney 1: The Heat-Draw Point

Location: On the sole of the foot, in the depression about one-third of the way from the toes to the heel — the spot that dips when you curl your toes.

Why for menopause: KI-1 is the first point of the Kidney channel, and it earns its nickname here: Bubbling Spring. In TCM theory, warming the soles is a traditional way to guide warmth downward — the point for the woman who gets hot flashes while her feet stay icy, and for the freezing-feet-at-4-a.m. experience. It's the signature point of the mixed-pattern protocol.

How to use: A moxa stick holder keeps the burning end safely positioned above the foot while you sit. 5–10 minutes per foot. Never use moxa on broken, numb, or diabetic feet.

BL-23 (Shenshu) — Bladder 23: The Kidney's Back Port

Location: On the lower back, about two finger-widths from the spine, level with the second lumbar vertebra — roughly the small of the back, where your hands naturally rest.

Why for menopause: This is the back communication port of the Kidney — the point most directly tied to low-back ache and frequent urination, two of the most common cold-side complaints of the transition.

How to use: A moxa box positioned over the area for 15–20 minutes, or ask a partner to hold the stick. A warm compress is a gentler fallback.

CV-6 (Qihai) — Conception Vessel 6: The Sea of Qi

Location: On the midline, about 1.5 finger-widths below the navel.

Why for menopause: A reservoir point for the body's fundamental energy. Many people alternate CV-6 and CV-4 within a single session — CV-6 for general Qi, CV-4 for the deeper transition reserve.

How to use: Same technique as CV-4, 10 minutes with a slow, grounding motion.

If you only do one thing: ST-36 on both legs. It's the easiest point to locate, the most-studied point in moxibustion research, and the safest place to test whether your body welcomes warmth — with no risk of adding heat where it might not be wanted.

The Signature Protocol: Warm the Ground Floor, Let the Attic Vent

Moxibustion is a cumulative practice — think of warming soil before planting, not flipping a light switch. Plan on a four-week arc, and evaluate how you feel at the end of each week rather than after each session. Choose the track that matches your three-question test results.

Track A — The Mixed Pattern (upper heat, lower cold): lower body only

  • Session: KI-1 on both feet (5–10 min each), then ST-36 on both legs (10 min each), then CV-4 on the lower abdomen (10 min).
  • The ground-floor rule: Nothing above the waist. No upper back, no neck, no chest, no face points. The goal is to draw warmth downward, not to stoke the attic.
  • Frequency: 2–3 times per week, morning or early afternoon.
  • Watch and adjust: If your hot flashes increase in frequency or intensity during the week, reduce to once a week or pause for a week — and check in with a qualified practitioner. If cold feet improve and flashes stay the same or settle, continue.
  • Keep sessions modest: Shorter and gentler than the cold-dominant track — 20–25 minutes total is plenty.

Track B — The Cold-Dominant Pattern: the four-week arc

  • Weeks 1–2 — Foundation: ST-36 on both legs (10–15 min each) plus CV-4 (10–15 min), 3–4 times per week. Goal: build the habit, learn your heat tolerance, and notice any change in hand warmth, morning energy, or urinary frequency.
  • Weeks 3–4 — Add the lower back: The foundation above, plus a moxa box over the lower back (BL-23 area) for 15–20 minutes, 2–3 times per week. People with low-back ache and nighttime urination often notice this addition first.
  • Optional: KI-1 for cold feet, 5–10 minutes per foot, 2–3 times per week, added to the same session.

Timing and Aftercare (both tracks)

  • Timing: Morning and early afternoon are traditionally the "Yang hours" — the natural window for warming practice. Avoid moxibustion within 2 hours of bedtime; some people find it activating.
  • Aftercare: Drink a cup of warm water or ginger tea. Avoid cold drinks, cold showers, and drafts for at least an hour. Rest 10–15 minutes — the warmth keeps working after the moxa is out.
  • Extinguish properly: Press the burning end firmly into a ceramic plate or sand until no smoke remains. Moxa can smolder for hours if left.
  • Frequency guidance: After four weeks of regular use, take a week off. If a session ever feels draining rather than restorative, reduce the frequency or duration, and check in with a qualified practitioner.

What the Research Says

Research on moxibustion is growing, though most studies are small and the results are preliminary. The menopause-specific evidence clusters in two areas:

  • Moxibustion and menopausal symptoms: A number of small trials — most conducted in East Asia — have investigated moxibustion, often combined with acupressure, for menopause symptoms, measuring outcomes with standardized questionnaires like the Kupperman Index. Several reported lower symptom scores compared with no intervention, with points like ST-36, CV-4, and SP-6 appearing frequently in the protocols. The authors consistently flag small sample sizes, short durations, and the need for larger, higher-quality trials before firm conclusions.
  • Point-based therapies and hot flashes: The strongest evidence for point-based approaches in menopause sits with acupuncture — recent randomized trials have reported reductions in vasomotor symptom frequency compared with sham acupuncture. Moxibustion-specific evidence for hot flashes is thinner, which is one reason this article positions moxibustion for the cold and mixed presentations rather than as a first-line approach for heat-dominant ones.

Western bridge: The transition years are a period of thermoregulatory recalibration — the hypothalamus adjusts its set point, which produces flushing episodes. Cold extremities are also common in midlife, reflecting reduced peripheral circulation. Some thermoregulation research suggests that warming cold extremities may support more even heat dissipation across the body — a physiological lens on the TCM idea of warming the ground floor to relieve an overheated upper story. The far-infrared radiation of burning moxa penetrates several centimeters into tissue, deeper than the conductive warmth of a heating pad.

Research note: The evidence base for moxibustion in menopause is promising but preliminary. Think of moxibustion as one layer of a broader approach — sleep, nutrition, movement, stress management, and guidance from your healthcare team — not as a standalone answer. And if you have any postmenopausal bleeding, put the moxa away and see a doctor promptly, before exploring any self-care practice.

Using Moxibustion Safely

  • Never during an active hot flash. If a flush comes on mid-session, put the moxa out, cool down, and wait. Warming into a heat surge is traditionally considered counterproductive.
  • Mixed pattern means lower body only. If you have flashes, keep all warmth below the waist. If flushing worsens with practice, stop and check in with a practitioner.
  • Skin changes matter. Skin can become thinner and more sensitive during the transition. Keep a comfortable distance (2–3 cm), use shorter sessions, and watch for redness beyond what feels normal.
  • Burns are the main risk. The sensation should be warm, never sharp or burning. Never fall asleep with a lit stick. A moxa stick holder with an ash guard adds a layer of safety.
  • Reduced sensation equals high burn risk. If you have diabetes-related neuropathy, or any area with reduced feeling, do not use moxibustion there. Diabetic feet should never be warmed with moxa.
  • Pregnancy: If you are still in perimenopause and there is any chance you could be pregnant, avoid moxibustion on the lower abdomen and lower back. Some points (including ST-36 and CV-4) are traditionally considered contraindicated in pregnancy.
  • Fever or acute infection: Do not use moxibustion during an active fever or acute illness.
  • Smoke sensitivity: Moxa smoke can irritate sensitive airways. If you have asthma or respiratory sensitivity, choose smokeless moxa, ensure excellent ventilation, or use a smoke absorber.
  • Fire safety: Extinguish fully after every session. Never leave a lit stick unattended, and keep it away from curtains and bedding.

When to See a Doctor

Menopause deserves support — and some symptoms deserve prompt medical attention, with or without a complementary practice. See a healthcare provider if:

  • You have any postmenopausal bleeding — even a single spot, even once. Bleeding a year or more after your last period always requires evaluation by a doctor.
  • Hot flashes are severe, frequent, or disabling — or new after years without them — and are affecting sleep and daily life
  • Night sweats come with fever, chills, or unexplained weight loss
  • You have new or worsening pelvic pain, pressure, or pain during sex
  • You have sudden symptoms such as chest pain, shortness of breath, or a severe headache
  • Low mood, anxiety, or irritability is persistent and interfering with your life — or you have thoughts of harming yourself
  • You develop a burn, skin reaction, or infection at a moxibustion site that isn't recovering normally

Postmenopausal bleeding in particular is not something to wait out or self-manage — make that appointment. Everything else here can be explored alongside proper medical care.

Practical Takeaways

Quick summary — what to remember:

  • Best for: The cold-dominant menopause (cold hands and feet, low-back ache, urinary frequency) and the mixed pattern (hot flashes with cold feet or lower body). Not for pure heat patterns — intense flashes, drenching night sweats, feeling warm all over.
  • Core points: CV-4 (transition reserve), ST-36 (stamina conversion), KI-1 (the heat-draw point for cold feet during flashes), BL-23 (Kidney back port), with CV-6 as an alternation.
  • Mixed pattern: Lower body only — KI-1, ST-36, CV-4 — 2–3 times per week, modest sessions, and stop if flashes increase.
  • Cold-dominant: A four-week arc — ST-36 + CV-4 first, then add the lower-back moxa box in weeks 3–4. Morning timing, warm aftercare.
  • Safety first: Never moxa during a hot flash, keep 2–3 cm distance, never on numb skin or diabetic feet, skip in pregnancy and fever, and extinguish the stick fully after every session.
  • The one red flag that overrides everything: Postmenopausal bleeding of any kind means a prompt visit to your doctor.
