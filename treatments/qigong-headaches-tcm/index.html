Qigong for Headaches: Gentle Movements That Release Tension

Treatment Guide · 7 min read

It starts as a faint pressure behind your eyes around mid-morning. By early afternoon it has tightened into a band across your forehead, and your neck and shoulders feel like they're carrying the whole day. You reach for whatever is in the medicine cabinet. It works for a few hours — then the ache creeps back, sometimes sharper than before.

You've been told the usual things: drink more water, take breaks from the screen, watch your posture. And you've probably noticed none of them quite address the specific kind of tension that builds in your neck, jaw, and temples — the kind that shows up like clockwork.

What if the release wasn't in your head at all, but in how your shoulders, neck, and breath move together? That's the premise of qigong (pronounced chee-gong), a gentle movement practice from the Chinese tradition that has been used for centuries to regulate the body's energy flow — and that modern research is increasingly curious about.

Your Headache Isn't Just in Your Head

In TCM theory, most recurring headaches share a common theme: Qi — the body's vital energy — isn't moving smoothly, or it's rising when it should be flowing evenly. Think of Qi as the body's operating system. When the system runs smoothly, energy circulates to every tissue without drama. When it gets stuck — under stress, poor sleep, long hours of screen work — the congestion shows up somewhere physical.

In many people, that "somewhere" is the neck and shoulders. The Gallbladder channel, one of the body's major energy pathways, runs from the outer corner of the eye up over the side of the head and down through the neck and shoulders. The Liver channel, which governs the smooth flow of Qi overall, reaches the top of the head through its connections. When Qi stagnates in the neck and shoulders — like a kink in a garden hose — the pressure can register as a headache at the temples, forehead, or back of the head.

Modern research describes the same geography in different words. Tension-type headache — the most common headache type worldwide — is characterized in part by tenderness in the pericranial muscles: the muscles of the scalp, neck, and shoulders. The tension you feel in your shoulders and the ache in your head are not two separate problems. In both frameworks, they're one system.

Why Qigong for a Headache? The TCM Logic

Qigong approaches the headache from three directions at once:

1. It moves Qi. Gentle, coordinated movement is designed to encourage Qi to flow through the channels. Where a headache pattern involves stagnation — the kink in the hose — qigong works to release the kink from the inside out, rather than forcing the water through it.

2. It lengthens the exhale. In TCM, the Lungs govern Qi, and the breath is the most direct lever you have over your energy. A slow, extended exhale is associated with activating the body's calming response — and in TCM terms, it encourages Qi to descend rather than crowd upward toward the head. This matters because many headaches are "rising" in nature: heat and pressure moving up when they should be settling.

3. It releases the storage sites. The neck, shoulders, and jaw are where daily tension physically accumulates. Qigong movements that slowly mobilize these areas — with breath, not force — address the physical storage sites of headache-related tension directly.

This is different from a workout. The goal is not to raise your heart rate or exhaust the muscles. It's the opposite: to signal to the nervous system that it's safe to let go — and to give the body's energy a clear, gentle path to flow.

Three Headache Patterns, Three Qigong Approaches

Not all headaches feel the same, and in TCM they aren't all addressed the same way. Use your actual experience to find the pattern that sounds most like you.

🔹 The Tension Band (Liver Qi Stagnation)

A tight band around the forehead, pressure behind the eyes, and a neck and jaw that feel clenched. It builds through the day, worsens with stress, and you may notice yourself sighing often. In TCM terms, this is Qi stuck in the neck and shoulders — like a river with a dam. The approach: slow neck and shoulder release with long exhales, plus movements that open the chest and sides.

🔹 The Rising Throb (Liver Yang Rising)

A pulsating, throbbing pain, often on one side of the head or at the temples. It flares with frustration, hot rooms, lack of sleep, or after caffeine and alcohol. In TCM terms, this is Yang — the warm, active, upward-moving aspect of the body — surging upward like steam in a pressure cooker. The approach: movements that encourage Qi to descend, with extra attention to slow, extended exhales and grounding the weight.

🔹 The Dull, Heavy Ache (Qi Stagnation with Deficiency)

A dull, pressing ache that's worse by the end of the day, sometimes with fatigue, and often better when you press or rub the area. It may feel like your head is "too heavy" for your body. In TCM terms, this often involves Qi that is both stagnant and running low — the body is tired and the circulation is sluggish. The approach: gentle, restorative movements with a strong grounding focus, avoiding anything vigorous.

If you're unsure which pattern fits, the routine below is designed to be safe and useful across all three — start with the movements, and let your body's response guide which ones you repeat.

Four Qigong Movements for Headache Relief

These four movements are drawn from common qigong forms. Practice them slowly, with soft knees, relaxed shoulders, and the breath as the pacemaker. Stop if anything feels sharp or dizzying — gentle is the whole point.

Movement 1: Wind Gate Neck Release (for the Tension Band)

Stand or sit with your spine tall. Place your fingertips at the base of your skull, in the two hollows just below the ridge of bone — an area TCM knows as Wind Gate (GB-20), a classic point for head and neck tension.

On a slow inhale, gently turn your head to the right as far as is comfortable. On the exhale, return to center. Repeat to the left. Then, on the next exhale, let your chin drop toward your chest, feeling a long stretch up the back of the neck; on the inhale, return to neutral. Move at the speed of your breath — roughly five full rounds.

In TCM terms, this releases the Gallbladder channel as it passes through the neck — the physical storage site of the tension band — and invites Qi to flow up and over the head rather than pooling at the base of the skull.

🌿 When This Movement Shows Results

Many people notice the neck and shoulder muscles unclench within a few slow rounds — the "band" sensation often softens in the same session. In TCM theory, this movement gently opens the channel that serves the sides of the head, so it's especially suited to tension-band headaches that build through the workday.

Movement 2: Mountain Shrug (for the Tension Band)

Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, arms hanging loose. On a slow inhale, lift both shoulders toward your ears, as if trying to touch them. Hold for one breath. On a long exhale, let them drop completely — feel the release travel down through your arms. Repeat six to eight times, letting each exhale be longer than the inhale.

The point between the shoulder and neck — called Shoulder Well (GB-21) in TCM — is a classic site where stress physically parks itself. This simple up-and-down movement, done with the breath, repeatedly invites that holding pattern to release.

Movement 3: Two Hands Hold Up the Heavens (for the Rising Throb and the Dull Ache)

This is the first movement of the Eight Pieces of Brocade, one of the most famous qigong sets. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart. Interlace your fingers, palms facing upward at belly height. On a slow inhale, raise the interlaced hands overhead, palms pushing toward the sky; let the stretch travel up through your arms and sides. On a long exhale, release the hands, separate them, and let them float down to your sides like falling leaves. Repeat five to eight times.

In TCM terms, this movement stretches the channels along the sides of the body and encourages Qi to flow downward through the torso — a gentle counter to the upward surge of a rising headache. It also decompresses the neck and spine, which serves the dull-ache pattern's tired, heavy feeling.

Movement 4: Cloud Hands (for the Rising Throb)

Stand with feet a little wider than shoulder-width, knees soft. Shift your weight onto the left leg as you bring your right hand across your body at chest height, palm facing you, tracing a slow horizontal arc — as if you were wiping a window at chest level. Let your gaze follow the hand. As the right hand reaches the left side, shift your weight onto the right leg and bring the left hand across to trace the same arc in the opposite direction. The hands "chase" each other in a slow, continuous circle. Keep the movement at breath speed — five to eight full arcs each way.

Cloud Hands is one of the most famous qigong movements, and in TCM terms it circulates Qi through the upper body and the Liver channel — the system that governs smooth, even flow. The continuous lateral motion is a reminder that Qi is meant to move in all directions, not just upward. It's an excellent choice when your headache feels like pressure building toward the top of the body.

The 5-Minute Daily Routine for Headache-Prone Days

Consistency matters more than length. A short daily practice — especially on days you know are headache-prone — tends to build a cumulative effect that a longer weekly session doesn't.

  1. Wind Gate Neck Release — 1 minute (five slow head turns and chin drops)
  2. Mountain Shrug — 1 minute (six to eight slow lifts and drops)
  3. Cloud Hands — 1.5 minutes (slow arcs, gaze following the hand)
  4. Two Hands Hold Up the Heavens — 1.5 minutes (five to eight stretches, long exhales)

Finish by standing still for three breaths: palms resting on your lower belly, inhaling gently, and letting each exhale be longer than the inhale. Notice how the neck and shoulders feel compared with when you started.

Try this routine at the times your headache typically begins — for many people that's late morning or mid-afternoon — as well as first thing in the morning as a preemptive reset.

What Research Says About Qigong and Headaches

The research base on qigong specifically for headaches is still early, but several strands of evidence point in the same direction as the TCM logic:

  • Mind-body movement and chronic pain. Several systematic reviews of qigong and tai chi for chronic pain conditions report modest improvements in pain intensity and quality of life. The movement quality — slow, breath-coordinated, low-intensity — is quite different from typical exercise, and researchers are still untangling which components matter most.
  • Tai chi and tension-type headache. In a 2018 randomized trial, adults with chronic tension-type headache who practiced tai chi over 12 weeks reported fewer headache days and reduced headache intensity compared with usual care. Tai chi is a close cousin of qigong, sharing the same slow, breath-led movement principles — a promising signal, though larger studies are still needed.
  • Pericranial muscle tenderness. The diagnostic criteria for tension-type headache (ICHD-3) note tenderness in the muscles of the head, neck, and shoulders as a characteristic feature. Qigong's slow neck and shoulder mobilization targets exactly this tissue.
  • Slow breathing and the nervous system. Slow, coordinated breathing — the heartbeat of qigong practice — is associated with increased heart rate variability and baroreflex sensitivity, markers of the body's calming (parasympathetic) tone. In TCM language, this is the breath guiding Qi down instead of letting it crowd upward.
  • Targeted neck and shoulder exercise. Some trials of specific stretching and strengthening programs for the neck and shoulders report reduced headache frequency in tension-type headache. Qigong is gentler than most such programs, but shares their anatomical focus.

Practical Takeaways:

  • Match the movement to the pattern: neck and shoulder release for the tension band, descending slow movements for the rising throb, grounding gentle practice for the dull ache.
  • Practice 5 minutes daily, ideally at the time your headache usually begins — consistency outranks intensity.
  • Let the exhale lead: a longer exhale than inhale is the single most accessible "descending" tool in the whole practice.
  • Stop any movement that feels sharp, dizzying, or wrong — gentleness is the method, not a limitation.
  • Use qigong as one layer of a broader approach: regular meals, hydration, sleep, and screen breaks all feed the same system.

When to See a Doctor

Qigong is a gentle practice that is generally safe for most people. However, headaches can occasionally signal something serious. Seek medical attention promptly if you experience:

  • A sudden, severe headache — the worst you've ever had — often described as a "thunderclap"
  • Headache with fever, stiff neck, or a rash
  • Headache with weakness, numbness, vision changes, confusion, or difficulty speaking
  • A new type of headache after age 50
  • Headache that wakes you from sleep or is at its worst in the morning with nausea and vomiting
  • Headache following a head injury
  • Headaches that are increasing in frequency or severity despite rest and self-care

If you have a condition affecting your neck (such as cervical spine issues) or any chronic health concern, check with your healthcare provider before starting a movement practice. This article is educational and informational only, and is not a substitute for professional medical advice.
