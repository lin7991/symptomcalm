Moxibustion for Vertigo: Warming the Root When Dizziness Runs on Empty

Treatment Guide · 6 min read

There are two very different kinds of dizziness, and telling them apart could change what you reach for. There is the hot kind: the room spins, your head throbs, your ears ring, your face feels flushed — and you just want everything to stop. Then there is the cold, empty kind: you stand up too fast and the world tilts; you feel light-headed, washed out, and oddly chilly; the dizziness comes with fatigue rather than fire, and it has been creeping up on you for months.

If the second description sounds familiar, you have probably heard "it's all in your head," "it's just your age," or "maybe it's low blood pressure." And you may have tried more caffeine, more salt, or simply more white-knuckling through it. This article looks at one traditional tool that is sometimes used for exactly this drained, cold presentation: moxibustion — burning dried mugwort (moxa) near specific points to deliver deep warmth. We will cover when the heat fits, when it is actively wrong, which points to use, and the safety rules that matter more here than almost anywhere else.

A moment of seriousness first: dizziness can be a warning sign of something serious. If this is your first severe vertigo episode, if dizziness comes with chest pain, trouble speaking, weakness or numbness on one side, double vision, a severe headache, or fainting — stop reading and seek emergency care now. Moxibustion is for the slow, cold, chronic picture described below, never for an acute event you have not had evaluated.

Why Moxibustion Is a Picky Tool for Vertigo

Here is the honest headline: moxibustion is appropriate for only a slice of dizziness. In TCM theory, most vertigo is not a cold condition at all. The classic patterns — Liver Yang rising, Internal Wind, Phlegm-dampness — are excess pictures of heat, pressure, or congestion. Adding fire to those is traditionally described as pouring fuel on a flame, and it can genuinely make things worse.

The slice where warmth belongs is the empty-cold picture. Imagine the body as a building with a penthouse (the head) and a boiler (the lower body, especially the Kidneys). In TCM theory, clear-headedness depends on Qi and Blood rising adequately to the head — the penthouse needs a steady supply of warm air. When the boiler runs low (Kidney Yang deficiency) or the fuel line is thin (Qi and Blood deficiency), the supply weakens. The penthouse still exists — it is just under-heated and under-supplied. The result is the classic empty dizziness: light-headedness, worse on standing or exertion, worse when tired, better with rest and warmth.

Moxibustion is the traditional way to relight that boiler and thicken the supply line. The warmth does not go to the head (usually) — it goes to the lower body, the legs, and at most the crown point, to build the foundation that pushes energy upward. It is a slow, cumulative support for a depleted system, not a rescue for a spinning room.

The Cold-Vertigo Signature: When the Heat Fits

Two deficiency patterns are the traditional fit for moxibustion. Both run on "empty," and both lean cold.

Kidney Yang and Essence Decline — The Low-Battery Dizziness

  • What it feels like: Light-headedness rather than true spinning; symptoms triggered by standing up quickly, long walks, or a full day on your feet; a washed-out, "I need to sit down" feeling rather than a room that rotates; cold hands, feet, or lower back; weak or tired knees; frequent nighttime urination; a sense of running on a shrinking battery; more common with age or after years of hard living. Symptoms often worse in cold weather.
  • Why moxibustion fits: This is the low-boiler picture. In TCM theory, Kidney Yang is the body's deep warmth and the Kidneys are tied to the marrow and brain. Warming the lower back and lower abdomen is traditionally used to recharge that deep warmth and support the foundation the head depends on.
  • Where the warmth goes: BL-23 (Shenshu) on the lower back with a moxa box, and CV-4 (Guanyuan) on the lower abdomen.

Qi and Blood Deficiency — The Empty-Tank Dizziness

  • What it feels like: Dizziness that arrives with fatigue, poor sleep, skipped meals, or after illness or significant blood loss (including heavy periods); a pale complexion; poor appetite or weak digestion; feeling faint when you exert yourself or bend down and stand back up; a general thin, drained quality; dizziness that improves when you rest.
  • Why moxibustion fits (gently): In TCM theory, the head needs Qi and Blood to be clear — when the tank is thin, the penthouse is the first place to notice. Gentle warmth at the "Sea of Qi" and the legs' great Stomach point is traditionally used to help the body rebuild its Qi and Blood over weeks.
  • Where the warmth goes: CV-6 (Qihai) on the lower abdomen and ST-36 (Zusanli) on the legs — the classic rebuilding pair.

A quick three-question test. (1) Is your dizziness mostly light-headedness, worse on standing or exertion, rather than true room-spinning? (2) Do you run cold — cold hands, feet, or back — and does the dizziness feel worse in cold weather? (3) Does rest, warmth, and a good meal tend to help? Three yeses point to the empty-cold picture where moxibustion fits. If instead your head throbs, your face flushes, your ears ring loudly, or the room genuinely spins with nausea, the heat is probably not your tool — read the next section.

When the Heat Is Wrong: Patterns to Never Warm

These are the dizziness patterns where moxibustion is traditionally considered counterproductive — and in some cases genuinely risky. If one of these sounds like you, the warmth is not the answer.

Liver Yang Rising and Internal Wind — The Pressure-Cooker Dizziness

  • What it feels like: Dizziness with a pounding or throbbing headache, often on the sides of the head; ringing in the ears; a red or flushed face; irritability or a short fuse; feeling hot; symptoms worse with stress, anger, or lack of sleep; a history of high blood pressure. In TCM terms this is Yang rising and Wind stirring from heat and pressure — the opposite of empty.
  • Why moxibustion is not the fit — and can be risky: Adding heat to a pressure-cooker picture is traditionally described as fanning the flames. If your blood pressure runs high, extra warmth on the body is not something to experiment with casually. This pattern needs cooling, settling, and a medical check of blood pressure — not fire.
  • What to do instead: See our Vertigo Through a TCM Lens guide for the broader pattern picture, and the Internal Wind concept article for why this pattern behaves the way it does. Cooling practices and acupressure fit better here.

Phlegm-Dampness — The Heavy-Boat Dizziness

  • What it feels like: A sensation of swimming, rocking, or "cotton wool" in the head rather than sharp spinning; dizziness worse when you move your head or change position; a heavy, fuzzy feeling; nausea or a queasy stomach; a full, oppressed feeling in the chest; a tendency to feel bloated; sometimes a greasy coating on the tongue.
  • Why moxibustion is not the first move: In TCM theory this is Dampness and Phlegm clouding the head — a matter of congestion and turbidity, not empty cold. While some cold-damp presentations can tolerate warmth, the classic phlegm-dizziness picture is better served by resolving dampness and improving digestion than by piling on heat.
  • What to do instead: The Acupressure for Vertigo protocol is a gentler fit, and dietary shifts toward light, dry, easy-to-digest food traditionally support this pattern.

Acute Positional Vertigo (BPPV) and Meniere's — See a Professional

  • What it feels like: True spinning that comes on when you roll over in bed, tilt your head back, or look up — lasting under a minute — is the classic signature of benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), the most common cause of genuine room-spinning. Meniere's adds hearing loss, ringing, and a feeling of fullness in the ear, with episodes lasting 20 minutes to hours.
  • Why moxibustion is not the tool: BPPV is a mechanical inner-ear issue — tiny crystals dislodged into a sensitive canal — and the evidence-based fix is a repositioning maneuver performed by a trained professional. Home warming does not reposition crystals, and a first-ever spinning episode deserves a diagnosis, not a guess.
  • What to do instead: See a healthcare provider — ideally one who can perform positional testing and the Epley maneuver. Our vertigo guide covers when this matters most.

Key Points for the Empty-Cold Pattern

When the empty-cold picture fits, four points do most of the work. Notice the direction: the core warmth goes to the lower body and legs — the boiler and the fuel line — with one optional crown point for the "lift."

BL-23 (Shenshu) — Bladder 23: The Boiler Access

Location: On the lower back, roughly at waist level, about two finger-widths out from the spine on each side.

Why for dizziness: Shenshu is the Kidney back access point — in TCM terms, the door to the boiler room. For the low-battery pattern, warming here is the most direct traditional route to Kidney Yang. It is also the easiest point to warm safely at home with a moxa box, which matters doubly when you feel unsteady.

How to use: Moxa box over both points for 15–20 minutes, sitting or lying down — always seated or reclined, never standing.

CV-4 (Guanyuan) — Conception Vessel 4: The Gate of Origin

Location: On the midline, about four finger-widths below the navel.

Why for dizziness: CV-4 is tied to Kidney Yang and the body's constitutional warmth. For the cold, drained, "low-battery" dizziness, warming it is traditionally understood to recharge the deep reserve that the whole system — head included — draws from.

How to use: 10–12 minutes with a moxa stick in slow circles, or folded into a lower-abdomen moxa box session.

CV-6 (Qihai) — Conception Vessel 6: The Sea of Qi

Location: On the midline, about two finger-widths below the navel.

Why for dizziness: Qihai is where foundational Qi gathers in TCM theory. In the empty-tank pattern, warming it supports the body's ability to generate and hold Qi — the pressure behind the supply line that reaches the head.

How to use: 10–12 minutes with a moxa stick, or include it in the same lower-abdomen session as CV-4.

ST-36 (Zusanli) — Stomach 36: The Fuel-Line Point

Location: On the outer front of the lower leg, about four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width out from the shin bone.

Why for dizziness: ST-36 is the classic point for building Qi and Blood from food — the fuel line itself. When dizziness runs on a thin tank, warming ST-36 supports the rebuilding that the empty-tank pattern needs most.

How to use: 5–8 minutes per leg with a moxa stick.

GV-20 (Baihui) — Governing Vessel 20: The Crown (Optional)

Location: On the top of the head, on the midline at the point where a line between the ear tips crosses the midline.

Why for dizziness: Baihui means "Hundred Meetings." In traditional practice, moxibustion at the crown is used for the sinking, empty feeling of dizziness and faintness — the idea being to warm the very top of the body and help clear the head. It is optional, and it is the one point where the heat goes to the head rather than the foundation.

How to use — and when to skip: This point needs a steady hand and hair out of the way, so a partner is ideal; a moxa stick holder can work if you are comfortable. 5–8 minutes, keeping the burning end well clear of hair. Skip it entirely if you feel at all unsteady, if you are alone, or if the idea of heat near your head makes you uneasy — the lower-body points do the main work anyway.

If you only do one thing: the moxa box on BL-23, sitting down, 15–20 minutes. It is the safest, the most comfortable, and it speaks directly to the Kidney warmth that the empty-cold dizziness depends on. Add the abdominal points only after a few sessions, and only if they feel good.

A Gentle Warming Sequence

Warmth for dizziness is a slow rebuild, not a quick fix. Run this sequence 3 times per week for 3–4 weeks, then take a week off and assess. Always seated or lying down — never stand while moxa is lit.

The Sequence (25–30 minutes total)

  1. Prepare (2 min): Sit somewhere comfortable with your back supported. Ventilate the room, set a timer, place a ceramic plate or moxa ash tray under the burning end, and keep water nearby. Have someone home with you for the first few sessions if you can — feeling unsteady and handling fire is a combination to avoid.
  2. Lower back (15–20 min): Moxa box over BL-23. Breathe slowly and stay seated. This is the boiler recharge.
  3. Lower abdomen (10–12 min): Moxa stick over CV-6 and CV-4 in slow circles, or move the box down. Comfortably warm, never sharp.
  4. Optional legs (5–8 min per leg): ST-36 on each leg if the empty-tank pattern fits you.
  5. Optional crown (5–8 min): GV-20, only with a partner or a secure holder, hair brushed aside.
  6. Rest (after the moxa is out): Stay seated for a few minutes before standing. Stand up slowly — the very move that triggers your dizziness deserves extra care. Drink warm water or ginger tea; avoid cold drinks and drafts for an hour.

Rhythm and Honest Feedback

  • Best window: Morning or early afternoon — the Yang part of the day, which suits building warmth.
  • Frequency: 3 times per week for 3–4 weeks, then a week off. Cumulative, not instant.
  • Listen to your body: If a session leaves you more dizzy, flushed, or irritable, stop and reassess — the empty-cold picture can occasionally be more complex than it looks, and warmth is not right for everyone. Fewer, shorter sessions, or skipping moxa entirely and using the acupressure protocol instead, are both reasonable responses.
  • Extinguish properly: Press the burning end firmly into a ceramic plate until no smoke remains.

What the Research Says

Research on moxibustion for dizziness exists but is early-stage, and it clusters around the deficiency-type and neck-related dizziness rather than acute inner-ear vertigo. Honest summary:

  • Moxibustion at GV-20 and related points: Small studies, mostly from China, have examined moxibustion — often at Baihui — for dizziness in older adults and in contexts of reduced blood flow to the brain (vertebrobasilar insufficiency). Many report improved dizziness symptoms and some report changes on ultrasound measures of cerebral blood flow. Sample sizes are small, designs vary, and researchers consistently call for larger trials.
  • Cervical vertigo trials: Randomized trials of moxibustion or warm-needle acupuncture for dizziness linked to neck problems (cervical spondylosis-related vertigo) report symptom improvements versus control groups. These too are mostly small and single-center.
  • What is NOT in the evidence: There is no strong evidence that moxibustion helps acute positional vertigo (BPPV), Meniere's episodes, or vertigo of unknown cause — and those conditions need diagnosis and standard care, not home warming.
  • The Western bridge: Light-headedness on standing overlaps with conditions like orthostatic (postural) hypotension and anemia — real, diagnosable states where the "empty" description is medically meaningful. If you suspect either, a blood pressure check and a basic blood panel are the right first steps, and they belong before (or alongside) any complementary practice.

Research note: The evidence for moxibustion and dizziness is preliminary and pattern-specific. Think of it as one supportive layer for a diagnosed or clearly deficiency-type picture — never as a substitute for figuring out what the dizziness actually is. If dizziness is unexplained, recurrent, or worsening, the first appointment to make is with a healthcare provider.

Using Moxibustion Safely

  • Dizziness plus fire needs rules. Only use moxibustion while seated or lying down, never standing. If you feel a wave of dizziness mid-session, put the moxa down on the ceramic plate immediately and sit still until it passes.
  • The crown point needs extra care. Hair is flammable. Keep the burning end well clear, brush hair away, and prefer a partner or a secure holder for GV-20. When in doubt, skip the head point — the lower-body points carry the practice.
  • High blood pressure: If your blood pressure runs high, or you have the throbbing, flushed, ringing-eared picture of Liver Yang rising, do not use moxibustion. Get the blood pressure checked and follow medical guidance.
  • Burns: Keep the moxa stick 2–3 cm from the skin; the feeling should be comfortably warm, never sharp. Set a timer and use an ash guard. Reduced sensation (e.g., diabetic neuropathy) means no moxa on that area.
  • Pregnancy: Avoid moxibustion on the lower abdomen and lower back during pregnancy. CV-4, CV-6, and the lower-back points are traditionally considered contraindicated in pregnancy.
  • Heat-prone constitutions and acute illness: Skip moxibustion during fever or acute illness, and if you regularly run hot, flush, or sweat at night, warmth is probably not your tool.
  • Smoke and fire: Moxa smoke can irritate sensitive airways — choose smokeless moxa or ventilate well. Keep sticks away from curtains and bedding, and never leave a lit stick unattended.

When to See a Doctor

Dizziness deserves a diagnosis, and moxibustion should never delay one. Seek medical attention promptly if you experience:

  • Sudden, severe dizziness or the worst headache of your life
  • Dizziness with any stroke warning sign: face drooping, arm or leg weakness, slurred speech, trouble speaking or understanding, numbness on one side, or double vision
  • Dizziness with chest pain, palpitations, or shortness of breath
  • Dizziness after a head injury
  • Dizziness with fever and a stiff neck
  • New hearing loss or persistent ringing with the dizziness
  • Fainting or nearly fainting, especially when standing
  • Dizziness that is new, unexplained, recurrent, or worsening — a workup can identify treatable causes such as inner-ear conditions, blood pressure issues, anemia, or medication side effects

Dizziness that is truly spinning, positional, or accompanied by hearing changes is very often diagnosable and very often manageable with the right professional care. Getting that diagnosis is the foundation — everything complementary builds on it.

Practical Takeaways

Quick summary — what to remember:

  • Moxibustion is a picky tool for vertigo. It fits the empty-cold picture only: light-headedness on standing or exertion, cold limbs, worse in cold weather, better with rest and warmth. It is not for throbbing, flushed, ringing, or true room-spinning dizziness.
  • First, get a diagnosis. Acute spinning (especially positional), hearing changes, or any red flag means a healthcare provider first — BPPV and Meniere's need professional care, not home heat.
  • Core points: BL-23 with a moxa box (the main move), CV-4 and CV-6 on the lower abdomen, ST-36 on the legs, optional GV-20 at the crown with a partner only.
  • Routine: 25–30 minutes seated, 3 times per week for 3–4 weeks, then a week off. Morning or early afternoon. If it makes you feel worse, stop and reassess.
  • Safety: Never standing, timer on, 2–3 cm distance, hair away from the crown point, skip with high blood pressure or in pregnancy, extinguish fully.
