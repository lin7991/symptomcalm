Moxibustion for Arthritis: Warmth Therapy for Aching Joints

Treatment Guide · 7 min read

If your joints ache worst in the morning, stiffen up before rain, and feel better after a hot shower — you already know something that TCM has built an entire therapy around: cold and damp make joints unhappy; warmth helps.

Moxibustion (灸, jiǔ) is the TCM practice of applying sustained, gentle heat to specific points on the body using a dried herb bundle made from mugwort (Artemisia vulgaris). It's one of the oldest tools in the TCM toolkit, and for people living with arthritis — osteoarthritis especially — it addresses the one thing many find hardest to articulate: the cold inside the joint.

This guide looks at how moxibustion is traditionally used for arthritis, which joint patterns it fits best, the specific points practitioners commonly target, and what the modern research actually shows. As always, think of it as one layer of a broader approach — not a replacement for your medical care.

Why Arthritis and Cold Are Linked in TCM

Western medicine describes osteoarthritis as wear-and-tear on cartilage and rheumatoid arthritis as an autoimmune attack on joints. TCM starts from a different question: what is the joint environment?

In TCM theory, joints are flow hubs — places where Qi and Blood must move continuously for the area to stay nourished, lubricated, and resilient. When the body's defensive layer (Wei Qi) is strong, the joints stay warm and well-supplied. When cold, damp, or wind get past that defense — or when the body's inner warmth (Yang Qi) runs low — the joint environment changes:

Cold-Damp Bi: The Frozen Hinge

Cold contracts, and damp is heavy. When the two settle into a joint, TCM theory describes the result as Bi Syndrome (痹证) — a blockage pattern. Picture a door hinge in winter: the grease thickens, the metal tightens, and every swing of the door is slow and creaky. That's how TCM understands a cold, damp-exposed joint: movement becomes effortful, stiffness settles in, and the whole area feels heavier and tighter than it should.

Kidney Yang Deficiency: The Low Furnace

The Kidneys are considered the deep reserve of Yang Qi — the body's central heating. As this reserve runs low (a natural process with age, and accelerated by overwork, chronic illness, or cold climates), the body's ability to keep distant regions warm drops. Knees and hands, being farthest from the core, get cold first. TCM links Kidney Yang deficiency to deep joint ache, lower-back weakness, and the type of arthritis that feels worse in winter and better in warmth.

Blood Stasis: The Sluggish Delivery Route

When cold or repeated strain slows circulation in a joint over months and years, TCM describes the result as Blood Stasis — a buildup of stagnant material that hasn't been cleared. The joint feels painful at a fixed spot, is stiff after resting, and may ache more at night. The warmth from moxibustion is traditionally seen as a way to re-melt this sluggishness and get the delivery route moving again.

These three patterns frequently overlap in real life — a person can have Cold-Damp Bi in the knees, a low furnace overall, and a stubborn knot of stagnation in one particular joint. What unites them is a shared TCM conclusion: the joint needs more warmth and better flow. That's precisely the niche moxibustion was designed to fill.

The Arthritis Patterns Moxibustion Fits (and the One It Doesn't)

Moxibustion is not a one-size-fits-all joint therapy. In TCM practice, its use depends on the pattern — and one pattern is a clear no. Use this quick self-check to see where you land:

✅ Best fit: Cold-Damp pattern

Signs: joints ache worse in cold or damp weather · morning stiffness that eases with movement or warmth · a heavy, tight feeling in the joint · hands, knees, or feet that run cold generally.

Why moxibustion fits: heat is the direct counter to cold, and its deep-penetrating warmth is traditionally seen as the most effective way to reach into a joint that surface heat can't touch.

✅ Good fit: Kidney Yang deficiency pattern

Signs: deep, chronic joint ache plus lower-back weakness · feeling cold overall, especially the legs · fatigue · symptoms worse in winter, better in warm rooms or under blankets.

Why moxibustion fits: moxa warmth applied to the lower back and abdomen is traditionally used to support the body's deep reserve — a furnace-top-up rather than a spot-repair.

✅ Moderate fit: Blood Stasis pattern

Signs: pain at a fixed spot · joints that stiffen after sitting still · nighttime ache · a history of old injury in the joint.

Why moxibustion fits: warmth encourages movement in sluggish circulation, and repeated sessions are traditionally considered a gradual way to re-activate a stagnant area.

❌ Not a fit: Heat / red-hot flare pattern

Signs: joints that are visibly red, hot to the touch, and swollen · burning pain · a recent gout attack or active rheumatoid flare · fever alongside the joint pain.

Why moxibustion doesn't fit: in TCM terms this is Heat, not Cold — adding warmth would be like pouring fuel on a fire. If your joint is hot, red, and swollen, skip the moxa and see a healthcare provider; a hot, swollen joint always warrants a professional opinion.

If you're unsure which pattern you have, ask a simple question: does warmth make this joint feel better, or worse? Better = a cold-type pattern, and moxibustion fits. Worse, or no change with redness present = a heat-type pattern, and this therapy isn't the right tool.

Where the Warmth Goes: Key Points for Arthritic Joints

Moxibustion is not applied randomly — it targets specific points where warmth can reach the joint and its surrounding channels most effectively. The points below are those most commonly used in traditional practice and clinical research for arthritis. Always work with a qualified TCM practitioner, especially when you're new to moxibustion — precise point location and safe technique make a real difference.

PointLocationWhy It's Used for Arthritis
ST-36 (Zusanli)Below the kneecap, about four finger-widths down the outer shinThe classic "tune-up" point for the whole lower body — used in most arthritis protocols as an anchor for warmth and circulation.
ST-35 (Dubi)In the hollow just below the kneecap, on the outer side of the knee tendonLiterally translates to "calf's nose" — sits right beside the knee joint, delivering warmth directly to the most common arthritis site.
EX-LE-4 (Neixiyan)In the hollow below the kneecap, inner side of the knee tendonPairs with ST-35 to bracket the knee from both sides — the classic knee pair in moxibustion studies.
GB-34 (Yanglingquan)Outer side of the lower leg, in the depression just below the knee jointKnown as the "meeting point of tendons" — traditionally used for joint and tendon stiffness anywhere in the body.
LI-4 (Hegu)On the back of the hand, in the web between thumb and index fingerA general point for pain and the upper body; commonly warmed for hand and wrist arthritis. Not used during pregnancy.
BL-23 (Shenshu)Lower back, about two finger-widths from the spine at waist levelThe "Kidney back point" — the classic location for supporting Kidney Yang and the deep warmth that the joints depend on.

A Gentle Warming Routine for Aching Joints

If you and a qualified practitioner decide moxibustion is appropriate for your pattern, a typical home routine might look something like this. This is a general illustration of how the therapy is traditionally structured — not a prescription. Your practitioner will tailor points, duration, and frequency to your specific joint and pattern.

For knees (the most common site)

  1. Prepare: sit with the knee bent and supported. Have your moxa stick lit and burning evenly, with a safe catching tray or moxa box ready. Ensure the room is ventilated.
  2. Warm the anchor: circle ST-36 (Zusanli) slowly with the moxa stick for 2–3 minutes, keeping it a comfortable distance away — warm, never burning.
  3. Target the joint: move to ST-35 and EX-LE-4, circling each for 3–5 minutes. The warmth should feel pleasant and penetrating, not hot or uncomfortable.
  4. Finish wide: sweep GB-34 (Yanglingquan) for 2–3 minutes to close the session.
  5. Frequency: in traditional practice this might be done 2–3 times per week, with a rest day between sessions. Sessions typically run 10–15 minutes per knee.

For the lower back (Kidney support)

  1. Lie face-down with the lower back exposed and warm.
  2. Use a moxa box placed over the BL-23 (Shenshu) area, or circle both sides of the spine with a moxa stick for 10–15 minutes total.
  3. Some practitioners combine this with CV-4 (Guanyuan), about four finger-widths below the navel, to support the body's deep reserve. Ask your practitioner whether this fits your pattern.

Aftercare

  • Drink warm water or ginger tea afterward — the traditional guidance is to avoid cold drinks and cold baths for about an hour.
  • Rest for 10–15 minutes. Many people feel a pleasant, deep warmth radiating from the area.
  • Extinguish the moxa completely in sand, water, or a sealed metal container. Never leave a burning or smoldering stick unattended.
  • If your joint feels more sore, red, or hot after a session, pause and check in with your practitioner — that's a sign the pattern may be different than assumed.

What the Research Says

Moxibustion research for arthritis is a growing but still early field. The strongest evidence cluster is in knee osteoarthritis, where randomized trials and systematic reviews have investigated moxibustion more than in any other joint condition:

  • Knee osteoarthritis trials: randomized controlled trials comparing moxibustion with sham moxibustion or conventional care have reported improvements in pain scores and joint function for knee osteoarthritis. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 randomized trials concluded that moxibustion was associated with reduced knee pain and improved function compared with control groups, while noting that most trials were small and at risk of bias.
  • Warmth mechanisms: laboratory studies suggest that the far-infrared component of moxa heat can penetrate several centimeters into tissue — deeper than a surface heating pad — which aligns with the TCM claim that moxibustion reaches "into" the joint rather than just warming the skin.
  • Inflammation markers: preliminary studies in osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis have examined whether moxibustion is associated with changes in inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and TNF-α. Results are mixed and mostly from small samples — researchers consistently call for larger, higher-quality trials before drawing conclusions.
  • What the reviews agree on: authors across these studies describe the evidence as preliminary, note methodological limitations, and emphasize that moxibustion should be considered a complementary practice alongside — never instead of — conventional arthritis care.

In short: the early science is encouraging, the direction matches the traditional logic (heat, applied deeply and repeatedly, appears to support joint comfort), and the field is honest about needing better studies. That's a reasonable evidence profile for a complementary comfort measure — not a basis for abandoning your medical treatment plan.

Using Moxibustion Safely

Moxibustion is generally considered low-risk when performed properly, but it is a burn risk, and a few situations call for extra caution:

  • Hot, red, or swollen joints: never apply moxa to an actively inflamed joint — gout flares, hot rheumatoid flares, or any joint that's hot to the touch. Warmth can worsen heat-type inflammation.
  • Pregnancy: several points commonly used in moxibustion — including LI-4 (Hegu) and lower-abdomen points — are traditionally avoided during pregnancy. If you are or may be pregnant, work only with a qualified practitioner who is experienced in prenatal care.
  • Reduced skin sensation: if you have diabetes-related neuropathy or any condition that reduces feeling in your hands or feet, you may not notice a burn developing. Extra distance from the skin and a second person supervising are strongly advised.
  • Poor circulation or fragile skin: take extra care, use shorter sessions, and keep the moxa further from the skin.
  • On top of blood-thinning medication or recent surgery: check with your healthcare provider before adding moxibustion to your routine.
  • Never fall asleep during a session. A lit moxa stick left unattended is a fire hazard. Use a proper holder and extinguishing container.

When to See a Doctor

Seek medical attention promptly if:
  • A joint becomes suddenly hot, red, swollen, or intensely painful — this can signal gout, infection, or an inflammatory flare that needs proper evaluation.
  • Joint pain is accompanied by fever, chills, or unexplained weight loss.
  • You have new joint symptoms that don't improve with rest and over-the-counter care within a couple of weeks.
  • You have rheumatoid arthritis or another diagnosed inflammatory condition and notice a flare that doesn't respond to your usual management.

Practical Takeaways

  • Moxibustion fits cold-type arthritis, not hot flares. If warmth relieves your joint and cold aggravates it, moxa is a traditional match. If the joint is red, hot, or swollen — skip it and see a doctor.
  • The warmth is the medicine. Moxa heat penetrates deeper than a surface heating pad, and repeated sessions are the traditional model — consistency matters more than one long session.
  • Target points, not random spots: ST-36, ST-35, EX-LE-4, GB-34, and LI-4 are the points most commonly used for arthritic joints, with BL-23 for the Kidney layer.
  • Use it as one layer, not the whole plan: keep up with your medical care, gentle movement, and the other supports your provider recommends.
  • Safety first: proper technique, a real moxa holder, full extinguishing, and practitioner guidance — especially during pregnancy or with reduced skin sensation.

If your joints tell you the same story every winter — cold makes it worse, warmth makes it better — moxibustion offers a traditional answer to that specific problem. It's a simple idea, thousands of years old, and increasingly supported by early research: give the cold joint some deep, patient warmth, and let the body's own circulation do the rest.
