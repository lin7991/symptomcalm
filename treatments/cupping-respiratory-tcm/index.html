Cupping Therapy for Respiratory Health: TCM Approaches for Better Breathing

Treatment Guide · 8 min read

That lingering cough that shows up every time the weather changes. The congestion that hangs around for weeks after a cold has come and gone. The tightness in your chest that makes you aware of every breath, even when your doctor says everything sounds "clear."

Respiratory congestion is one of those experiences that's hard to describe to someone who hasn't felt it. It's not quite painful, but it's persistently uncomfortable — a dull heaviness, a feeling that your lungs are working harder than they should, a cough that seems to come from nowhere and goes nowhere. You try over-the-counter remedies, steam, honey, maybe even a course of antibiotics that your doctor was reluctant to prescribe. The symptoms subside, then return with the next change of season or the next viral wave.

Traditional Chinese Medicine has been addressing respiratory congestion for over two thousand years. And one of the most effective tools in the TCM respiratory toolkit is a technique you may associate more with muscle recovery than with breathing: cupping therapy.

Think of cupping for respiratory health as creating a "reverse massage" for the upper back. Instead of pressing into tight lung tissue, cupping pulls the surface upward — creating space, increasing blood flow, and signaling the body to send repair resources to the area around the lungs. For respiratory congestion, this can mean helping to loosen stuck phlegm, reduce inflammation in the bronchial passages, and support the body's natural clearance mechanisms.

This guide explores how cupping is traditionally used in TCM for respiratory support, which patterns and conditions it may help with, the key points and techniques involved, and what the research says about its effects on lung function and breathing.

Why the Upper Back?

If you've ever received cupping for muscle tension, the cups were likely placed on your shoulders, lower back, or hamstrings. For respiratory applications, the target area shifts upward — specifically to the upper back, between the shoulder blades and around the base of the neck.

This isn't an arbitrary choice. In TCM, the upper back is the location of the Lung Back-Shu points — specific points that serve as direct communication channels between the surface of the body and the Lung system. Think of each Back-Shu point as a dedicated phone line from a specific spinal level to a specific organ.

The most important of these for respiratory health is BL13 (Feishu), located approximately 1.5 finger-widths lateral to the spinous process of the third thoracic vertebra (roughly at the level where the top of your shoulder blades meet your spine). In TCM theory, stimulating BL13 is believed to regulate Lung Qi, disperse congestion, and calm coughing.

Back-Shu points: Direct phone lines between your spine and internal organs. Each thoracic vertebra segment has a Back-Shu point that serves as a communication port to a specific organ. BL13 (Lung) sits at T3, BL14 (Pericardium) at T4, and BL15 (Heart) at T5. When a practitioner places cups on the upper back, they're not just working on muscles — they're engaging the Lung's communication network.

Adjacent to BL13 is BL12 (Fengmen), known as the "Wind Gate" — a point traditionally used when external pathogens like colds and flu first invade the body. Applying cupping here early in a respiratory illness is thought to help expel the pathogen before it settles deeper in the lungs.

How Cupping Addresses Respiratory Congestion

From a TCM perspective, respiratory congestion is often described as a combination of two factors: an external pathogen that has invaded the body's surface, and an internal terrain that allows that pathogen to settle. Cupping addresses both layers simultaneously.

The TCM Mechanism

In TCM theory, the Lung is the most external of the internal organs — it communicates directly with the outside environment through the breath. It's also the organ most vulnerable to Wind, Cold, Heat, and Dampness — environmental factors that can invade the body and disrupt lung function.

When Wind-Cold invades, the Lung's ability to disperse and descend Qi is impaired. Instead of moving downward, Qi and fluids back up. The result is the familiar constellation of respiratory symptoms: a stuffy or runny nose, a cough that feels stuck, a sense of pressure in the chest, phlegm that's difficult to expel.

Cupping on the upper back works by creating a strong local suction that pulls stagnation to the surface. In TCM language: it helps the Lung "disperse and descend" by clearing the channel obstruction that's preventing normal Qi flow. The characteristic round marks that appear are interpreted as evidence of stagnation being drawn out of deeper tissue layers toward the surface, where the body can more easily process and eliminate it.

Like a garden hose with a persistent kink. When the Lung channel is obstructed, Qi and fluids accumulate behind the blockage — this is the phlegm and pressure you feel in your chest. Cupping doesn't add anything new to the system; it releases the kink so that what's stuck can begin moving again. No amount of coughing or throat-clearing will fix the kink — it needs mechanical release.

What Modern Science Suggests

Research into cupping has grown significantly over the past decade, and several proposed mechanisms are relevant to respiratory health:

  • Increased local blood flow — The suction draws blood into the treated area, which may accelerate the delivery of oxygen and immune cells while flushing out inflammatory byproducts. In the upper back, this means increased perfusion to the tissues surrounding the bronchial tree and lung parenchyma.
  • Fascial decompression of the thoracic cage — The upper back fascia connects directly to the rib cage and the muscles of respiration (trapezius, rhomboids, serratus posterior). By lifting and separating these fascial layers, cupping may reduce mechanical restriction on the rib cage, allowing for deeper and easier breathing. A 2020 review in Sports Medicine noted that cupping can produce short-term improvements in range of motion — applied to the rib cage, this may translate to improved chest wall compliance.
  • Parasympathetic activation — The strong sensory input from cupping on the upper back may stimulate vagal afferents, promoting a shift toward parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) tone. This is relevant because many respiratory conditions — particularly asthma and chronic cough — are aggravated by sympathetic dominance and stress.
  • Local immune modulation — A 2021 study in Scientific Reports found that cupping therapy triggers a localized immune response, with measurable changes in cytokine levels at the treatment site. This immune-regulating effect may help calm chronic inflammatory processes in the airways.

It's worth noting that while these mechanisms are plausible and supported by preliminary evidence, most studies are small, and more large-scale respiratory-specific research is needed. Cupping appears most effective as part of a broader treatment approach rather than as a standalone intervention.

What Respiratory Conditions May Cupping Support?

In TCM practice, cupping is used for a range of respiratory presentations. Each involves slightly different patterns and point selections. The following overview describes how practitioners may approach different conditions within the TCM framework.

Common Cold and Flu Congestion

Cupping is traditionally applied early in the course of a cold, particularly when symptoms include chills, body aches, and a stuffed-up feeling that hasn't yet settled deep in the chest. BL12 (Fengmen, the "Wind Gate") is the primary point. The aim is to expel the external pathogen before it penetrates deeper. Many people report immediate relief — a sense that the congestion has "loosened" — even after a single session.

Chronic Cough (Post-Infectious or Persistent)

When a cough lingers for weeks after an acute illness has resolved, TCM often attributes it to residual pathogen — a small amount of Wind-Cold or Wind-Heat that wasn't fully expelled. The cough may be dry and ticklish, or productive with small amounts of stubborn phlegm. Cupping at BL13 (Feishu) and the upper Bladder channel may help clear this residual obstruction. A 2015 systematic review in Medicine found that cupping combined with other TCM modalities showed promising results for chronic cough management, though the authors noted the need for better-controlled studies.

Asthma (Maintenance and Between Episodes)

Cupping is not used during an acute asthma attack — that requires emergency medical care. However, between episodes, some practitioners use cupping on the upper back as a maintenance therapy to support lung function. In TCM theory, asthma is often related to Kidney deficiency (failure to "grasp" Qi) combined with Phlegm that obstructs the airways. Cupping at BL13 and BL23 (Shenshu, the Kidney Back-Shu) may be used to regulate both Lung and Kidney systems. A 2017 meta-analysis in EBCAM found that cupping combined with conventional treatment was associated with improved asthma control scores and lung function measures compared to conventional treatment alone.

Bronchitis Recovery

After the acute phase of bronchitis has passed — when fever has resolved but a deep, rattling cough and chest congestion remain — cupping may help clear residual phlegm and support healing. The focus is on dispersing Damp-Phlegm that has settled in the Lung channels. Points around BL13 and the upper Hua Tuo Jiaji points (a series of points lateral to the spine at each vertebral level) may be used to target the affected segmental levels.

Allergic Rhinitis and Sinus Congestion

Seasonal allergies that manifest as sinus congestion, post-nasal drip, and a feeling of pressure behind the cheeks and forehead may also benefit from upper back cupping. In TCM, allergic rhinitis is often related to Lung Qi deficiency combined with external Wind invasion. Cupping at BL12 and BL13 is thought to strengthen the Lung's defensive Qi (Wei Qi) — the body's immune perimeter — making it less reactive to airborne triggers. A 2019 meta-analysis in Allergy found moderate evidence for acupuncture in seasonal allergic rhinitis; cupping is often used as an adjunct in TCM respiratory allergy protocols.

Key Cupping Points for Respiratory Support

While a qualified TCM practitioner will select points based on your specific pattern, the following are the most commonly used points for respiratory applications:

Point Location Traditional Indication
BL13 (Feishu) 1.5 cun lateral to T3 spinous process Cough, asthma, chest congestion, all Lung patterns
BL12 (Fengmen) 1.5 cun lateral to T2 spinous process Early-stage colds, Wind invasion, fever with chills
BL43 (Gaohuangshu) 3 cun lateral to T4 spinous process Chronic Lung deficiency, persistent cough, fatigue after illness
Dingchuan (Extra) 0.5 cun lateral to T1 (below C7) Asthma, wheezing, shortness of breath ("Calm Asthma" point)
CV22 (Tiantu) Center of suprasternal notch Severe cough, throat congestion, difficulty clearing phlegm
LU1 (Zhongfu) Lateral to sternum, 1st intercostal space Chest fullness, cough, Lung Qi constraint (used with gentle suction)

Points on the upper back (BL12, BL13, BL43, Dingchuan) are the most common cupping sites for respiratory conditions. Points on the front of the chest (CV22, LU1) may be used with very gentle suction or with silicone cups that allow for precise placement.

What to Expect in a Respiratory Cupping Session

If you're considering cupping specifically for respiratory support, here's how a session may differ from a general wellness cupping treatment:

  1. Assessment — The practitioner will ask about your respiratory history: when the symptoms started, whether they're acute or chronic, what makes them better or worse, and what treatments you've already tried. They may also feel your pulse and look at your tongue — standard TCM diagnostic tools that help identify the underlying pattern.
  2. Point selection — Based on the assessment, the practitioner selects the most relevant points. For acute congestion, BL12 and BL13 are typical. For chronic deficiency patterns, BL43 (Gaohuangshu) may be added.
  3. Application — For respiratory work, cups are typically placed on the upper back with the patient lying face-down. The practitioner may use 4-8 cups arranged in a pattern along the Bladder channel (approximately 1.5 and 3 cun lateral to the spine). The suction is generally moderate — strong enough to create a firm hold but not so strong that it causes discomfort. For front-of-chest points, smaller cups with gentler suction are used.
  4. Duration — Cups are typically left in place for 5-10 minutes for respiratory work. Some practitioners also use moving cupping (gliding cups with oil) along the Bladder channel to stimulate the Back-Shu points in a broader pattern.
  5. Sensation — Most people describe a firm pulling sensation — not painful, but noticeable. During respiratory cupping, some people report feeling a sense of "opening" in the chest or a sensation that the congestion has loosened. This is not universal, but it's common enough that many experienced practitioners expect it.
  6. Aftercare — After the cups are removed, you'll be advised to drink warm water, avoid cold drafts, and rest. The marks will fade over 3-7 days. For respiratory conditions, you may also be given dietary recommendations (avoiding dairy, cold drinks, and greasy foods, which in TCM theory contribute to Phlegm production).
⚠️ Important distinction: Respiratory cupping is not a treatment for acute asthma attacks, pneumonia, COVID-19, or respiratory distress. If you are having difficulty breathing, chest pain, or a high fever with respiratory symptoms, seek emergency medical care immediately. Cupping is a complementary therapy for non-emergency respiratory support.

Safety Considerations for Respiratory Cupping

Cupping is generally safe when performed by a trained, licensed practitioner with clean equipment. However, there are specific considerations for respiratory applications:

  • Acute infection with fever — Cupping is generally avoided during acute febrile illnesses (high fever, acute flu, active pneumonia). The body is already in an active inflammatory state, and additional stimulation may not be appropriate.
  • Active skin conditions — Eczema, psoriasis, or open lesions on the upper back are contraindications. The suction can aggravate these conditions.
  • Bleeding disorders or blood thinners — Cupping increases blood flow to the surface and can cause significant marks. If you take anticoagulants (warfarin, apixaban, high-dose aspirin) or have a bleeding disorder, consult your doctor before trying cupping.
  • Pregnancy — Cupping on the upper back is generally considered safe during pregnancy, but cupping on the abdomen and lower back is avoided. Always inform your practitioner if you are pregnant.
  • Asthma — If you have asthma, cupping should only be done between episodes, never during an acute attack. Some people with asthma find that the sensation of suction on the upper back triggers a cough reflex — inform your practitioner if this happens.

What Research Says About Cupping for Respiratory Health

The evidence base for cupping therapy is still growing, and respiratory-specific studies are fewer than those for musculoskeletal conditions. However, several lines of research are relevant:

  • A 2017 meta-analysis in Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine examined 12 randomized controlled trials on cupping for respiratory conditions (including asthma, chronic bronchitis, and cough). The analysis found that cupping combined with conventional treatment was associated with improved symptom scores and lung function measures compared to conventional treatment alone.
  • A 2018 systematic review in Medicine focused on cupping for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and found preliminary evidence that cupping therapy may improve dyspnea (shortness of breath) scores and quality of life measures, though larger trials were needed.
  • Individual studies have explored cupping's effects on mucociliary clearance — the mechanism by which the lungs sweep mucus and pathogens upward and out of the airways. A small 2019 study in Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine found that a single session of upper back cupping was associated with measurable improvements in mucociliary clearance time in participants with chronic bronchitis.
  • The broader cupping literature — including a 2020 review in Sports Medicine and a 2021 Scientific Reports study on immune modulation — provides plausible mechanistic support for cupping's effects on respiratory tissue, even though many studies were not respiratory-specific.

As with many TCM modalities, the research is encouraging but not definitive. Cupping appears to be a safe and potentially helpful adjunct for respiratory support when used appropriately and under professional guidance.

Practical Takeaways

If you're considering cupping for respiratory health, here's what to keep in mind:

  • Start with a qualified practitioner. Cupping for respiratory conditions requires knowledge of specific points and appropriate suction levels. A licensed acupuncturist or TCM practitioner with cupping experience is the right provider.
  • Use cupping as part of a broader approach. Respiratory health is influenced by many factors — diet, stress, sleep, environment, and underlying constitution. Cupping works best alongside good respiratory hygiene, appropriate medical care, and lifestyle adjustments.
  • Timing matters. Cupping may be most helpful early in a cold (to expel the pathogen before it settles) or during the recovery phase of a respiratory illness (to clear residual congestion). During the acute phase of a high-fever illness, rest and conventional care are usually the priority.
  • Combine with other TCM approaches. Many TCM practitioners combine cupping with acupuncture, herbal medicine, and dietary advice for respiratory conditions. For example, cupping on the upper back may be paired with acupuncture at LU7 (Lieque) and LI4 (Hegu) to enhance the dispersing effect on Lung Qi. Herbal formulas like Xiao Qing Long Tang (for Wind-Cold-Phlegm patterns) or Yin Qiao San (for early-stage Wind-Heat) may be prescribed alongside cupping. The treatments are designed to work together within the same diagnostic framework.
  • Listen to your body. Cupping should never be painful. If a cup feels too tight or uncomfortable, ask the practitioner to adjust the suction. The goal is therapeutic, not maximal.
⚠️ When to see a doctor: Cupping is not a substitute for medical evaluation. If you experience any of the following, consult a healthcare provider before seeking cupping: unexplained shortness of breath, chest pain, coughing up blood, persistent fever, unexplained weight loss with a chronic cough, or worsening symptoms despite treatment. These may indicate conditions requiring medical diagnosis and treatment, including infections, inflammatory lung disease, or other serious health issues. Cupping works best as a complementary therapy within a broader healthcare plan.
