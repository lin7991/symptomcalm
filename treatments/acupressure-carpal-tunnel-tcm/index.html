Acupressure for Carpal Tunnel: A Point-by-Point Wrist Protocol

Treatment Guide · 9 min read

It is 2 a.m. and your hand is gone. Not asleep in the ordinary way — gone. You sit up, shake it out, flex the wrist, and wait for the pins and needles to crawl back down your fingers. By breakfast everything feels roughly normal again. Then by mid-afternoon, four hours into the keyboard, the dull ache settles back into your wrist like a low hum you cannot switch off.

Carpal tunnel syndrome affects an estimated 3–6% of adults, and it is the most common nerve compression problem in the upper limb. The conventional explanation is simple and mechanical: the median nerve is being squeezed in a narrow passage at the wrist. That explanation is useful — and worth checking with a doctor, because some cases do need medical care. But it leaves an obvious question unanswered: if the passage is the same width on both sides, why does one person's wrist stay quiet while another person's flares every time they work overtime?

That question is where a different map becomes useful. This article is not about the causes of carpal tunnel or its TCM pattern descriptions — we cover that in a companion guide. This one is strictly the hands-on layer: which points get used, exactly where they sit, how hard to press, in what order, and — just as importantly — where you should never press at all.

Why a Point on Your Forearm Can Change a Nerve You Never Touch

Picture the carpal tunnel as a corridor. Nine tendons and one cable run through it side by side, and the corridor has a fixed width. When the cable starts complaining, most people instinctively go straight to the corridor and press on it. That is understandable — and it is also the least useful thing you can do when the corridor is already inflamed.

Think about what actually changes day to day. The corridor does not change width. What changes is the environment around the corridor: how swollen the surroundings are, how tense the forearm muscles are, how well the tissue is supplied with fluid and Blood, how much you have been gripping. In TCM theory, those surrounding influences are described as the flow of Qi and Blood through the channels that pass across the wrist — and the wrist is where several of them converge.

Here is the analogy that makes acupressure click: the points are maintenance hatches along a long cable run. If a fault shows up at one section of the line, you do not have to open the wall at that exact spot. You open a hatch upstream, where the line is accessible, and change what the line is carrying. That is why a point two finger-widths above your wrist crease, or down near your knee, is traditionally used for a problem in your hand.

  • Local points (on and immediately around the wrist) act like hatches right at the junction box. Fast, direct, and best kept gentle.
  • Forearm points work the segment of the line between the junction box and the main cable — useful when the wrist feels tight and the forearm feels hard.
  • Distant points (below the elbow, even the leg) are the upstream switches. In TCM theory they act on the quality of what the line carries — the supply side rather than the site of the complaint.
The TCM view in one sentence: A nerve can be compressed mechanically and, at the same time, the channel system around it may be stagnant or under-supplied — and the second half of that picture is what acupressure works on.

The Wrist Point Map, in Three Tiers

There are dozens of points a practitioner might choose from. For at-home use, a tiered set is easier to learn, and it keeps you from doing too much pressing in the one place that is already irritated. Work the tiers in order.

Tier 1 — On and Beside the Tunnel (local)

  • PC-7 (Daling — "Great Mound"). Dead centre of the inner wrist crease, in the soft dip between the two central tendons. This is the point that sits most directly over the tunnel entrance, and it is the one most often paired with forearm points in clinical research on carpal tunnel. Press with the pad of the opposite thumb, angled slightly toward the elbow rather than straight down, 30–60 seconds.
  • PC-6 (Neiguan — "Inner Gate"). Two finger-widths above the inner wrist crease, between the two prominent tendons. Note that this one is upstream of the tunnel rather than on it — which is exactly why it is useful when the wrist is too tender to press directly.
  • LU-9 (Taiyuan — "Supreme Abyss"). At the thumb-side end of the inner wrist crease, in the hollow just beside the thumb-side tendon. Traditionally used when the wrist feels weak or the pulse feels thin at the wrist.
  • HT-7 (Shenmen — "Spirit Gate"). At the little-finger end of the inner wrist crease, in the hollow beside the tendon. This is the classic point for restlessness and disturbed sleep — which matters here, because a lot of carpal tunnel suffering happens at night rather than during the day.
  • LI-5 (Yangxi) and SJ-4 (Yangchi). On the back of the wrist, in the two dips either side of the central bump of the wrist joint. The dorsal side gets ignored by most self-care routines, yet it is where the wrist feels stiff rather than sore.

Tier 2 — The Forearm Circuit

  • SJ-5 (Waiguan — "Outer Gate"). Two finger-widths above the wrist crease on the back of the forearm, midway between the two forearm bones. The counterpart to PC-6 on the other side of the arm.
  • LI-10 (Shousanli) and LI-11 (Quchi — "Pool at the Crook"). LI-10 sits two finger-widths below the elbow crease on the outer forearm; LI-11 sits in the hollow at the outer end of the elbow crease when the arm is bent. LI-11 is the workhorse for the whole arm in TCM theory — traditionally used for channel obstruction running the length of the limb.
  • The elbow-crease trio: PC-3 (Quze), LU-5 (Chize), HT-3 (Shaohai). Three points spread across the inner elbow crease, one on each of the three Yin lines of the arm. In the acupuncture research on carpal tunnel described further down this page, forearm points including PC-3, HT-3 and LU-5 were commonly combined with PC-7 and SJ-5 — a useful reminder that the hand is part of an arm, not an isolated joint.

Tier 3 — The Distant Switches

  • LI-4 (Hegu — "Joining Valley"). In the fleshy web between thumb and index finger, on the highest point of the muscle when the thumb and finger are brought together. Traditionally used to move Qi and Blood through the entire arm. Skip this point during pregnancy.
  • ST-36 (Zusanli). Four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width outside the shin bone. The body's general "build Qi and Blood" point — relevant because the wrist tissues need supply, not only release.
  • SP-6 (Sanyinjiao). Four finger-widths directly above the inner ankle bone, just behind the shin bone. Traditionally used to nourish Blood and Yin. Skip this point during pregnancy.
  • KI-3 (Taixi). In the hollow midway between the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon. The deepest reserve point of the set — used when the hands and wrists feel depleted rather than inflamed.
  • SI-3 (Houxi). On the little-finger side of the hand, in the crease that forms when you make a loose fist. Traditionally described as opening the Governing Vessel, the line that runs up the spine — which is why it is often paired with neck and posture work.
A practical shortcut: If you only ever learn four points, learn PC-7 and PC-6 on the wrist, SJ-5 on the forearm, and LI-11 at the elbow. That four-point set covers the local layer, the forearm segment, and the upper arm in a single line, and takes about three minutes.

How to Tell You Have Actually Found the Point

Most failed acupressure is not a technique problem — it is a location problem. Pressing the wrong spot hard does nothing useful, and it can leave you sore. Four checks will keep you honest:

  1. The pocket test. A point usually sits in a small depression, a gap between tendons, or the edge of a bone — not on the flat of a bone or the middle of a muscle belly. Your fingertip should feel it settle into a dip.
  2. The quality-of-sensation test. Correct pressure produces a distinct, deep, slightly spreading sensation — often described as "not quite pain." If it is sharp and superficial, you are on a nerve or a tendon, or pressing too hard. If you feel nothing at all, you are probably a few millimetres off.
  3. The breathing test. If you are holding your breath, bracing your jaw, or pulling the arm away, you are pressing too hard. Aim for a 4–6 out of 10 — firm enough to feel, gentle enough to breathe through.
  4. The next-day test. The area should feel warmer and looser for a while afterwards. If you have a bruise, or the wrist is more tender the next day than before, your pressure was too much. Reduce it, and press for less time.

For most points, use the pad of the thumb (not the tip), press in slowly over three or four seconds, hold for 30–90 seconds with small circular movements, and release slowly. Breathe out as you press. One to two rounds per point, both sides. Twice a day is plenty — acupressure is not a case where more is better.

Three Signals That Change Which Points You Emphasize

The same set of points gets used differently depending on what your wrist is actually doing that week. These three signals are the point-selection layer — the fuller TCM pattern descriptions behind each one live in our companion guide to carpal tunnel, so we will keep them to a line each here.

Signal 1: Worse with use, better with rest — "The Overworked Line"

  • What it feels like: Sharp or achy discomfort clearly linked to activity. Fine on holiday, sore after a deadline week. Stiff in the morning, looser once you get moving.
  • Where to put the emphasis: Tier 1 and Tier 2. Work PC-7, PC-6, SJ-5 and LI-11, and add gentle wrist circles and finger spreads between rounds. Movement between presses matters more than pressure here.
  • What to avoid: Long static holds on a hard-pressed PC-7 on a day the wrist already worked hard. Keep local pressure light and spend longer on the forearm.

Signal 2: Puffy, warm, heavy, worse after rich food or humid weather — "The Congested Tunnel"

  • What it feels like: A fuller, heavier wrist rather than a sharp one. May be warm to the touch. Often accompanied by a heavy body feeling, sluggish digestion, or flare-ups after alcohol, fried food or a humid week.
  • Where to put the emphasis: De-emphasize deep local pressure and work upstream instead: SP-9 (inner lower leg, just below the knee joint on the inner side), ST-40 (midway between the kneecap and the outer ankle bone), plus gentle LI-11. Cooling, draining foods and steady hydration support this pattern in TCM theory.
  • What to avoid: Pressing firmly into a hot, swollen wrist — that is the one situation where local pressure is the wrong move. Also skip hot soaks during an acute flare.

Signal 3: Numbness more than soreness, worse at night, dry hands — "The Under-Supplied Circuit"

  • What it feels like: Tingling and deadness dominate over pain. You wake with a numb hand. Fingertips feel dry, nails are brittle, and the discomfort does not fully resolve between episodes.
  • Where to put the emphasis: Tier 3. ST-36, SP-6, KI-3 and LI-11, plus generous gentle work on PC-6 and HT-7 in the evening. This is the pattern where local tissue needs supply more than release.
  • What to avoid: Vigorous deep-tissue work. And remember the two pregnancy cautions: LI-4 and SP-6 are traditionally avoided during pregnancy — carpal tunnel is common in pregnancy for fluid-related reasons, so if that applies to you, work with your midwife or doctor and keep to gentle forearm points.

One more consideration worth knowing: if your wrist is stiffer in cold weather and eases with warmth, add five minutes of warmth before pressing — a warm shower, a wheat bag, or simply a scarf wrapped around the hand. In TCM theory, pressing into a cold-contracted area without warming it first is working uphill.

The Three Protocols

Points are easy to learn and easy to forget. Three short protocols, tied to moments already in your day, do more good than an ambitious routine you run twice and abandon.

The 5-Minute Desk Reset

Run this every 60–90 minutes during screen work — the length of the break matters far less than the frequency.

  1. Minute 1 — release the forearm. PC-6 and SJ-5, 30 seconds each side, pressing and breathing out.
  2. Minute 2 — the dorsal side. LI-5 and SJ-4 on the back of the wrist, 20 seconds each. Most people have never pressed here, and it is where stiffness lives.
  3. Minute 3 — elbow. LI-11, 30 seconds each arm, in the hollow at the outer elbow crease.
  4. Minute 4 — movement. Ten slow wrist circles each direction, then spread the fingers wide for five seconds and make a soft fist, ten times.
  5. Minute 5 — position reset. Check that your wrist is straight, not bent back, and that your forearm is supported.

The Night Protocol (for 2 a.m. Numbness)

Night is when carpal tunnel tends to make itself known, and it is also where some of the clearest research interest sits. In one randomised trial of 77 people with mild-to-moderate carpal tunnel syndrome, acupuncture was compared with a short course of oral steroid; both groups improved, and among the five symptoms tracked, nocturnal awakening was the one that improved significantly more in the acupuncture group at week four. Other trials report similar improvements in night symptoms. That is a suggestive signal rather than proof — but it fits what practitioners have described for a long time.

The practical overnight routine:

  • Before bed (3 minutes). PC-7, PC-6 and HT-7 on the inner wrist, 45 seconds each side. Finish with slow breathing — four counts in, six to eight counts out.
  • Wrist position matters more than pressure. The most heavily researched mechanical measure for night symptoms is a neutral-position wrist splint, worn at night. The reasoning is straightforward: pressure inside the tunnel is at its lowest when the wrist is straight, and many people sleep with the wrist curled or bent. If you use a splint, keep pressing the points — the two are not in competition.
  • If you wake numb (90 seconds). Shake the hand out gently, then press PC-6 and SJ-5 only — no deep pressure on the wrist itself while it is tingling. Finish with ten slow wrist circles before settling back down.
  • Reduce evening load. Long evening scrolling sessions on a phone, tightly gripping a book, or sleeping with the hand curled under the pillow all keep the tunnel compromised through the night.

The Two-Minute Morning Wake-Up

Hands often feel stiffest in the first twenty minutes of the day, when circulation is low.

  • Finger fan and soft fist, ten repetitions.
  • Ten slow wrist circles each direction.
  • LI-4 and PC-6, 30 seconds each hand.
  • Thirty seconds of gentle shaking out, wrists loose, as if flicking water off your hands.

Where Not to Press: The Wrist's No-Go Zones

This section matters more than the point list. Acupressure is low-risk, but the wrist is a small space with a lot of important structures in it, and there are situations where pressing is the wrong choice.

  • Never press hard directly into an acutely swollen, hot wrist. Work upstream — PC-6, SJ-5, LI-11 — and let the local area settle.
  • Numbness is not permission. A numb patch tells you the signal is not getting through. Because you cannot feel what you are doing, the risk of pressing too hard without noticing is higher, not lower. Keep pressure light.
  • After carpal tunnel surgery, do not press the surgical area until your surgeon or hand therapist says it is fine. Scar tissue and recovering nerve tissue should not be loaded by self-massage. Work on the forearm, elbow and leg points instead, if you feel well enough.
  • Reduced sensation from other causes — diabetic neuropathy, cervical nerve involvement, or any condition affecting hand feeling — calls for a lighter approach and clearance from your doctor first.
  • Bleeding disorders or blood-thinning medication: press very gently or skip, because bruising becomes easy.
  • Pregnancy: avoid LI-4 and SP-6, and keep stimulation gentle. Carpal tunnel is common in pregnancy and usually eases after birth — but get it assessed rather than relying on self-care.
  • Never press into broken, infected, bruised or inflamed skin, or over varicose veins, cysts or any new lump you have not had looked at.
  • Stop immediately if pressing makes the numbness spread, makes tingling worse, or produces a sharp electric jolt down the arm. That is a signal the nerve is being irritated, not helped.

What Research Suggests About Acupressure and the Carpal Tunnel

The honest summary is that the evidence is promising but not settled, and it is strongest for the needle version of this practice rather than finger pressure. Since acupressure uses the same point locations, that research is still relevant — with appropriate caution.

  • Night symptoms: In a randomised controlled trial of 77 people with mild-to-moderate carpal tunnel syndrome, eight acupuncture sessions over four weeks improved symptom scores broadly, and nocturnal awakening improved significantly more than in the steroid comparison group at week four, along with an improvement in distal motor latency on nerve conduction testing (Yang et al., Clinical Journal of Pain, 2009).
  • Which points were studied: In a neuroimaging study of electroacupuncture for carpal tunnel syndrome, the protocol was standardised on TE-5 (SJ-5) and PC-7 with additional local forearm points drawn from HT-3, PC-3, SI-4, LI-5, LI-10 and LU-5 — the same neighbourhood described in the Tier 1 and Tier 2 map above. The study reported changes in sensory cortex connectivity linked to median nerve function (Fisher et al., 2021).
  • Systematic reviews are more cautious: A Cochrane review of acupuncture and related interventions for carpal tunnel syndrome concluded that evidence for benefit remains of low to very low certainty, with no clear difference from night splints in the studies available. It is worth knowing this before you decide how much to expect.
  • Wrist position has the clearest mechanical evidence: Cochrane reviews of splinting report that a neutral-position night splint may improve overall symptoms compared with no splint, based on low-certainty evidence — the rationale being that pressure on the median nerve is lowest when the wrist is straight.
  • Manual work on the surrounding tissue: A 2022 meta-analysis of manual therapy for carpal tunnel reported improvements in pain, function and some nerve conduction measures in mild to moderate cases, though study quality varied.
How to read this honestly: Nothing here suggests acupressure replaces splinting, medical assessment or surgery where those are indicated. The reasonable framing is that gentle point work may support comfort, circulation and sleep quality alongside standard care — and that the strongest evidence in this area concerns the wrist's position overnight, which is free and worth doing tonight.

When to See a Doctor

Seek medical assessment rather than self-care if you notice any of the following:

  • Constant numbness that no longer comes and goes, or numbness that is present all day.
  • Weakness or visible wasting at the base of the thumb — a small dent where the fleshy pad used to be. This is a late sign and needs prompt assessment.
  • Dropping objects, trouble with buttons, or grip that fails unexpectedly.
  • Symptoms in both hands, or symptoms spreading up the arm, or neck pain with arm symptoms.
  • Numbness in the little finger and ring finger together — carpal tunnel typically spares the little finger, so this pattern points somewhere else.
  • Symptoms after a fall or wrist injury, or a new lump, swelling or deformity at the wrist.
  • Severe pain, fever, or a hot red swollen wrist, which needs urgent attention.
  • No improvement after several weeks of sensible self-care including splinting and activity changes.
  • Pregnancy-related carpal tunnel that is affecting sleep or grip — mention it to your midwife or doctor.
  • Diabetes, thyroid conditions or rheumatoid arthritis alongside wrist symptoms — these change the assessment and the plan.

Practical Takeaways

  • Learn four points, not twenty: PC-7 and PC-6 on the inner wrist, SJ-5 on the forearm, LI-11 at the elbow. Three minutes, both sides, twice a day.
  • Work in tiers: local points gently, forearm points more firmly, distant points (ST-36, SP-6, KI-3, LI-4) when the wrist feels depleted rather than inflamed.
  • Calibrate, don't push: aim for a deep "not quite pain" sensation at 4–6 out of 10. If you bruise, you pressed too hard.
  • Skip the local press when the wrist is hot and swollen. Go upstream instead, and avoid hot soaks during a flare.
  • Night is the highest-value window: 45 seconds each on PC-7, PC-6 and HT-7 before bed, wrist kept straight overnight. Position beats pressure here.
  • Always run the safety list first: no deep pressure on a numb patch, nothing over the surgical site until cleared, and skip LI-4 and SP-6 in pregnancy.
  • Self-care has a ceiling. Constant numbness, thumb-pad wasting, or dropping things means it is time for a proper assessment — not a firmer press.
