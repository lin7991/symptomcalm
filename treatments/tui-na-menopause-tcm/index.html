Tui Na for Menopause: Self-Massage Through the Transition

Treatment Guide · 9 min read

Menopause doesn't arrive as a single event. It arrives as a series of small surprises: a flash of heat in a meeting, a night that ends at 3 a.m., a shoulder that has somehow become permanently braced, a lower back that aches for no reason you can name. Nothing is broken. Something is being recalibrated — and recalibration is rarely comfortable.

Tui Na (推拿, tuī ná) is the hands-on branch of TCM: Chinese therapeutic massage. Unlike acupressure, which holds still on one point, Tui Na works with movement — gliding, kneading, grasping, and rubbing. That difference matters during the menopausal transition, because a lot of what this stage feels like is energy that has stopped circulating properly: heat that pools upward, tension that parks in the shoulders, and sleep that never quite settles.

This guide teaches four self-Tui Na techniques and shows you how to match them to the four complaints that show up most often in this transition. No oils required, no appointment, no equipment beyond your own hands.

What Tui Na Does That a Static Press Can't

If you've tried acupressure and found it helpful but somehow incomplete, the missing ingredient is often motion.

Think of the difference between pressing a single button on a keyboard and playing a scale. Both are intentional. But one moves a signal through a system, and the other only pokes a spot. In TCM theory, Tui Na's gliding and kneading strokes are used to move Qi and Blood along a channel — not just to stimulate a point, but to clear the path between points.

There's a straightforward western reading too. Manual strokes influence:

  • Local circulation. Sustained pressure with movement increases blood flow to the muscle being worked, which is why a stiff trapezius feels different after two minutes of kneading than after two minutes of simply pressing.
  • Muscle tone. Slow, rhythmic compression can reduce the resting tension in a guarded muscle — the shoulder-bracing, jaw-clenching kind of tension that accumulates across a day.
  • The autonomic nervous system. Repeated, predictable, moderate-pressure touch tends to shift the body toward parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) activity. During a transition marked by vasomotor instability, a calmer nervous system is not a small thing.
  • The sensory gate. The same gate-control principle behind acupressure applies — but with a moving stimulus, a larger territory of sensory input is engaged.

Why This Transition Responds to Movement-Based Pressure

In TCM, menopause is described as a gradual decline in the Kidney system's stores of Yin — the cooling, moistening, anchoring half of the body's balance. Picture a building's climate system where the cooling loop has slowly lost capacity. The heating system isn't malfunctioning; there's simply less coolant moving through the same pipes.

Two things follow, and both are central to why Tui Na suits this stage:

  1. Heat rises when it isn't anchored. With less Yin to hold it down, Yang (warmth, activity, upward movement) drifts upward — into the face, chest, and head. The classic flash. At the same time, the lower body can feel cool or depleted, which is why so many women in this transition describe cold feet alongside a hot face.
  2. Tension parks where it can. TCM reads the menopausal emotional landscape — irritability, sudden tears, the short fuse — as Liver Qi that has less room to move. When Qi can't flow freely, it accumulates. In the body, that shows up as braced shoulders, a tight jaw, and a band of pressure across the neck. That's not a metaphor; it's where you'll find the knots.

Tui Na addresses both by moving rather than targeting. Long glides down the neck help redirect rising energy downward. Grasping the shoulders releases where the Liver has parked its frustration. Slow friction over the lower back warms and settles the Kidney region — the root, in TCM terms.

The Four Hand Techniques You Actually Need

You don't need a Tui Na certification. Four strokes cover almost everything described in this article.

🖐️ 推 Tuī — The Long Glide

What it is: A flat, steady glide. Place your whole palm (or the heel of your palm) on the skin and move in one direction with even pressure, then lift and reset rather than scrubbing back and forth.

How it's used: For descending energy — down the back of the neck, down the outer arms, down the legs. In TCM, direction carries meaning: downward strokes are traditionally used when heat or tension is rising.

Feel: Warm, broad, calming. Pressure should be firm enough to move the skin slightly, never enough to drag or pinch.

🌀 揉 Róu — The Circular Knead

What it is: Small, slow circles with the pad of your thumb, the knuckle, or the palm — staying in one area rather than travelling.

How it's used: This is your point-work stroke. It's how you address a specific point like Shenmen or Taixi without the "jabbing" quality that static pressure sometimes has.

Feel: A deep, spreading ache — the satisfying kind. Slow circles, roughly one per second.

🤏 拿 Ná — The Grasp and Squeeze

What it is: Use your thumb on one side of a muscle and your fingers on the other, pick the muscle up, and squeeze rhythmically — lift, squeeze, release, repeat.

How it's used: The trapezius (top of the shoulders), the upper arms, the calves. This is the stroke that most people recognise from a professional massage, and it's the one that unloads a day of accumulated bracing.

Feel: Intense but bearable. If you're holding your breath, ease off.

🔥 擦 Cā — The Warming Friction

What it is: Rapid back-and-forth rubbing with the palm or the fleshy base of your thumb, fast enough to generate real warmth in the skin.

How it's used: Over the lower back (the Kidney region), the sacrum, and the soles of the feet. Traditionally used where an area needs warming and settling rather than kneading.

Feel: Heat building under your palm within 20–30 seconds. Stop before the skin gets uncomfortably hot.

A 12-Minute Evening Sequence

Evening is the natural slot: the day's tension is fully loaded, and the sequence doubles as a wind-down signal. Sit somewhere warm, with your back supported. Ten to twelve minutes is the target — consistency matters more than duration.

  1. Warm your hands (30 seconds). Rub your palms together until they're hot. Cold hands make a cold treatment.
  2. Neck glide — 推 (2 minutes). Place your palm at the base of the skull and glide slowly down the back of the neck to the top of the shoulder. One direction only; lift and reset each time. Both sides. Let your exhales get longer than your inhales.
  3. Shoulder grasp — 拿 (2 minutes). Using thumb and fingers, grasp the top of each shoulder and squeeze rhythmically: lift, squeeze, release. Work from the neck outward toward the shoulder tip. One minute per side. This is where irritability usually lives.
  4. Wrist knead — 揉 (3 minutes). Find HT-7 in the crease of the inner wrist, on the little-finger side. Small circles, about a second each, for one minute. Then PC-6, three finger-widths up the inner forearm between the tendons — one minute. Switch sides. This is the sleep-setting part of the sequence.
  5. Lower-back friction — 擦 (2 minutes). With the heel of your palm, rub briskly up and down over the lower back and sacrum. You're looking for warmth, not effort. If reaching your own back is awkward, sit in a chair with a firm back and lean into your hands, or use a rounded object against a wall with light pressure.
  6. Ankle knead — 揉 (2 minutes). Knead KI-3 between the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon on each side, then KI-6 just below the inner ankle bone. Slow circles, one minute per side.
  7. Close (30 seconds). Rest both palms flat on your lower abdomen, breathe slowly, and let your hands go still. In TCM terms, you've moved the Qi; the pause is what lets it settle.

Technique essentials:

  • Press with the pad, never the fingertip. The flat of the thumb spreads force; the tip concentrates it into something sharp.
  • Aim for "satisfying ache," not pain. Around 4–5 out of 10. Sharp pain means stop and reposition.
  • Move slowly. Speed reduces the calming effect of Tui Na and increases the chance of irritating tissue. Roughly one circle or one glide per second.
  • Breathe out into the stroke. Slow exhalation relaxes the guarding that keeps a muscle tight — which is half of why the stroke works.
  • Work on skin, not clothing, where you can. Direct contact gives you better feedback about what you're actually feeling underneath.
  • Stop for numbness, tingling, shooting pain, or a spot that becomes more tender as you work.

Safety Notes for This Stage of Life

Self-massage is generally low risk, but the menopausal years come with a few specific considerations:

  • Bone density. If you have osteopenia, osteoporosis, or a history of fragility fracture, use lighter pressure and avoid strong, deep work directly over the spine and ribs. Ask your clinician whether self-massage is appropriate for you — and consider a professional who is trained to work with reduced bone density.
  • Breast and underarm tissue. If you've had breast surgery, lymph node removal, or radiation, do not massage the chest, underarm, or affected arm unless your care team has specifically cleared it. This matters because of lymphedema risk.
  • Legs and circulation. Avoid deep, vigorous pressure on the calves if you have varicose veins, a history of blood clots, or current leg swelling — especially one-sided swelling.
  • After a hot flash. If a flash comes with palpitations, dizziness, or chest discomfort, stop and let it pass; if chest pain or shortness of breath occurs, seek urgent medical care.
  • Skin. Skip any area with a rash, bruise, open wound, recent injection, or unusual tenderness.
  • Bruising easily. If you're on an anticoagulant or bruise very easily, keep pressure light and stop at the first sign of a new bruise.
  • Tell your clinician. Self-massage doesn't interact with hormone therapy or other medications, but your care team should know what you're doing alongside your plan.

What Research Says About Massage and Menopause

As with most hands-on therapies, the research is promising in direction and modest in scale. An honest summary:

  • Massage for menopausal symptoms: Small randomised trials of massage therapy in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women have reported reductions in anxiety, depressed mood, and self-reported vasomotor symptoms compared with waitlist controls. Sample sizes are small, and sham-controlled designs are difficult for hands-on treatments — so these results are suggestive rather than conclusive.
  • Acupuncture and hot flashes: Here, the literature is genuinely humbling. The largest randomised trial of acupuncture for menopausal hot flashes (published in Annals of Internal Medicine, 2016) found that real acupuncture and sham acupuncture produced similar improvements, with both better than no treatment. The honest reading is that a substantial part of the benefit may come from expectation and the ritual of care itself — which is still a real effect, but worth knowing.
  • Hand and foot massage for sleep: Several small trials in older adults and hospital settings have found that brief hand or foot massage before bed improves subjective sleep quality and reduces time to sleep onset. The mechanism proposed is autonomic — slower heart rate, more parasympathetic activity.
  • Massage and stress physiology: Reviews of massage therapy consistently report short-term reductions in self-reported stress and modest effects on cortisol and heart-rate variability. Effects are transient, which is precisely why the practice is framed as a daily or near-daily ritual rather than a one-off.
  • Neck and shoulder work: Manual therapy for neck and shoulder tension has reasonably consistent short-term evidence for reducing discomfort and improving range of motion — relevant if your irritability shows up as a braced upper body.

What none of this suggests is that self-massage replaces medical care, alters hormone levels, or changes the course of the transition. It's a low-risk practice that may improve how the transition feels — and for a stage that can last several years, how it feels matters.

When to See a Doctor

Menopause is a normal life stage, but some symptoms deserve a professional conversation. Please contact a healthcare provider if you notice any of the following:

  • Chest pain, palpitations, shortness of breath, or fainting during a hot flash — seek urgent care.
  • Any vaginal bleeding after menopause — this always warrants an assessment, even if it's light or a one-off.
  • Night sweats with unexplained weight loss, fever, or a persistent cough — these need proper evaluation.
  • Symptoms that are disrupting your sleep, work, or relationships and that you haven't discussed with a clinician. There are evidence-based options, including hormone therapy, non-hormonal medication, and cognitive behavioural approaches.
  • Low mood, anxiety, or loss of interest lasting more than a couple of weeks.
  • A new breast lump, skin change, or nipple discharge.
  • Joint pain that is new, severe, or accompanied by swelling and redness.

Self-massage is a supportive practice, not a substitute for the care that addresses the transition itself. Use both.

Practical Takeaways

Five things worth remembering:

  1. Four strokes cover it: 推 glide, 揉 knead, 拿 grasp, 擦 friction. Long glides to bring rising energy down, kneading for points, grasping for accumulated tension, friction for cold and depleted areas.
  2. Twelve minutes in the evening beats an hour on the weekend. The effect is cumulative and short-lived, which makes frequency the whole game.
  3. Direction matters. Downward glides down the neck and arms; clockwise circles on the abdomen; brisk warmth into the lower back.
  4. Match the stroke to the symptom. Flashes → glide + LI-11. Broken sleep → knead HT-7 and PC-6. Irritability → grasp the shoulders. Cold, aching, depleted → friction the lower back, knead KI-3.
  5. Adapt to your bones and your history. Lighter pressure if bone density is a concern; skip the chest and underarm entirely if you've had breast surgery or lymph node removal. When in doubt, ask your clinician.
