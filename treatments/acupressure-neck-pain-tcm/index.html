Acupressure for Neck Pain: The Points That Reach the Deep Tension

Treatment Guide · 7 min read

It is four in the afternoon and your neck has already filed a complaint. There is a band of tightness running from the base of your skull out across both shoulders, and when you try to turn your head to check behind you in the car, the movement stops short of where it should. You have stretched. You have rolled your shoulders. The relief lasts about ninety seconds, and then the same rope tightens again.

Here is the part that frustrates people most: they are working on the wrong layer. Neck tension is not one single band of muscle. It is a stack of layers, and the layer that hurts the most on the surface is very often not the layer that is holding the tension in place.

Acupressure works on this problem differently from stretching. Instead of pulling on the tight tissue, you press specific points on a channel map — some of them right on the neck, and some of them all the way down on your hand and ankle. In TCM theory, those distant points are switches on the same circuit as the neck, and pressing them can change the tone of tissue you are not touching at all.

This article focuses specifically on acupressure for the neck: which points reach which layer, how to tell whether you have actually found a point, where on the neck you must never press, and a short sequence you can run at your desk.

Tension Has Layers: Why Your Neck Stays Tight When You Stretch

Picture the neck as a tent pegged into the ground. It is held up by three different systems, and they fail in different ways.

  • The guy lines (surface layer). These are the tension lines that react to weather first. In TCM terms this is the Taiyang layer — the Bladder channel territory running down the back of the neck and the upper trapezius. This is the layer that stiffens when you sleep under an air conditioner, when a cold front moves in, or when you wake up with a "crick."
  • The poles (middle layer). The poles decide which way the tent can face. On the neck, this maps to the Shaoyang layer — the Gallbladder channel running down the side of the neck into the top of the shoulder. This layer governs rotation: checking a blind spot, turning to speak to someone beside you, tilting your ear toward your shoulder. In TCM theory it is the layer that tightens under stress and long static postures, and it is where most desk-driven neck pain lives.
  • The pegs (deep layer). The pegs are buried. They do not move when you adjust the lines. On the body, this is the deep structural layer anchored by the Governing Vessel along the spine and the small muscles at the very base of the skull. When this layer is loaded — after years of forward-head posture, or after a long stretch of accumulated strain — adjusting the surface will not hold. The pull simply returns.

That is why stretching feels good and does not last. You are loosening the guy lines while the pegs stay pulled tight. Acupressure is interesting precisely because some of the points traditionally used for the neck are not on the neck at all — they are distal points that, in TCM theory, act on the deepest layer of the channel system.

The TCM view in one sentence: The neck is where several major channels pass close to the surface, so when Qi and Blood flow slows down here it shows up physically — as heaviness, tightness, and a loss of rotation — before it shows up anywhere else.

The Zone-by-Zone Point Map

Rather than a numbered list, it helps to learn the neck's points by zone, because each zone is reached with a different hand position and a different amount of pressure. Seven points, three zones.

Zone 1 — The Wind Pool (base of the skull)

This is the zone where tension collects first and leaves last. Two points sit here, and they are close enough that you can work them together.

  • GB-20 (Fengchi — "Wind Pool"). Find the hollows at the base of your skull, in the two soft dips just below the ridge of bone behind your ears, roughly a thumb-width apart from the midline on each side. Press inward and slightly upward, toward the opposite eye. In TCM theory GB-20 is the classic point for Wind — the influence used to explain why stiffness arrives with weather, drafts, and air conditioning. It is also traditionally used for the heavy, foggy head that comes with a tight neck.
  • BL-10 (Tianzhu). A finger-width below and slightly inward from GB-20, on the same muscle band at the back of the neck. This is the Taiyang point: traditionally used for the "too heavy to hold up" feeling at the back of the head.

How to work it: Use both thumbs, or the knuckles of both index fingers, and press inward and upward. Hold for 30 to 60 seconds per point while you breathe slowly and let your shoulders drop. This is also the zone where slow, small circles work better than static pressure.

Zone 2 — The Shoulder Ridge

The top of the shoulder is the most commonly self-reachable zone, and it is also the zone that tells you the most about your stress level. If the muscle here feels like a hard rope, that is information.

  • GB-21 (Jianjing). Find the midpoint of the line between the base of your neck and the bony tip of your shoulder. It sits on the highest point of the muscle ridge. Press down firmly and hold. In TCM theory this point releases the "shoulder guard" — the reflexive bracing that pulls your shoulders up toward your ears when you are concentrating or tense.
  • SI-11 (Tianzong). On the shoulder blade itself, in the soft hollow at the center. Because it is on the back, this point is easiest to reach with a tennis ball against a wall — lean into it and let the ball do the work. This is the point that reaches the deep muscle of the shoulder blade that thumb pressure rarely gets.

Zone 3 — The Distant Switches

This is the part of acupressure that surprises people. Three of the neck's most relied-upon points are nowhere near the neck.

  • SI-3 (Houxi). On the outer edge of your hand, in the crease that forms when you make a loose fist, just behind the knuckle of your little finger. SI-3 is traditionally described as the master point of the Governing Vessel — the channel running up the spine to the base of the skull. In TCM theory, pressing it can influence stiffness anywhere along that line, which is why it features in so many neck protocols.
  • LI-4 (Hegu). In the webbing between your thumb and index finger, at the highest point of the muscle when you squeeze the thumb toward the index finger. LI-4 is the general mover of Qi in the upper body and one of the most widely used points for head and neck tension.
  • BL-60 (Kunlun). In the hollow between the outer ankle bone and the Achilles tendon. This is the Bladder channel's far end — the same Taiyang channel that runs down the back of your neck. In TCM theory it is used when the back of the neck and the occiput ache together.

How to work them: Press with the thumb of the opposite hand, firmly but not to the point of wincing. Hold 30 to 60 seconds each, then release slowly rather than pulling away. These points are convenient because you can work them anywhere — in a meeting, on a train, in a waiting room.

How Do I Know I Have Actually Found the Point?

This is the question that decides whether acupressure does anything for you. Two checks, and both should be true.

Check one: the notch. Classical points are almost always found in a depression — a gap between a muscle and a bone, a tendon and a joint, or two muscle bellies. If your fingertip is resting on the firm belly of a muscle, slide a few millimeters until you fall into the dip. The dip is usually slightly tender when you press it.

Check two: the spread. When you press a point correctly, the sensation often does not stay put. It spreads — a dull ache that travels toward the shoulder, or a heaviness that reaches up over the skull. Think of it as pressing a doorbell that rings in a room down the hall: what you want is a signal on the circuit, not simply a sore spot under your thumb.

If you feel only sharp, local stinging, you are pressing too hard or too close to a nerve. Ease off. In TCM theory the goal is a steady, "grabbing" ache, sometimes described as sour or distended — not pain you have to brace against.

Borrowed pressure for the points you cannot reach: The base of the skull and the shoulder blade are genuinely hard to work on yourself, because your arms get tired before the muscle lets go. Use a wall instead. Place one or two tennis balls in a long sock (the sock keeps them from rolling apart), put them against a wall at the height of the point you want, and lean in slowly. You control how much pressure you take, and it holds far longer than your thumbs can.

Three Neck Patterns and Where to Put the Emphasis

A stiff neck is not one condition in TCM. The same set of points gets used differently depending on which pattern is dominant. Read these three cards and notice which one sounds most like your week.

Pattern 1: Wind-Cold in the Superficial Channels — "The Air-Conditioned Neck"

  • What it feels like: Stiffness that arrives with weather, drafts, air conditioning, or a cold morning, and moves around a little from day to day. The back of the head feels heavy. A hot shower helps noticeably.
  • What TCM theory suggests: The surface layer has been exposed to Cold, which contracts and slows. This is the layer that governs the guy lines.
  • Where to put the emphasis: GB-20 and BL-10 first, worked slowly and gently with warmth — a warm shower or a scarf around the neck before you press. In TCM theory, pressing a cold-contracted layer without warming it is fighting uphill, so keep the neck covered afterwards.

Pattern 2: Liver Qi Stagnation in the Shaoyang — "The Braced Shoulders"

  • What it feels like: Shoulders that live up near your ears, a band of tightness across the top of the shoulders, a tendency to sigh, tightness that tracks with your stress level rather than your weather app. Headaches that settle behind the eyes or at the temples.
  • What TCM theory suggests: The Gallbladder channel runs down the side of the neck, and Liver Qi is the influence that keeps the body's movement smooth. Under sustained stress, the Shaoyang layer braces — the poles stop pivoting.
  • Where to put the emphasis: GB-21 and LI-4, worked firmly, with slow exhales. Because the trigger is often emotional rather than mechanical, these points tend to work best paired with a minute of slow breathing and an honest look at where the bracing started today.

Pattern 3: Deep-Layer Stagnation with Blood Stasis — "The Hinge That Never Gets Greased"

  • What it feels like: Long-standing stiffness that is worse the longer you sit and better once you are thoroughly warm. Rotation is reduced in a specific direction, and one side is stiffer than the other. Rest, sleep, and gentle movement ease it; more screen time tightens it again.
  • What TCM theory suggests: Qi stagnation that has persisted long enough to affect Blood, plus depletion of the deep structural layer anchored by the Governing Vessel. This is the peg layer.
  • Where to put the emphasis: SI-3 and BL-60 first — the distal points that in TCM theory reach the Governing Vessel and the Taiyang line — then SI-11 for the deep shoulder blade. Because this pattern took months or years to build, it responds to frequency rather than intensity: short and often beats long and occasional.

If more than one card sounds like you, start with the one that feels most accurate and add the others only after a week. It is more useful to learn one pattern well than to run all three points badly.

The 7-Minute Neck Reset (For Desk Hours)

This sequence is designed for the middle of a workday, when you have seven minutes and no privacy. It moves from the top of the channel system to the bottom, then finishes with movement — because in TCM theory, points open the channel and movement teaches the tissue to stay open.

  1. Minute 1 — GB-20 and BL-10 (base of skull). Both thumbs, pressing inward and slightly upward, 30 seconds each. Let your jaw go slack. Long exhales.
  2. Minute 2 — GB-21 (shoulder ridge). Use the opposite hand on each side, 30 seconds per shoulder. Press down, then let the shoulder drop beneath your thumb.
  3. Minute 3 — SI-11 (shoulder blade), wall version. If you have a wall and a tennis ball, this is the minute to use it. Lean in for 20 to 30 seconds per side.
  4. Minutes 4 and 5 — SI-3 and LI-4 (hands). One minute each, pressing both hands at once if you like. This is the part you can keep doing later, quietly, anywhere.
  5. Minute 6 — BL-60 (ankles). Reach down, press the hollow between the ankle bone and Achilles tendon on each side for 30 seconds.
  6. Minute 7 — Slow rotation. Turn your head gently left and right, then tilt each ear toward the shoulder. Do not force the end range. Move only as far as feels comfortable, several times, breathing slowly.

How often: In TCM theory, stagnation responds to regularity more than intensity. Two sessions a day — one mid-morning, one late afternoon — tends to be more useful for a desk-driven neck than one long session at night. Where Not to Press: The Neck's Three No-Go Zones

This section matters more than the rest of the article. The neck is the one region of the body where a well-meaning self-massage can cause a genuinely serious problem, because some of its structures do not tolerate pressure.

  • No-go 1: the front and front-sides of the neck. The carotid arteries and the carotid sinus sit here. Pressure over the carotid sinus can slow your heart rate and drop your blood pressure, which can make you lightheaded or faint. Never press the throat or the front-side of the neck, no matter which point you think you are looking for.
  • No-go 2: the hollow just above the collarbone. A dense bundle of nerves and major blood vessels passes through this small space. Pressure here is not a self-care technique.
  • No-go 3: anywhere that reproduces sharp, radiating pain. If pressing a spot sends a sharp or electric sensation down your arm, or makes you feel dizzy or nauseated, stop immediately. That is a signal to have it assessed, not a point to press harder.

Three more precautions worth knowing:

  • Pregnancy: LI-4, GB-21, and BL-60 are traditionally avoided during pregnancy. If you are pregnant, speak with your healthcare provider before using acupressure on these points.
  • Recent injury: In the first 48 hours after a strain, a fall, or a car accident, do not press directly into the injured area. Our guide to whiplash covers why the acute phase is different.
  • Skip broken or irritated skin, and never combine acupressure with forceful twisting or "cracking" of your own neck. Slow pressure is the tool here; high-velocity neck manipulation is not a self-care technique.

What Research Suggests About Acupressure and the Neck

Acupressure research is smaller and less mature than acupuncture research, and the honest summary is that the evidence is promising but not conclusive. A few studies are worth knowing about, because they describe the kind of neck that responds.

  • A systematic review of acupressure for neck pain syndrome. Published in Integrative Medicine Research, this review looked at studies of acupressure for the common non-traumatic neck pain and stiffness that East Asian medicine calls nakchim — a syndrome so familiar in the region that it has its own name. The authors concluded that acupressure may be effective, but that the finding is not conclusive because the included studies were of low methodological quality. GB-20 appeared as a baseline point in most of the protocols reviewed.
  • The Cochrane review of acupuncture for neck disorders. This is the strongest evidence in the neighborhood. Across 27 trials and more than 5,000 participants, reviewers found moderate-quality evidence that acupuncture relieves neck pain better than sham acupuncture at the end of a course of sessions and at short-term follow-up. Notably, the effect did not appear to be sustained over the long term, and reported side effects were minor. Acupressure is not acupuncture, but the two share the same point map and the same rationale, which is why this review is often cited alongside acupressure research.
  • Trigger point acupuncture in office workers. A randomized trial published in the Journal of Occupational Health studied trigger point acupuncture in office workers with chronic neck and shoulder pain and reduced work productivity. It is one of several trials interested specifically in desk-driven neck and shoulder tension — the pattern most readers of this article will recognize.
  • Acupressure as an at-home companion to acupuncture. In a small trial of female office workers with long-standing neck and shoulder pain published in Acupuncture in Medicine, participants received intensive acupuncture over three to four weeks — and acupressure was also given between sessions. The study reported improvements in pain-related interference at work, sleep quality, anxiety, and life satisfaction, with some differences still detectable at follow-up. The sample was small and should be read cautiously, but the design is telling: acupressure was the between-session tool, exactly the way it is used here.
What this means for you: The research does not support the idea that acupressure fixes a neck. What it does support is a modest, low-risk, self-directed practice that some people find reduces how much their neck interferes with their day — particularly when it is done regularly rather than as a one-off, and paired with attention to posture, sleep, and stress. Think of it as one part of a routine, not a standalone answer.

When to See a Doctor

Neck pain is usually mechanical and benign, but some presentations need medical assessment rather than self-care. Please speak with a qualified healthcare provider if any of the following apply to you:

  • Pain that began after a fall, a car accident, or a blow to the head or neck
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness in an arm or hand, or a loss of grip strength
  • Pain that shoots down the arm, or pain accompanied by difficulty walking or problems with balance
  • A severe headache that came on suddenly, or a new headache alongside a stiff neck and fever
  • Neck stiffness with fever, unexplained weight loss, or drenching night sweats
  • Difficulty swallowing, changes in your voice, or a sensation of pressure in the throat
  • Bladder or bowel changes, or numbness around the groin or saddle area
  • Pain that is steadily worsening over weeks, or that wakes you at night
  • Any history of cancer, osteoporosis, or a weakened immune system

If any of these apply, speak with a qualified healthcare provider before using acupressure or any other self-care approach on your neck.

Practical Takeaways

Using Acupressure Wisely for a Stiff Neck:
  • Work the layers, not just the sore spot: GB-20 and BL-10 for the surface, GB-21 and SI-11 for the shoulder ridge that governs rotation, and SI-3, LI-4, and BL-60 for the deep channel line.
  • Use the two-check test: the point should sit in a notch, and pressing it should produce a spreading ache — not a sharp, local sting.
  • Borrow pressure for what you cannot reach: one or two tennis balls in a sock against a wall beat tired thumbs for the base of the skull and shoulder blade.
  • Match your pattern: weather-reactive stiffness responds to warmth plus GB-20 and BL-10; stress-driven bracing responds to GB-21 and LI-4; long-standing one-sided stiffness responds to frequency, not force.
  • Never press the front or front-side of the neck, the hollow above the collarbone, or any spot that sends sharp pain down your arm. Avoid LI-4, GB-21, and BL-60 during pregnancy.
  • Two short sessions beat one long one: consistency is the variable that matters most in TCM theory for long-standing stagnation.
