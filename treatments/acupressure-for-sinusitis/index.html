Acupressure for Sinusitis Relief: Pressure Points That Clear Congestion

Treatment Guide · 8 min read

Beyond the Decongestant Bottle

That dull, heavy pressure behind your eyes. The stuffy nose that makes every breath feel like a negotiation. The headache that settles in like an uninvited house guest. Sinusitis — whether acute or chronic — can turn something as basic as breathing into a constant source of frustration.

Over-the-counter decongestants offer temporary relief, but they often come with side effects: jitteriness, rebound congestion, dry mouth, and the creeping sensation that you need more to get the same effect. Antibiotics only work if bacteria are involved, and many sinus infections are viral or inflammatory in nature.

Acupressure — a cornerstone of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) — offers a different path. It uses gentle finger pressure on specific points along the body's energy channels to encourage the body's own healing mechanisms. No needles. No chemicals. Just your own hands and a few well-placed techniques.

  • When acupressure works best — and when you need a doctor

The TCM View: Why Sinuses Get Stuck

In TCM, the sinuses are closely connected to the Lung system. One of the Lung's core functions is to "open into the nose" — meaning the health of your nasal passages reflects the state of your respiratory system. When Lung Qi is strong and flowing freely, your nasal passages stay clear and moist. When it's disrupted, congestion follows.

Three common TCM patterns contribute to sinus issues:

Wind-Cold Invasion

What it feels like: Clear, watery nasal discharge. Sneezing. Sensitivity to cold air. Aversion to wind. Symptoms appeared after exposure to cold weather or a draft.

TCM logic: External Cold contracts the channels, like a frozen pipe — it narrows the airways and traps fluid. This is the classic "caught a chill" sinus scenario.

Wind-Heat Attack

What it feels like: Thick yellow or green nasal discharge. Sore throat. Thirst. Feverish sensation. Symptoms appeared suddenly, possibly with a fever.

TCM logic: External Heat inflames the nasal passages — like steam pressure building in a closed container. The mucus thickens and the sinus membranes swell.

Lung and Spleen Qi Deficiency (Chronic)

What it feels like: Recurrent sinus infections. Persistent mild congestion that never fully clears. Fatigue. Weak immunity. Frequent colds that settle in the sinuses.

TCM logic: Like a house with a weak security system, deficient Lung and Spleen Qi can't maintain the body's defenses (Wei Qi). The sinuses become a vulnerable entry point that pathogens return to again and again.

The 5 Key Acupressure Points for Sinusitis Relief

These points work on different aspects of sinus congestion — some clear the nasal passages directly, others address the underlying patterns. For best results, work through all five points in sequence.

1. LI20 (Yingxiang — "Welcome Fragrance")

Location: In the nasolabial groove, beside the midpoint of the nostril. You'll find a small depression on each side, directly beside the flare of the nose.

How to press: Use your index fingers to press both sides simultaneously. Apply firm, steady pressure angled slightly upward (toward the bridge of the nose). Hold for 30-60 seconds while breathing slowly. You may feel a spreading sensation through the nasal cavity.

Best for: Immediate nasal congestion relief. This is the primary point for sinus issues in TCM — think of it as the "on/off switch" for stuffy noses.

2. BL2 (Zanzhu — "Bamboo Gathering")

Location: In the depression at the inner end of each eyebrow, right where the eyebrow meets the bridge of the nose.

How to press: Use your thumbs to press both points simultaneously, applying pressure upward into the bony ridge of the eye socket. Use small circular movements for 30-60 seconds.

Best for: Frontal sinus pressure and headache — the heavy, achey sensation above and behind the eyes.

3. GB20 (Fengchi — "Wind Pool")

Location: In the hollows at the base of the skull, between the two large neck muscles (sternocleidomastoid and trapezius). About two finger-widths apart on either side of the spine.

How to press: Hook your thumbs into the hollows at the base of your skull, fingers resting on top of your head. Press upward into the skull base with firm pressure. Hold for 1-2 minutes while breathing deeply. Many people feel a releasing sensation in the head and neck.

Best for: Sinus-related headache, neck tension from sinus pressure, and dispelling external Wind — the TCM concept of environmental factors that trigger sinus symptoms.

4. LI4 (Hegu — "Joining Valley")

Location: On the back of the hand, in the web between the thumb and index finger. Squeeze the thumb and index finger together; the highest point of the muscle mound is the point.

How to press: Pinch the webbing between the thumb and index finger of your opposite hand. Apply firm, deep pressure toward the index finger bone. Hold for 1-2 minutes, then switch hands.

Best for: A powerhouse point for head and face conditions — toothache, headache, sinus pain, and congestion. In TCM, LI4 moves Qi through the face region and relieves pain throughout the head.

Note: Avoid this point during pregnancy, as it is traditionally contraindicated for expectant mothers.

5. ST36 (Zusanli — "Leg Three Miles")

Location: About four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width toward the outside of the shin bone (tibia). The point lies in a depression in the shin muscle.

How to press: Use your thumb to press firmly into the muscle just below and outside the shinbone. You should feel a distinct sensation — not sharp pain, but a deep ache or heaviness. Hold for 1-2 minutes per leg.

Best for: Strengthening overall immunity and energy. While not a direct sinus point, ST36 builds Wei Qi (defensive energy) and is a key point for preventing recurrent sinus infections.

Step-by-Step Acupressure Routine (5 Minutes)

For acute sinus congestion, follow this sequence once or twice daily:

  1. Warm up your hands by rubbing them together for 10 seconds. Gentle warmth in your fingers makes the pressure more comfortable.
  2. BL2 — Inner brow points. 30-60 seconds of circular pressure.
  3. LI20 — Beside nostrils. 30-60 seconds of upward-angled pressure.
  4. GB20 — Base of skull. 1-2 minutes of sustained pressure.
  5. LI4 — Hand web. 1-2 minutes per hand.
  6. ST36 — Below knee. 1-2 minutes per leg.
  7. Finish by gently pressing your palms over your eyes and sinuses for 30 seconds — a self-soothing gesture that consolidates the effect.

Pro Tips for Best Results

  • Consistency matters: For chronic sinus issues, practice this routine twice daily for at least 2 weeks. Acute flare-ups may respond in a single session.
  • Breathe through it: Take slow, deep breaths while applying pressure. The breathing itself helps open the nasal passages.
  • Warm compress synergy: Applying a warm, damp washcloth over the sinus area for 5 minutes before acupressure can enhance results.
  • Stay hydrated: Drink warm water after your acupressure session to support the movement of Qi and fluids.
  • Gentle is better: You should feel pressure, not sharp pain. If a point is very tender, use lighter pressure and hold longer.

TCM Adjuncts That Support Sinus Health

While acupressure is the focus of this guide, TCM offers several complementary approaches for sinusitis management:

  • Steam inhalation with herbs: Adding a few drops of peppermint or eucalyptus essential oil to hot water and inhaling the steam helps open congested passages. In TCM theory, the aromatic, dispersing nature of these herbs moves Stagnation in the Lung channels.
  • Nasal irrigation: A saline rinse (neti pot) is widely used in both Western and Eastern traditions. In TCM, it's considered a way to clear external pathogens from the nasal passages before they penetrate deeper.
  • Dietary adjustments: TCM recommends avoiding dairy, cold foods, and sugar during a sinus flare-up, as these are believed to create Dampness and Phlegm. Warm, cooked foods with ginger, garlic, and scallions support the body's ability to expel pathogens.
  • Moxibustion at ST36: For chronic or recurrent sinusitis, warming ST36 with moxa (burning mugwort) can strengthen the body's defensive Qi. This is ideally done under the guidance of a TCM practitioner.

What the Research Says

While acupressure research for sinusitis specifically is still emerging, several lines of evidence support the approach:

  • Acupuncture for chronic sinusitis: A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis (BMJ Open, 2019) of 12 RCTs involving 1,162 participants found that acupuncture was associated with improvements in nasal congestion, discharge, and quality of life in chronic sinusitis patients compared to conventional treatment alone.
  • LI4 and GB20 for head and face pain: A 2017 neuroimaging study (Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine) found that stimulating LI4 activates brain regions associated with pain modulation and parasympathetic activity, which helps explain its broad application for head and face discomfort.
  • ST36 for immune function: A 2018 review (Frontiers in Immunology) examined how ST36 stimulation influences immune cell activity and inflammatory markers, suggesting potential mechanisms for reducing recurrent sinus infections.
  • Facial acupressure for allergic rhinitis: A small 2020 pilot study (Complementary Therapies in Medicine) found that daily facial acupressure reduced nasal symptoms in allergic rhinitis patients, supporting the approach for congestion-related conditions.

When to See a Doctor

Acupressure is a supportive technique, not a replacement for medical care. Seek medical attention if:

  • Your symptoms last more than 10 days without improvement
  • You have a high fever (above 101°F / 38.5°C)
  • You experience severe headache or facial pain
  • You notice swelling or redness around your eyes
  • Your vision changes or becomes blurry
  • You have a history of recurrent sinusitis that doesn't respond to home care
  • You experience neck stiffness or confusion (possible signs of a more serious infection)

Practical Takeaways

  • Start with LI20 and BL2 for immediate congestion relief — these are the most direct sinus points on the face
  • Add GB20 and LI4 for sinus-related headache and pressure
  • Use ST36 daily for long-term sinus health and immune support
  • Practice consistently: Twice daily for 2+ weeks for chronic cases
  • Combine with warm compresses and steam for enhanced effects
  • See a doctor for persistent symptoms, high fever, or vision changes
  • For personalized care, consult a licensed acupuncturist or TCM practitioner who can tailor the point selection to your specific pattern
