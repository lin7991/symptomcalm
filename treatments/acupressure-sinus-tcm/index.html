Acupressure for Sinus Relief: Clear Congestion with Pressure Points

Treatment Guide · 7 min read

That familiar pressure behind your eyes. The dull ache across your cheekbones. The stuffiness that makes every breath feel like you're breathing through a straw. And the frustration of reaching for decongestants that work for a few hours before the pressure builds right back up again.

Sinus congestion is one of those complaints that isn't dangerous — but it can make you feel absolutely miserable. Your face feels heavy. Your head feels clouded. Your energy drops. And if it's chronic, the cycle of congestion, medication, and rebound congestion can feel endless.

But your body already has a built-in system for addressing sinus congestion — you just need to know which buttons to press. Acupressure offers a targeted, side-effect-free way to encourage drainage, reduce pressure, and support the underlying systems that keep your sinuses clear.

Think of acupressure points as shortcut keys for your body's natural drainage system. Instead of reaching for an external tool (a nasal spray, a decongestant pill), you're activating your own internal plumbing — encouraging the sinuses to drain, the tissues to relax, and the congestion to release using nothing but your fingertips.

How TCM Understands Sinus Congestion

From a TCM perspective, sinus congestion is rarely just a local problem. The sinuses sit at the intersection of several body systems, and chronic or recurrent congestion usually points to an underlying imbalance in one (or more) of them.

The Lung-Spleen Connection

In TCM theory, the Lungs govern the skin and body surface — including the mucous membranes of the nose and sinuses. The Lungs are considered the "delicate organ" because they're the first to be affected by external factors like cold air, wind, and dry environments. When the Lung system is strong, the nasal passages stay moist, clean, and open. When it's compromised, the nose and sinuses become vulnerable.

The Spleen, meanwhile, is responsible for transforming fluids in the body. In TCM theory, when the Spleen is functioning well, fluids are properly metabolized and moved. When it's weak, fluids can accumulate and turn into Dampness or Phlegm — the TCM equivalents of the thick, sticky mucus that packs your sinuses and refuses to drain.

Think of it like a house with two vulnerabilities: the windows (Lungs) are letting in drafts, and the plumbing (Spleen) is backing up. Fixing one without the other may offer temporary relief, but the pattern will likely return.

Three Common Sinus Patterns

Pattern 1: Wind-Cold Invasion (The Acute Stuffy Nose)

What it feels like: Clear or white mucus, sneezing, chills, sensitivity to cold air. The congestion came on suddenly — often after being in cold or windy weather. You may feel a mild headache at the back of your head or between your eyebrows.

TCM logic: External Cold has entered through the body's surface (the Lung's domain), causing the nasal passages to constrict and fluids to congeal. Like pipes freezing in winter — the fluid (mucus) is there but can't move because the environment has turned it stiff.

Best acupressure approach: Warming points that expel Cold and open the nasal passages. LI20 and BL2 are your first-line points here, paired with GB20 to release the exterior.

Pattern 2: Damp-Heat in the Sinuses (Thick, Stubborn Congestion)

What it feels like: Thick, yellow or green mucus. Deep pressure in the cheekbones and forehead. A sensation of fullness that doesn't shift with blowing your nose. You may also feel heavy, sluggish, or foggy-headed.

TCM logic: This is the classic Phlegm-Dampness pattern. The Spleen isn't transforming fluids properly, and the accumulated Dampness has become "cooked" by internal Heat, creating thick, sticky mucus that's hard to move. Like a humid, warm environment where everything feels sticky and nothing dries out.

Best acupressure approach: Points that transform Dampness and clear Heat. LI4 becomes especially important here, along with ST36 (Zusanli) to strengthen the Spleen's fluid-processing function from below.

Pattern 3: Lung Qi Deficiency (Chronic or Recurrent Sinus Trouble)

What it feels like: You catch every cold that goes around. Your sinuses are chronically mildly congested — never completely blocked, never completely clear. You may have a weak voice, low energy, and find yourself susceptible to seasonal allergies.

TCM logic: The Lung system's defensive function (Wei Qi) is weak. Like a security system with a dying battery — it still works but triggers false alarms (allergic reactions) and lets intruders through (infections). The sinuses are the canary in the coal mine for a deeper immune vulnerability.

Best acupressure approach: Strengthening points that build Lung Qi and Wei Qi. LU7 (Lieque) on the wrist and ST36 are key, combined with LI20 for local sinus drainage.

The Top Acupressure Points for Sinus Relief

These are the most effective points for addressing sinus congestion. Each serves a different function — some work locally on the sinuses, others address the underlying imbalances that create chronic congestion.

1. LI20 (Yingxiang) — The Sinus Drainage Point

Location: In the groove on either side of your nostrils, right where the nose meets the cheek. If you slide your finger from the corner of your nostril outward about half an inch, you'll feel a small depression.

Why it works for sinuses: LI20 is the most direct point for sinus and nasal congestion in the entire acupressure system. The Large Intestine meridian connects to the nose and face, making LI20 the "local exit" for sinus pressure. In TCM theory, stimulating this point helps the Qi in the sinus channels to move, encouraging mucus drainage and reducing facial pressure.

How to apply: Use your index fingers to press firmly into the grooves on both sides of your nostrils. Hold for 30–60 seconds while breathing slowly through your mouth. You may feel the pressure shift or a sensation of "opening" in your nasal passages. Repeat 2–3 times throughout the day.

2. BL2 (Cuanzhu) — The Forehead & Eye Pressure Release

Location: In the small indent at the inner edge of each eyebrow, right where the eyebrow meets the bridge of your nose.

Why it works for sinuses: BL2 sits at the top of the sinus cavity. The Bladder meridian runs along the back of the head and over the top of the skull, and BL2 is one of its first points on the face. It's particularly effective for the forehead pressure and pain behind the eyes that often accompany sinus congestion.

How to apply: Use your thumbs or middle fingers to press the inner corners of your eyebrows. Apply gentle to moderate pressure in an upward direction — as if you're trying to lift the bone slightly. Hold for 30 seconds. Many people find this point brings almost immediate relief for frontal sinus pressure.

3. LI4 (Hegu) — The Master Pain & Facial Point

Location: In the fleshy webbing between your thumb and index finger. Bring your thumb and index finger together — the highest point of the muscle mound is LI4.

Why it works for sinuses: LI4 is one of the most versatile and powerful points in the entire acupressure system — traditionally called a "command point" for the face and head. In TCM theory, the Large Intestine meridian ascends to the face, and LI4 regulates Qi flow throughout this entire pathway. It's effective for sinus headaches, facial pain, nasal congestion, and general inflammation in the head region. Some studies suggest that LI4 stimulation may influence pain perception and immune function through the body's endogenous opioid system.

How to apply: Press the fleshy web firmly with your opposite thumb and index finger, squeezing from both sides. Hold for 30–60 seconds. You may feel a dull ache or a radiating sensation. Alternate hands. ⚠️ Avoid during pregnancy — LI4 may stimulate uterine contractions.

4. GB20 (Fengchi) — The External Wind Release

Location: In the hollows at the base of your skull, between the two large vertical neck muscles. Run your fingers up from the back of your neck until you feel the skull bone, then slide outward into the natural depressions.

Why it works for sinuses: GB20 is the key point for releasing "Wind" from the body's exterior — the TCM term for external factors like cold drafts, allergens, and weather changes that trigger sinus congestion. In TCM theory, Wind carries other pathogens (Cold, Heat, Dampness) into the body through the neck and upper back. Releasing GB20 is like closing the door through which sinus-triggering factors enter. It's also one of the best points for the tension headaches that often accompany sinus pressure.

How to apply: Use both thumbs simultaneously to press upward and inward into the hollows at the base of your skull. Apply steady pressure for 1–2 minutes while tilting your head back slightly into your thumbs. Deep breathing enhances the effect.

5. ST36 (Zusanli) — The Chronic Congestion Foundation Point

Location: About four finger-widths below your kneecap, one finger-width outward from the shinbone. Find the tibial ridge and slide your finger to the outside of it — you'll feel a muscle belly.

Why it works for sinuses: ST36 may seem far from your sinuses, but it's one of the most important points for chronic or recurrent sinus congestion. The Stomach meridian, in TCM theory, is closely linked to the Spleen's fluid-transformation function. ST36 strengthens the digestive system's ability to manage fluids — addressing the Phlegm-Dampness root of chronic sinus congestion at its source. If your sinus problems are recurrent rather than acute, ST36 is arguably more important than any facial point.

How to apply: Press firmly with your thumb into the muscle belly below your knee. Hold for 1–2 minutes per leg. This point can feel tender if your Spleen/Stomach system is out of balance — the tenderness often decreases with regular practice.

6. Yintang (EX-HN3) — The Third Eye Pressure Release

Location: In the center of your forehead, midway between your eyebrows — the spot often called the "third eye."

Why it works for sinuses: Yintang sits directly above the ethmoid sinuses and is an excellent point for deep forehead congestion and the headache that comes with it. In TCM theory, Yintang helps calm the Shen (mind) and descend rebellious Qi — addressing both the physical pressure and the mental irritability that sinus congestion often creates.

How to apply: Use your middle finger to apply gentle, steady pressure directly between your eyebrows. Hold for 1 minute. You can also use small circular motions. Many people find this point deeply relaxing, making it a good choice when sinus pressure is making it hard to sleep.

Acupressure Protocols by Sinus Pattern

Matching your acupressure sequence to your sinus pattern gives you the best results. Here are three protocols, each designed for a different TCM sinus presentation:

Protocol A: Acute Cold-Onset Congestion (Wind-Cold)

Use this when your sinuses suddenly stuffed up after exposure to cold weather or a draft, and you have clear or white mucus.

  1. GB20 (base of skull) — 1–2 minutes. Release the exterior and expel the Cold.
  2. LI20 (beside nostrils) — 1 minute. Open the nasal passages locally.
  3. BL2 (inner eyebrows) — 30 seconds. Clear the frontal sinuses.
  4. LI4 (hand web) — 1 minute per hand. General face-and-head activation.

Repeat this sequence every 3–4 hours during the acute phase. Adding a warm compress to your face between sessions can enhance the effect.

Protocol B: Thick Mucus & Deep Congestion (Damp-Heat)

Use this when your mucus is thick, yellow or green, and your face feels heavy and full.

  1. ST36 (below knee) — 2 minutes per leg. Strengthen the Spleen's fluid-transformation capacity from the foundation.
  2. LI4 (hand web) — 1 minute per hand. Clear Heat and move stagnation in the face.
  3. LI20 (beside nostrils) — 1 minute. Local sinus drainage.
  4. Yintang (between eyebrows) — 1 minute. Deep forehead pressure release.

This pattern tends to be stubborn — apply the sequence twice daily for 3–5 days rather than expecting instant results. A 2017 study in Complementary Therapies in Medicine found that acupoint stimulation in patients with chronic sinusitis was associated with improved symptom scores over 4 weeks of consistent practice.

Protocol C: Chronic, Recurrent Congestion (Lung Qi Deficiency)

Use this as a maintenance protocol if you're someone who gets sinus trouble repeatedly — every cold settles in your sinuses, seasonal changes trigger congestion, or your nose is never quite clear.

  1. ST36 (below knee) — 2 minutes per leg. Build overall Qi and strengthen the Lung-Spleen axis.
  2. LU7 (above wrist) — 1 minute per arm. To find it: on the thumb side of your forearm, about 1.5 inches above the wrist crease, in the small hollow between the radius bone and the muscle. This point specifically strengthens Lung Qi and opens the nasal passages in TCM theory.
  3. LI20 (beside nostrils) — 30 seconds. Brief local activation.
  4. GB20 (base of skull) — 1 minute. Strengthen the body's exterior resistance.

Practice this sequence once daily as a preventive measure. In TCM logic, consistency is more important than intensity — 5 minutes daily for several weeks can meaningfully shift the underlying pattern.

A note on combining approaches: Acupressure works well alongside steam inhalation, warm compresses, and adequate hydration. In TCM dietary theory, avoiding dairy, cold drinks, and greasy foods during an acute sinus episode may help reduce mucus production — these foods are considered Dampness-producing in TCM nutritional thinking. Research and What Studies Suggest

While research specifically on acupressure for sinus conditions is still developing, several lines of evidence support the approach:

  • Acupoint stimulation for sinusitis: A 2017 study in Complementary Therapies in Medicine found that acupoint stimulation (including acupressure) was associated with improved symptom scores in patients with chronic sinusitis over a 4-week period. Participants reported reduced nasal congestion, improved drainage, and less facial pain.
  • LI4 and facial pain: Research has shown that LI4 stimulation activates brain regions involved in pain modulation and parasympathetic function (including the insula and anterior cingulate cortex). This helps explain why a point on the hand can influence facial congestion and headache.
  • GB20 and autonomic regulation: A 2017 study in Autonomic Neuroscience found that pressure on points in the neck and base-of-skull region can shift the nervous system from sympathetic to parasympathetic dominance — reducing the stress response that often exacerbates sinus congestion and immune vulnerability.
  • ST36 and immune function: ST36 has been studied extensively for its immunomodulatory effects. Research suggests that stimulation of ST36 may influence cytokine production and local immune activity — relevant to the recurrent-infection pattern of chronic sinus congestion.
  • Acupuncture for chronic rhinosinusitis: A 2020 systematic review in BMJ Open found that acupuncture showed promise in reducing symptom severity in chronic rhinosinusitis patients. While acupuncture and acupressure use different stimulation methods, they share the same theoretical basis and point selection principles.

These findings are encouraging but preliminary in many areas. Acupressure is best used as a complementary self-care tool — not a replacement for medical care when needed.

When to See a Doctor vs. When to Use Acupressure

Acupressure is most effective for common sinus congestion — the everyday stuffiness that comes with colds, weather changes, or mild seasonal allergies. But sinus symptoms can sometimes indicate a more serious condition that requires medical attention.

Acupressure is a good choice when:

  • You have mild to moderate sinus congestion without fever
  • Your symptoms began after a cold or weather change
  • You have a known pattern of seasonal allergies
  • You want to reduce your reliance on decongestant medications

⚠️ When to See a Doctor Instead

Acupressure may support sinus comfort, but it is not appropriate for serious or undiagnosed sinus conditions. Seek medical attention if you experience:

  • Fever over 100.4°F (38°C) with sinus pain — this may indicate a bacterial sinus infection requiring antibiotics
  • Symptoms lasting more than 10 days without improvement
  • Severe headache that doesn't respond to acupressure or over-the-counter pain relief
  • Swelling or redness around the eyes or forehead
  • Vision changes, double vision, or bulging eyes
  • Nasal congestion that is only on one side (unilateral) for an extended period
  • Nosebleeds that are frequent or difficult to stop

If you are pregnant, have high blood pressure, or have a chronic medical condition, talk to your healthcare provider before using acupressure routinely.

Practical Takeaways

Your Daily Sinus Acupressure Routine

  1. Identify your pattern — Is this acute (come on fast, triggered by cold or weather change) or chronic (recurrent, lingers)? Choose Protocol A, B, or C above.
  2. Find your starting point — For acute congestion, start at the neck (GB20) and work toward the face. For chronic congestion, start at the legs (ST36) and work upward.
  3. Apply steady pressure — Not painful, but firm enough that you feel a dull ache or sensation. Each point should feel like a "good hurt" — deep but tolerable.
  4. Breathe through it — Inhale for 4 counts, exhale for 6 counts. Each exhale, sink a little deeper into the pressure.
  5. Hold each point 30 seconds to 2 minutes — Facial points (LI20, BL2, Yintang) need less time. Leg points (ST36) benefit from longer holds.
  6. Use consistently — For acute congestion, every 3–4 hours. For chronic prevention, once daily.

Your sinuses are one of your body's first lines of defense — the place where the internal meets the external. When they're congested, your entire system feels the impact. Acupressure won't solve every sinus problem, and it shouldn't replace medical care when you need it. But as a tool for managing the common congestion that colds, weather, and seasonal changes bring, it's effective, zero-cost, and always available — right at your fingertips.
