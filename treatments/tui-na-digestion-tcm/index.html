Tui Na for Digestion: Self-Massage for Your Spleen and Stomach

Treatment Guide · 7 min read

You eat a normal dinner — nothing crazy, nothing spicy, a perfectly reasonable portion. And for the next three hours, you feel like you swallowed a brick. Your jeans are suddenly too tight, there's a low-grade gurgle in your gut, and the energy that should come from food seems to have gone somewhere else entirely.

You've tried the usual suspects: probiotics, more fiber, less dairy, eating slower, chewing more. Some of it helps around the edges, but the pattern keeps coming back — the heaviness, the bloating, the sluggish "food just sits there" feeling that turns every meal into a gamble.

Here's a tool you probably haven't tried, and it costs nothing, needs no equipment, and you can do it in your pajamas: your own two hands.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), there's a complete bodywork system built around exactly this idea. Tui Na (pronounced "twee-nah") is TCM's hands-on massage therapy — a family of techniques using kneading, pressing, pushing, and circular rubbing to keep Qi (your body's operating system) and Blood moving smoothly through the channels. While most people know Tui Na for stiff necks and sore backs, one of its oldest and most practical applications is digestion.

Think of it as giving your gut a manual nudge. Western medicine has pills that quiet symptoms; TCM has hands that work with the body's own mechanics. Abdominal Tui Na applies gentle, deliberate pressure and movement to the belly — helping the digestive tract do what it's supposed to do: move things down and through, at the right pace.

This guide covers how self-Tui Na is traditionally used for digestive support, the four core moves you can do at home, the patterns it may suit, and what modern research says about abdominal massage. If you're new to Tui Na itself, our Self-Tui Na Home Guide covers the fundamentals. And if you're weighing your options, our acupressure guide for digestion (sustained finger pressure on single points) and cupping guide for digestion (suction) cover the sister techniques — this article focuses on the hands-on massage side.

Why Tui Na for Digestion? The Hands-On Difference

To see why massage could matter for your gut, it helps to know how TCM thinks about digestion. It's not a single organ doing one job — it's a coordinated system with a clear division of labor:

  • The Stomach is the receiving bay and first processor — it "ripens and ripens" food, breaking it down. Its Qi is supposed to move downward, like a gravity-fed conveyor belt.
  • The Spleen (a TCM concept, not the Western organ) is the power plant — it transforms food into Qi and Blood and transports those resources upward and outward to the whole body.
  • The Liver is the traffic controller — it keeps Qi moving smoothly everywhere, including through the digestive tract. When the Liver gets jammed up by stress, the Stomach and Spleen feel it first.
Like a garden hose with a persistent kink. When Qi gets stuck — from stress, irregular meals, or an overworked Spleen — digestion slows to a trickle. Food sits in the gut longer than it should, gas builds up, and you feel bloated, heavy, and foggy. The hose isn't broken; it just needs the kink released. That's where hands come in.

What makes Tui Na different from the other TCM surface therapies is scope. Acupressure works one point at a time. Cupping applies suction to a handful of spots. Tui Na uses your whole palm and fingers to work the entire belly — the organ itself, its surrounding channels, and the muscles of the abdominal wall. In TCM terms, it moves Qi and Blood through the Stomach and Spleen channels directly; in modern terms, it's manual stimulation of the abdomen that most people find deeply relaxing.

The Core Moves: Four Self-Tui Na Techniques for Your Belly

You don't need a degree in TCM to do this safely at home. These are the four foundational Tui Na techniques, adapted for the abdomen. Start gently — the goal is comfort, not force.

Mo (摩) — Circular Rubbing: The Warm-Up

How to do it: Place your palm flat on your belly, centered on or just below the navel. Rub in slow, full circles — palm gliding over the skin or over a thin layer of clothing — about 1-2 seconds per circle. Traditionally, you rub clockwise (following the path food takes through the large intestine: up the right side, across the top, down the left).

What it's for: Mo is the gentle "wake-up" move. In TCM theory it warms the belly and gets Qi moving in the digestive tract without forcing anything. It's traditionally used for sluggish digestion, mild bloating, and general gut comfort. Think of it as stirring a slow-cooking pot — you're encouraging movement, not shoving it.

Rou (揉) — Kneading: The Deepen

How to do it: With your palm or the pads of your fingers, press gently into the belly and move in small, slow circles without sliding over the skin — the skin moves with your hand, and the pressure works the deeper tissue. One hand can rest on top of the other for support. Spend 30-60 seconds around the navel and along the midline.

What it's for: Rou reaches deeper than Mo. In TCM terms it's used to "massage the Spleen and harmonize the Stomach" — helping tense, tight abdominal tissue soften and supporting the Stomach's descending function. If you feel a tender spot, ease off; tenderness is a signal, not a target.

An (按) — Pressing: The Point Work

How to do it: Using your fingertip or thumb pad, press slowly into a specific point, hold for 30-60 seconds, then release gradually. Breathe out as you press deeper. The classic digestive points include:

  • CV-12 (Zhongwan) — midway between the navel and the base of the breastbone. Traditionally the "command point" for the Stomach.
  • ST-25 (Tianshu) — about two finger-widths to each side of the navel. The Stomach's "sky pivot," traditionally used for bloating and bowel regularity.
  • CV-6 (Qihai) — about two finger-widths below the navel. Traditionally used to support Qi when digestion feels weak and sluggish.

What it's for: An applies sustained, targeted pressure — the bridge between whole-belly massage and acupressure. It's traditionally used when digestion feels stuck: food sitting heavy, bloating that won't shift, or a belly that feels tense and guarded.

Tui (推) — Pushing: The Finisher

How to do it: With a flat palm, push in a single direction along the abdomen — most commonly from the breastbone down toward the navel (following the Stomach's descending motion), or from the sides toward the center. Repeat 10-15 times, slowly.

What it's for: Tui gives the digestive system a clear directional signal. In TCM theory, the Stomach's Qi should descend — Tui literally assists that motion. It's a good closing move after Mo and Rou, like sweeping the workbench after you've finished.

Three Digestive Patterns and the Moves That Match Them

In TCM, the same symptom can have different underlying patterns — and the technique gets tailored to the pattern. Here are the three most common digestive presentations and how self-Tui Na may be adjusted for each.

Pattern 1: Spleen Qi Deficiency (The Sluggish Power Plant)

What it feels like: Bloating after meals — even small ones — plus fatigue, soft or loose stools, brain fog, and heavy limbs. Food goes in, but the energy conversion feels inefficient: a laptop plugged in but not charging.

The Tui Na approach: Emphasize Mo (slow clockwise circles) and gentle An on CV-6, the Qi-supporting point. The aim is warmth and steady encouragement rather than vigorous pressure. Five to ten minutes of Mo before or after dinner, most evenings, is the classic self-care rhythm.

Pattern 2: Food Stagnation (The Overloaded Fridge)

What it feels like: A stuffed, uncomfortable fullness after rich or heavy meals — the food feels like it's just sitting there. Burping, sour regurgitation, poor appetite afterward.

The Tui Na approach: Use Rou around the navel and An on ST-25 and CV-12 to help the Stomach's descending function — moving the "stuck" contents along. Finish with Tui downward from breastbone to navel. This is the pattern where the moves feel most purposeful, like helping a jammed conveyor belt restart.

Pattern 3: Liver Qi Stagnation Affecting the Spleen (The Stressed Gut)

What it feels like: Bloating and gut discomfort that flare when you're stressed, anxious, or frustrated — "butterflies" turned up to eleven. Often with sighing, a tight chest, irritability, and symptoms that shift with your mood. Stress jams the traffic controller, and the power plant pays the price.

The Tui Na approach: Slow, breath-synchronized Mo (clockwise), with the exhale lengthened — the massage doubles as a nervous-system reset. Many people find pressing LV-3 (Taichong), on top of the foot between the first and second toes, alongside the belly work helps release the Liver's grip. A 7-Minute Daily Routine You Can Do Tonight

Here's a complete self-Tui Na sequence for digestion — about seven minutes, best done 1-2 hours after a meal (not immediately after eating) or in the evening. Choose the pattern moves that fit your symptoms; the skeleton stays the same.

  1. Warm up with Mo (2 min). Sit or lie down with knees bent. Palm flat on the belly, rub slow clockwise circles from the navel outward. Breathe normally. You're looking for warmth and softening, not pressure.
  2. Knead around the navel with Rou (2 min). Small, slow circles with the palm or fingertips, 30-60 seconds per side of the navel, plus the midline between navel and breastbone. Ease off on any tender spots.
  3. Press your key points with An (2 min). Choose 1-2 points from your pattern: CV-12 for Stomach heaviness, ST-25 (both sides) for bloating and regularity, CV-6 for sluggish, weak digestion. Press slowly with the breath — exhale in, hold, release.
  4. Finish with Tui (1 min). Flat palm, push downward from the base of the breastbone to the navel, 10-15 slow passes. Then rest a hand on your belly, breathe deeply for 30 seconds, and notice what settled.
Consistency beats intensity. Five gentle minutes most days will do more for a sluggish gut than one aggressive session a week. The abdominal area is soft and sensitive — if a move hurts, it's too strong. Back off, slow down, or skip that move.

What the Research Says About Abdominal Massage and Digestion

Abdominal massage — the broad category Tui Na belongs to — has been studied more than most people realize, though the evidence base is still developing. Here's what the research landscape looks like:

  • Abdominal massage and constipation. A 2016 Cochrane review of abdominal massage for constipation (12 randomized trials) concluded that some studies suggest it may improve constipation symptoms and quality of life, while noting the trials were small and varied in quality. Individual studies have reported increased bowel-movement frequency with daily abdominal massage, particularly in people with chronic or age-related constipation.
  • Abdominal massage and IBS. Several small trials have explored massage-based approaches for irritable bowel syndrome, with some studies suggesting improvements in bloating and discomfort when massage was added to routine care. As with most complementary approaches, larger and better-controlled trials are needed.
  • Tui Na specifically for functional dyspepsia. A number of randomized trials conducted mainly in China have examined Tui Na for functional dyspepsia — the "stuck, full, uncomfortable after meals" pattern. Some studies suggest that combining Tui Na with routine care was associated with greater symptom improvement than routine care alone, though study quality varied and most were short-term.
  • The relaxation mechanism. Slow, rhythmic abdominal massage is consistently associated with subjective relaxation, and some research links gentle massage to increased parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) activity — the nervous-system state the gut needs to do its work. This aligns with the TCM view that the Spleen and Stomach operate best when the system is calm.

The takeaway from the research is honest but encouraging: abdominal massage is low-risk, inexpensive, and has preliminary evidence behind it, especially for constipation and meal-related fullness. It's traditionally used as one piece of a broader digestive plan — warm, cooked foods; regular meal times; eating without rushing; and managing stress — not as a standalone fix. Safety First: When Abdominal Self-Massage Isn't Right

Tui Na for digestion is gentle, but the abdomen is not a place to improvise. Skip self-massage and consult a healthcare provider in these situations:

  • Pregnancy. Avoid deep abdominal massage during pregnancy. Gentle surface rubbing is usually fine, but check with your midwife or doctor first — and never press firmly on the abdomen.
  • Sudden or severe abdominal pain. If pain comes on quickly or is intense, massage is not appropriate — this needs medical evaluation, not hands-on therapy.
  • Recent abdominal surgery. Wait for full clearance from your surgeon before any abdominal massage, and even then, start very gently.
  • Hernias. If you have an abdominal or inguinal hernia, avoid pressing on or around the area.
  • Active digestive conditions. If you have inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's, ulcerative colitis) in an active flare, unexplained bleeding, or a diagnosed condition your doctor hasn't cleared for massage, get medical advice first.
  • Immediately after large meals. Wait at least 1-2 hours after eating before doing the routine — pressing a full stomach is uncomfortable and, in TCM terms, interferes with the very process you're trying to support.
⚠️ When to See a Doctor: Tui Na is not a substitute for medical evaluation. Consult a healthcare provider if you experience any of the following: sudden or severe abdominal pain, fever with digestive symptoms, blood in your stool, unexplained weight loss, persistent vomiting, difficulty swallowing, or jaundice (yellowing of the skin or eyes). These may indicate conditions that require medical diagnosis and care. Use self-massage as a complementary practice within a broader healthcare plan — not instead of one.

Practical Takeaways

If you want to try self-Tui Na for digestion, here's the short version:

  • Learn four moves. Mo (circular rubbing) warms and wakes; Rou (kneading) deepens and softens; An (pressing) targets points like CV-12, ST-25, and CV-6; Tui (pushing) finishes with downward direction.
  • Match the moves to your pattern. Sluggish and tired after meals → emphasize Mo and CV-6. Stuffed after rich meals → emphasize Rou and ST-25 with Tui downward. Stress-triggered bloating → slow breath-synchronized Mo, plus LV-3.
  • Do it daily, gently. Seven minutes, 1-2 hours after a meal or in the evening. Gentle and consistent beats intense and occasional.
  • Pair it with the basics. Warm, cooked foods; regular meal times; eating without rushing; and stress management. The massage works with these, not instead of them.
  • Know when to stop. Pain, tenderness that doesn't ease, pregnancy, recent surgery, or undiagnosed abdominal symptoms — skip the massage and talk to a healthcare provider.
