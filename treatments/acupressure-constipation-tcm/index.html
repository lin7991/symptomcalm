Acupressure for Constipation: 7 Pressure Points for a Sluggish Bowel

Treatment Guide · 9 min read

You have done everything the internet told you to do. More water. More fiber. Psyllium in your smoothie, magnesium at night, a stool softener you would rather not make a habit of. And still — nothing. You sit down, you wait, you strain, and eventually you get up feeling like the job is only half finished.

Maybe it is not every day. Maybe it is three days a week, and the other four are a gamble. But the mental load is constant: when did I last go? Should I be worried? Why does something so basic feel so difficult?

Here is a tool you probably have not tried, and it costs nothing, needs no equipment, and takes about ten minutes: your own fingers, on seven specific spots.

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), acupressure is the needle-free form of acupuncture — sustained finger pressure on the same points, done at home, on yourself. It has been used for bowel sluggishness for centuries, and it works on a completely different logic than fiber does.

Think of your bowel as a lawn irrigation system. Water has to be in the tank (moisture). The pipes have to be clear (flow). And the pump has to have power (push). If any one of those three fails, the lawn browns — but the fix is different in each case. Pouring in more water does nothing when the problem is the pump. That is the core insight TCM brings to constipation: it does not ask only how fast does it move? It asks which part of the system is failing?

This article focuses specifically on acupressure for constipation: the seven points traditionally used for bowel regularity, how to tell which ones fit your type of stuck, and a ten-minute routine you can run in the evening. If you want the full clinical picture of the TCM patterns behind constipation — including the herbal formulas traditionally used for each — our companion guide to constipation from a TCM perspective covers that ground in depth. And if you are new to acupressure itself, the beginner's guide to acupressure explains the basics of how to press correctly.

One note before we start: this is educational content, not a diagnosis. Constipation has many causes, some of them requiring medical attention — we cover the red flags carefully further down.

A 60-Second Self-Check: Which Issue Are You Describing?

Before learning the points, figure out where you sit. The language people use to describe their constipation is remarkably consistent — and it points straight at the underlying mechanism. Read the five descriptions below and see which one sounds most like you. Most people recognize one or two, and many are a blend.

What you would say What is missing TCM pattern
"My stools are small, hard, dry pellets and I strain to pass them." Moisture — the stool is too dry to slide Intestinal Dryness-Heat
"I feel bloated and full of pressure. I have the urge, but nothing comes out. It is worse when I am stressed." Flow — the route is congested Qi Stagnation (often with stress)
"I have the urge, but I do not have the strength to push. I am wiped out afterward." Power — not enough propulsive force Qi Deficiency
"My abdomen feels cold and sluggish. Warmth helps. I feel tired and low." Power and warmth — the engine is running cold Yang Deficiency / Cold in the Intestines
"My stool is dry AND I get no urge at all. I am not even aware I need to go." Moisture and signal — the call never arrives Blood and Yin Deficiency

Notice how different those five experiences are. Someone with the first pattern does not need more pushing — they need lubrication. Someone with the third has the opposite problem: plenty of moisture, no force. Someone with the second has both moisture and force, and still cannot go, because the pathway itself is congested.

Write your answer down. You will use it to choose your points in a moment. And do not worry if you relate to two or three — TCM patterns commonly overlap, and the routine at the end of this article covers the shared foundation that suits nearly everyone.

The Constipation Acupressure Toolbox: 7 Points to Know

There are more than 360 classical acupoints. What matters for constipation is that a small handful have been used for bowel sluggishness for centuries, and — interestingly — they cluster at the two ends of the Large Intestine channel: on the belly and lower back (where the organ sits) and on the forearm and leg (where the channel reaches out and can be accessed without pressing on a tender abdomen).

How to press any point below: use your thumb or index finger, and press with steadily increasing pressure until you feel a distinct dull ache or mild soreness — not sharp pain. Hold for 30 seconds, then make small circles for 60 to 90 seconds. Breathe slowly and do not hold your breath. A point that feels tender or "interesting" is usually the one your body wants; sharp pain is a signal to move off it.

1. ST25 — Tianshu (Celestial Pivot): the front door

Where: On your abdomen, about three finger-widths to either side of your navel — you have two, left and right, at the level of the belly button.

Why it is here: ST25 is what TCM calls the Front-Mu point of the Large Intestine — the place where the organ's Qi gathers on the front surface of the body. Think of it as the front door to the organ. It is the single most frequently used point in modern clinical research on constipation, and traditionally it is used for exactly what the large intestine does: regulating bowel movement, easing distension, and supporting the healthy downward movement of Qi through the gut.

How to press: Lie on your back with knees bent — this relaxes the abdominal wall. Use two fingers on each side and press gently but firmly, letting your breath do some of the work: press a little deeper as you exhale. Small slow circles. You may feel warmth, gurgling, or a sense of movement. That is the point of the exercise.

2. ST37 — Shangjuxu (Upper Great Hollow): the direct line

Where: On the outer shin. Find the point about a hand-width below your kneecap on the outside of the shin bone, then slide down the same line roughly another hand-width — you are looking for a slightly tender spot in the muscle just outside the bone, around the mid-to-upper shin.

Why it is here: ST37 is the Lower He-Sea point of the Large Intestine — a TCM category of point that acts like an extension phone to a specific organ. It means you can influence the large intestine from your leg, without touching your abdomen at all. In the classical texts it is paired directly with ST25 for bowel regularity, and it shows up near the top of the list in analyses of what practitioners actually use for constipation.

How to press: Sit down and rest your ankle on the opposite knee, or lie on your side. Use your thumb and press inward toward the bone, holding a firm, steady pressure for 60 to 90 seconds per leg. This point often feels productive rather than relaxing — a good sign that you have found it.

3. TE6 — Zhigou (Branching Ditch): the water valve

Where: On the back of your forearm, about four finger-widths up from the wrist crease, in the groove between the two forearm bones.

Why it is here: TE6 sits on the San Jiao (Triple Burner) channel, which TCM describes as the body's water-management system — the network that governs how fluids move, distribute, and drain. If your particular constipation is the dry, pellet-like kind, this is the classic point, because the traditional action here is to "moisten the intestines." Think of TE6 as a valve: when the system is running dry, this is the handle that traditionally gets opened. It is also the point most often paired with KI6 (below) for stools that are too hard.

How to press: Rest your forearm on a table, palm down. Use the thumb of your other hand to press into the groove between the bones, angling slightly toward the elbow. Hold 60 to 90 seconds on each arm. You will often feel a spreading ache up the forearm — that is expected.

4. CV6 — Qihai (Sea of Qi): the engine room

Where: On the midline of your abdomen, roughly two finger-widths below your navel.

Why it is here: CV6 translates as "Sea of Qi," and it is traditionally used to build energy rather than to move it. If your constipation is the exhausting kind — soft stool, no strength to push, feeling drained afterward — this is the point that addresses the missing power. It is also traditionally used when the abdomen feels cold, which is why it is often combined with warmth (a warm compress or a hot water bottle) in the cold-slow pattern.

How to press: Lie down, knees bent. Place two or three fingertips flat on the point and press straight down into the belly, gently, for two to three minutes. Keep the pressure broad rather than pointy — this is a soft area, and it responds better to a warm palm or flat fingers than to a digging thumb.

5. BL25 — Dachangshu (Large Intestine Shu): the service entrance

Where: On your lower back, level with the top of your hip bones (the iliac crests) — about two finger-widths to either side of the spine, at that same height.

Why it is here: BL25 is the Back-Shu point of the Large Intestine: the back-surface gathering place for the same organ as ST25. In practice, this gives you a second entrance to the same system — one that requires nothing but a wall and a tennis ball, and puts no pressure on a tender or distended abdomen. For anyone who finds belly work uncomfortable, this is the practical alternative.

How to press: Stand with your back to a wall and place a tennis ball (or two, held in a sock) between your lower back and the wall, at hip-bone height, just off the midline. Lean in slowly and shift your weight side to side so the ball works across the area. Aim for a comfortably intense pressure you can hold for one to two minutes. Alternatively, a partner can press both points with their thumbs while you lie face down.

6. KI6 — Zhaohai (Shining Sea): the water gauge

Where: On the inside of your ankle, in the natural hollow directly below the inner ankle bone.

Why it is here: KI6 is a classic point for dryness and fluid depletion, and it is almost always described as a pair with TE6 in the traditional literature on constipation. If TE6 is the valve on the water system, KI6 is the gauge that tells you how much reserve is left. For dry, hard stools accompanied by other dryness signs — dry skin, dry mouth at night, scanty urine — this pairing is the traditional starting place.

How to press: Sit cross-legged or with your ankle on the opposite knee. Use your thumb to press into the hollow below the ankle bone, angling slightly toward the heel. Hold 60 seconds per side and breathe slowly — this point is often calming, so it fits naturally at the end of the day.

7. LI4 — Hegu (Union Valley): the traffic controller

Where: On the back of your hand, in the fleshy web between your thumb and index finger. Squeeze your thumb toward your index finger — the point is at the highest point of the muscle mound that pops up.

Why it is here: LI4 sits on the Large Intestine channel itself and is one of the most famous points in all of TCM, traditionally used to get stagnant Qi moving. It fits the second pattern best — the stress-linked, bloated, "urge but nothing happens" kind of stuck. In TCM theory, stress disrupts the smooth flow that the Liver is meant to maintain, and that disruption echoes through the gut; LI4 is used to help release that congestion. Our article on the IBS patterns looks at why stress and bowel function are so tightly linked.

How to press: Use your opposite thumb and press firmly toward the index-finger bone. Hold 30 to 60 seconds per hand. This point is often quite sore — that is normal, but keep the pressure at a level that still lets you breathe easily.

⚠️ Pregnancy caution: LI4 and KI6 are traditionally considered off-limits during pregnancy, and strong or prolonged pressure on abdominal and lower-back points should also be avoided. If you are pregnant, skip this routine and speak with your healthcare provider or a qualified acupuncturist about what is appropriate for you.

Matching Your Points to Your Pattern

Now connect your self-check answer to the toolbox. You do not need all seven points every night — three or four, used consistently, is the traditional approach. Start with the row that matches your description.

Your pattern Start with these points Why this combination
Dryness — hard, pellet-like stools TE6 + KI6 + ST25 Moisten and move: TE6 and KI6 address the fluid side, ST25 prompts the bowel to move
Stagnation — bloated, stress-linked, urge but nothing LI4 + ST25 + TE6 Release congestion and restore smooth flow through the whole route
Deficiency — no strength to push, exhausted after CV6 + ST37 + ST25 Build force, then direct it: CV6 for energy, ST37 as the direct line to the bowel
Cold and sluggish — cold abdomen, low energy, better with warmth CV6 + ST25 + BL25 (with a warm compress) Warm and energize the abdomen; BL25 works the back angle if the belly feels tender
Not sure / mixed ST25 + ST37 + TE6 The shared foundation: the front door, the direct line, and the water valve

The 10-Minute Evening Routine

The traditional advice is not to press harder, but to press more regularly. Ten quiet minutes, most evenings, beats a heroic session once a week. Here is a sequence that flows naturally and finishes in bed.

  1. Lie down, knees bent (1 minute). Take ten slow breaths with a long exhale. This is not filler — a relaxed abdominal wall makes the belly points far easier to work.
  2. CV6, flat fingertips, circular (2 minutes). Broad, gentle pressure below the navel. Slow circles, following the same direction as your bowel: up on the right side, across the top, down on the left.
  3. ST25, both sides (2 minutes). A finger on each side of the navel, pressing a little deeper on the exhale. You may feel gurgling — welcome it.
  4. ST37, one leg at a time (2 minutes). Thumb pressure into the outer shin, 60 seconds per side. This is often the point people feel most clearly.
  5. TE6 + KI6, pairing arms and ankle (2 minutes). Press TE6 on each forearm, then KI6 below each inner ankle. If your stools are dry, give these the extra time.
  6. LI4, finish (1 minute). Press each hand for 30 seconds. Then let go, breathe out, and stop. Do not chase a result — the traditional view is that you are restoring a rhythm, not forcing a mechanism.
If your belly feels distended or tender, use the back route. Do BL25 against a wall with a tennis ball for two minutes instead of the abdominal points, and add CV6 lying down afterwards. It is the same system, entered from the other side.

A few practical extras that pair well with the routine, drawn from general habits rather than any specific remedy: eat your largest meal earlier in the day, keep warm fluids going, take a gentle walk after dinner, and give yourself an unhurried window in the morning when the body's natural urge is strongest. None of these are glamorous. All of them support the same downward, rhythmic movement this routine is working toward.

What the Research Suggests About Acupressure and Constipation

Acupressure is the most studied of the needle-free TCM methods, because it is easy to standardize and easy to test against a sham procedure. The picture is not settled, but it is more interesting than most people expect.

What practitioners actually use. Analyses that pool the acupoint prescriptions used across hundreds of clinical studies of functional constipation consistently find the same shortlist: ST25 is the most frequently used point, followed by ST37, BL23, TE6, ST36, and CV12, with ST25, TE6, and ST37 forming what one review described as the core prescription. In other words, the seven points in this article are not an internet invention — they reflect a stable clinical consensus. A related review of acupoint massage specifically for constipation occurring alongside anxiety found that the most frequently used points were, again, ST25 and TE6.

Acupressure tested directly. A 2023 randomized controlled trial published in Complementary Therapies in Clinical Practice studied acupressure for constipation in patients hospitalized during the acute phase of a stroke, comparing real acupressure points — including LI4, ST36, and ST25 — combined with abdominal massage against a sham procedure. A separate randomized trial protocol from Zhejiang Chinese Medical University was designed specifically to test self-administered acupressure for chronic severe functional constipation, with outcomes including complete spontaneous bowel movements, stool form on the Bristol scale, straining severity, and constipation-related quality of life. That last design detail is the one that matters for you: researchers are testing exactly the thing this article describes — a person pressing points on their own body at home.

The points are being studied individually, too. A meta-analysis of ten randomized trials focused exclusively on ST25 (Tianshu) for functional constipation. Singular points would not attract that kind of attention if researchers considered them inert.

A modern bridge from the leg to the gut. One of the more striking developments in gastroenterology is that stimulating a nerve near the inner ankle — the tibial nerve, right next to the area where KI6 and the spleen-channel points sit — has been studied as a treatment for chronic constipation, with patients applying it at home over six weeks and researchers tracking constipation-related quality of life. Western medicine arrived at the ankle-to-bowel route by a completely different path than TCM did. But the convergence is notable: both traditions ended up treating the lower leg as a place from which the bowel can be influenced.

How to read all of this. These trials are generally small, the sham controls are difficult to design well, and results vary. The most defensible statement is the modest one: some studies suggest acupressure may support bowel regularity and comfort, particularly as an addition to usual care, and it appears to be well tolerated. It is not a replacement for medical evaluation of persistent constipation — and it is not a reason to stop a medication you have been prescribed.

Doing This Safely

Acupressure is gentle, but it is not nothing. A few precautions worth knowing:

  • Pregnancy: avoid LI4, KI6, and strong abdominal or lower-back pressure. Speak with your provider first.
  • Skip abdominal points if you have had recent abdominal surgery, have a hernia, an abdominal wound, a skin infection, a rash, or unexplained abdominal swelling.
  • Never press on damaged skin: bruises, varicose veins, moles that have changed, or areas that are inflamed or numb.
  • Wait 30 to 60 minutes after eating before working the belly points. A full stomach is not the time.
  • Pressure should ache, not hurt. Sharp pain, numbness, or tingling down a limb means back off.
  • If you take blood-thinning medication or bruise easily, use lighter pressure and shorter holds.
  • Stop and reassess if a point leaves you feeling worse, dizzy, or unwell.

And a reminder of framing: this is a complementary practice. It sits alongside good hydration, movement, and appropriate medical care — it does not replace any of them.

When to See a Doctor

Constipation is usually ordinary. Sometimes it is a message that needs a professional ear. Please speak with a healthcare provider — promptly, not eventually — if you notice any of the following:

  • Blood in your stool, or black, tarry stools
  • Unexplained weight loss alongside a change in bowel habit
  • A new, persistent change in bowel habit lasting more than three weeks — especially if you are over 50
  • Severe abdominal pain, a hard or rigid abdomen, fever, or vomiting
  • Inability to pass stool or gas, particularly with abdominal swelling — seek urgent care
  • No bowel movement for a week with pain, distension, or feeling unwell
  • Stools that are consistently very thin ("pencil-thin")
  • A personal or family history of colon cancer or inflammatory bowel disease
  • Constipation that began with a new medication — opioids, iron supplements, and some antidepressants are common culprits. Talk to your prescriber rather than adjusting anything yourself.
  • Numbness, weakness, or changes in urination together with constipation and back pain — this needs urgent assessment.

Nothing in this article should delay that conversation. Acupressure is something you can do while you wait for an appointment, not instead of making one.

Practical Takeaways

If you remember five things:

  • Name your pattern first. Dry and pellet-like needs moisture (TE6 + KI6). Bloated and stress-linked needs flow (LI4 + ST25). Weak and exhausting needs power (CV6 + ST37).
  • Three or four points, most evenings. Consistency does more than intensity — ten minutes done regularly beats an aggressive session once a week.
  • A dull ache is the target. Stop short of sharp pain, and do not press on a full stomach.
  • Use the back door when the front is tender. BL25 against a wall with a tennis ball reaches the same system without touching a distended abdomen.
  • Know the red flags. Blood in the stool, weight loss, severe pain, or a new change lasting weeks means a medical visit, not a longer acupressure session.

There is something quietly satisfying about this approach. It asks nothing of you but attention — a few minutes with your own hands, learning the geography of your own body, and treating a stubborn daily problem as a system to be understood rather than a battle to be won.
