Acupressure for Migraines: A TCM Approach to Headache Relief

Treatment Guide · 9 min read

It starts with a flicker — a blind spot in your vision, a strange light in the corner of your eye, or that unmistakable feeling of something crawling under your skin. An hour later, you're in a dark room with a throbbing skull, wondering when the pain will stop.

Migraines are not headaches. They are a complex neurological event — a cascade of vascular, inflammatory, and nervous system changes that can leave you disabled for hours or days. Painkillers help some of the time, but for many migraine sufferers, the relief is partial, delayed, or comes with side effects that feel almost as bad as the migraine itself.

Acupressure offers something different: a tool you can use at the very first sign of an attack, with zero side effects and no prescription, that works with your body's own pain-modulating systems. In TCM theory, migraines reflect specific patterns of imbalance — and when you know your pattern, you can choose the points that match it.

Scope note: This article focuses specifically on migraine-type headaches and their acupressure management. For a broader overview of acupressure for all headache types — including tension headaches and sinus headaches — see our Acupressure for Headaches guide.

Why the Migraine Is Different in TCM

In TCM theory, a migraine is not simply a "bad headache." It represents a specific type of energetic disturbance — typically involving upward-rebellious Qi that surges uncontrollably to the head. Think of it like steam pressure building in a boiler: the energy is there, but instead of circulating through the body properly, it's rushing upward and getting stuck in your head.

While common tension headaches arise from Qi stagnation (traffic jam), migraines more often involve what TCM calls Liver Yang Rising or Liver Fire Blazing — an upward surge of energy that creates throbbing, pulsating pain, often on one side of the head. This is why migraine pain is often described as "pounding" or "throbbing," and why lying still in a dark room can feel better than moving around (which fans the flames).

Other common migraine patterns in TCM include:

  • Liver Fire Blazing — Throbbing, one-sided headache with irritability, red face, bitter taste in the mouth, and sensitivity to light. Often triggered by stress, anger, or frustration.
  • Phlegm-Dampness Obstructing the Channels — Heavy, pressing headache with dizziness, nausea, brain fog, and a sensation of the head being "wrapped" or "stuffed." Often accompanied by a feeling of fullness in the chest.
  • Blood Stasis in the Head Channels — Sharp, fixed, stabbing pain in one precise spot. May be a long-term pattern that worsens at night. Often seen in chronic migraine sufferers with a history of head trauma.
  • Kidney Essence Deficiency — Dull, empty sensation in the head, with dizziness, poor memory, and lower back soreness. The migraine may be less intense but more persistent and recurring.
Understanding your pattern changes everything. A Liver Fire migraine and a Phlegm-Dampness migraine respond to completely different acupressure points. Using the right point for your pattern is like using the right key for a lock — the wrong key won't budge it, but the right one opens the door.

5 Key Acupressure Points for Migraine Relief

These five points are specifically chosen for their effectiveness in migraine-type headaches. The key is to match the point to your migraine pattern and the timing of your attack.

1. LV3 (Taichong) — The Downward-Draining Point

Location: On the top of your foot, in the hollow between the big toe and second toe, about two finger-widths from the web. Slide your finger up from the webbing toward your ankle — it settles into a natural depression.

Why it works for migraines: LV3 is the most important point for Liver-related migraine patterns. In TCM theory, the Liver meridian is responsible for keeping Qi flowing smoothly throughout the body. When stress, frustration, or suppressed anger disrupts this flow, Qi surges upward instead of circulating — causing the throbbing, one-sided pain characteristic of migraines. LV3 helps "drain" this excess energy downward, like releasing pressure from a boiler.

Best for: Liver Yang Rising and Liver Fire migraine patterns — migraines triggered by stress, with throbbing pain, irritability, and sensitivity to light and sound.

How to apply: Press firmly into the hollow with your thumb for 1-2 minutes. This point is often tender — that tenderness typically decreases with consistent use. Repeat on both feet. You may feel a spreading sensation or a sense of "release" as the pressure builds then subsides.

2. GB20 (Fengchi) — The Migraine "Release Valve"

Location: In the hollow at the base of your skull, between the two large vertical neck muscles. Run your fingers up from the back of your neck until you feel the skull bone, then slide outward into the natural depressions just below the skull ridge.

Why it works for migraines: GB20 is one of the most versatile points for head pain of any kind, but it has particular relevance for migraines. The Gallbladder meridian runs along the sides of the head and neck — exactly where migraine pain tends to localize. GB20 acts as a "release valve" for rising Qi and is traditionally used to sedate Liver Yang — the pattern behind many migraines.

Best for: Migraines that start in the neck or base of the skull, migraines triggered by weather changes or wind, and as a general point for any migraine attack.

How to apply: Use both thumbs simultaneously to press upward and inward into the hollows. Apply steady pressure for 1-2 minutes while tilting your head back slightly into your thumbs. Deep breathing enhances the effect.

3. GB8 (Shuaigu) — The Temple-Side Migraine Point

Location: Above the tip of the ear, in the temporal region of the head. Find the highest point of the ear, then move straight up about one finger-width into the hairline. You should feel a subtle depression.

Why it works for migraines: GB8 is a little-known but highly specific point for temporal-lobe migraine pain. The name "Shuaigu" translates roughly to "leading valley" — suggesting it guides energy through a channel. In TCM theory, it's considered a key point for migraine pain focused on the sides of the head, especially where the pain feels like it's radiating from deep within the temporal region.

Best for: One-sided migraine pain at the temples or sides of the head. Less effective for tension-type headaches or whole-head pressure.

How to apply: Use your middle or index finger to apply gentle, steady pressure for 1 minute. This point may be tender — start lightly and increase pressure gradually. Some people find small circular movements more effective than static pressure here.

4. PC6 (Neiguan) — The Nausea & Pain Connection

Location: About two finger-widths (2 inches) above the wrist crease on the inner forearm, between the two prominent tendons. The point sits in the midline of the forearm.

Why it works for migraines: PC6 is famous for its effect on nausea — and since nausea accompanies migraines in up to 60% of cases (by some estimates), it's an essential point in any migraine toolkit. In TCM theory, the Pericardium channel relates to the chest and diaphragm, and PC6 can "descend rebellious Qi" — meaning it counters the upward surge of energy that characterizes migraines. A 2018 systematic review in Acupuncture in Medicine found PC6 stimulation associated with reduced nausea and autonomic nervous system modulation.

Best for: Migraines with significant nausea, the "wired but tired" feeling, or a sensation of chest tightness during the attack. Also useful for migraines with visual aura.

How to apply: Press firmly into the midline between the two tendons for 30-60 seconds. You may feel a dull ache or a spreading sensation down the forearm. Repeat on the other wrist. Many people find this point most effective when applied as soon as the aura begins.

5. ST36 (Zusanli) — The Qi-Building Point for Prevention

Location: About four finger-widths below your kneecap, one finger-width outward from the shin bone. Flex your foot while pressing — you should feel a muscle contract beneath your finger.

Why it works for migraines: While ST36 is not a "migraine point" in the acute sense, it plays a crucial role in migraine prevention. In TCM theory, ST36 strengthens the Stomach and Spleen systems — the digestive engine that produces Qi and Blood. Many chronic migraine sufferers have underlying Spleen Qi Deficiency (fatigue, poor digestion, brain fog between attacks). Strengthening ST36 can improve the body's overall energy reserves, making you less vulnerable to migraine triggers. Think of it as reinforcing the dike before the flood arrives.

Best for: Daily or weekly preventive practice for chronic migraine sufferers. Especially useful for the Phlegm-Dampness pattern where digestive weakness contributes to migraine attacks.

How to apply: Press firmly with your thumb or knuckle for 1-2 minutes. Many people use ST36 as part of a daily 5-minute acupressure routine for prevention rather than acute relief.

Migraine Timing Protocol: Before, During, and After

One of the most powerful aspects of acupressure is that you can use it differently depending on where you are in a migraine attack. Here's a timeline-based approach:

Phase 1: The Prodrome (Warning Signs, Hours Before)

You know the feeling — the subtle shift in mood, the craving for certain foods, the vague sense that something is coming. This is the best time to intervene.

Protocol: Apply LV3 and PC6 for 1-2 minutes each, twice — once when you first sense the prodrome and again 30 minutes later. The goal is to "redirect" the rising Qi before it becomes a full attack.

Phase 2: The Aura (Visual or Sensory Disturbances)

For those who experience aura (flashing lights, blind spots, tingling), acupressure can help reduce the intensity of what follows.

Protocol: PC6 (1 minute each wrist) + GB20 (1-2 minutes) + GB8 (1 minute each side). Focus on slow, deep breathing while applying pressure. The vagal stimulation from the combination of pressure and breathing may help modulate the neurological cascade.

Phase 3: The Attack (Peak Pain)

When the migraine is at full intensity, less is more. Don't try to apply all five points — pick one or two based on your pattern.

Protocol: If you have nausea: PC6 (hold for 2 minutes). If the pain is throbbing and one-sided: LV3 (1-2 minutes per foot). If the pain started in the neck: GB20 (2 minutes, steady pressure). Apply in a quiet, dark room if possible. The key is steady, sustained pressure — not aggressive kneading.

If you are able to access your feet or lower legs during the attack, ST36 can also help ground the energy and reduce the feeling of "spinning out."

Phase 4: The Postdrome (Recovery, "Migraine Hangover")

The pain has subsided, but you feel drained, foggy, and vulnerable. This is the time for gentle restoration, not aggressive acupressure.

Protocol: Gently massage ST36 for 1-2 minutes per leg. Then apply very light, soothing touch to Taiyang (temple points) — not pushing, just resting your fingers there for a minute. Drink water and rest.

Key principle: The earlier you apply acupressure in a migraine attack, the more effective it tends to be. Waiting until the pain is at 8/10 intensity means you're working against a fully activated pain cascade. Apply points at the very first signal — the flicker, the mood shift, the strange sensation — and you'll have far better results.

Research and What Studies Suggest

Research on acupressure specifically for migraines is growing, complementing the more extensive evidence on acupuncture. Here's what current findings suggest:

  • Reduced migraine frequency with regular practice: A 2021 review in Complementary Therapies in Clinical Practice noted that consistent acupressure practice over 4-8 weeks was associated with reduced migraine frequency and intensity, particularly when combined with lifestyle adjustments. The proposed mechanisms include endorphin release, modulation of pain-gating pathways in the brainstem, and activation of the descending pain-inhibitory system.
  • Acupuncture evidence as support for acupressure: A 2020 meta-analysis in BMJ Open (13 RCTs, 1,090 participants) found that acupoint stimulation — including both acupuncture and acupressure — may improve pain outcomes for migraine and other headache types. Since the same points are often used in both modalities, this provides indirect support for acupressure's potential.
  • PC6 and nausea: A 2018 systematic review in Acupuncture in Medicine found PC6 stimulation associated with reduced nausea in various clinical contexts — a finding with direct relevance for migraine patients, where nausea is a common and debilitating symptom.
  • Nervous system regulation: A 2017 study in Autonomic Neuroscience found that firm pressure on specific acupoints can shift the nervous system from sympathetic (fight-or-flight) to parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) dominance. For migraine patients with stress-related triggers, this may be particularly beneficial.
  • Comparative effectiveness: Some small trials suggest acupressure may be comparable to certain medications for mild-to-moderate migraine attacks in terms of pain reduction, with the advantage of fewer side effects. However, larger trials with standardized protocols are still needed.

It's important to note that while these findings are encouraging, the research is preliminary in many areas. Acupressure is best used as a complementary self-care tool — not as a replacement for prescribed migraine medications when they are needed. Always consult your healthcare provider before changing your migraine treatment plan.

When to Use Acupressure vs. When to See a Doctor

Acupressure can be an excellent complementary tool for managing migraines, but it has limits. Here's how to decide when self-care is appropriate and when you need medical attention.

Acupressure may be helpful when:

  • You recognize the early signs of your typical migraine pattern and want to reduce its intensity or duration
  • You have been diagnosed with migraines by a healthcare provider and understand your pattern
  • You want a non-pharmacological tool between attacks for prevention
  • You're looking for a complementary approach alongside your current treatment plan

⚠️ When to See a Doctor Instead

Seek medical attention if you experience:

  • A sudden, severe headache — especially if it's the "worst headache of your life" (thunderclap headache)
  • Migraine accompanied by fever, stiff neck, confusion, or seizure
  • Headache after a head injury or fall
  • New-onset migraine after age 50
  • Migraine with vision changes, slurred speech, or weakness that doesn't resolve with the attack
  • Increasing frequency or severity of migraines despite treatment
  • Any migraine that requires more than your usual medication dose to control

If you are pregnant, have a pacemaker, or have a chronic medical condition, talk to your healthcare provider before practicing acupressure routinely. Acupressure should never replace prescribed medications or professional care.

Practical Takeaways

Your Migraine Acupressure Toolkit

  1. Know your pattern. Is your migraine throbbing and one-sided (Liver pattern) or heavy with brain fog and nausea (Phlegm-Dampness)? Match your point selection accordingly using the table above.
  2. Act early. The first flicker of aura, the first mood shift, the first hint of a familiar sensation — that's when you apply LV3 and PC6. Waiting reduces effectiveness.
  3. Use the timing protocol. Different phases of a migraine attack need different acupressure approaches. Prodrome = LV3 + PC6. Aura = PC6 + GB20 + GB8. Peak = one or two points only. Recovery = gentle ST36 + Taiyang.
  4. Build a prevention routine. Daily 5-minute ST36 + LV3 practice between attacks may help reduce frequency over weeks. Consistency matters more than intensity.
  5. Track your triggers using TCM categories. Knowing your dominant trigger (stress? weather? food? hormonal?) helps you choose the right preventive points and lifestyle adjustments.

Acupressure for migraines is not a cure — and it won't replace appropriate medical care when needed. But as a tool for managing the early stages of an attack, reducing its intensity, and building resilience between episodes, it can be remarkably effective. The key is matching the point to your specific migraine pattern and applying it at the right time.
