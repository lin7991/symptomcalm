Acupressure for Vertigo: A Step-by-Step Guide to Calming Dizziness

Treatment Guide · 7 min read

You stand up from the sofa and the room tilts. Or you turn your head in bed and the ceiling does a slow, sickening spin. For a few seconds — sometimes much longer — the world refuses to hold still. You grab the wall, close your eyes, and wait for the floor to stop moving. It's one of the most unsettling sensations the body can produce, and it can strike whether you're 30 or 70.

Western medicine calls it vertigo, and it has precise mechanical explanations: inner-ear crystals that have drifted where they shouldn't be, vestibular nerve signals gone haywire, or blood-flow changes to the brain. TCM doesn't disagree with any of that. It simply asks a different question — why is your balance system sending those signals in the first place? — and offers a set of tools that work alongside medical care. Acupressure, the self-administered cousin of acupuncture, is one of the most accessible of those tools: no needles, no prescriptions, just steady thumb pressure on specific points while you breathe.

Before we dive in: For a broader look at how TCM understands the different patterns behind dizziness and vertigo, see our companion article Vertigo from a TCM Perspective. This guide focuses specifically on the hands-on acupressure side — which points, in what order, and when to use them. New to acupressure? Our Acupressure Beginner's Guide covers the basic technique.

Why Acupressure for Vertigo? The Channel Logic

Here's the part that seems counterintuitive at first: several of the most effective points for dizziness are on your hands, wrists, and feet — not your head. In TCM theory this isn't random. The body's energy channels run like a network of roads, and the head sits at the top of nearly all of them. When something goes wrong with balance, TCM describes it as Qi rising too aggressively to the head (like steam pushing up a pipe) or failing to hold the body's center of gravity steady (like a ship whose anchor chain is too thin).

Think of the wrist and foot points as pressure-release valves on the main lines feeding the head. Pressing them creates a distraction signal that travels up the same channels, helping to redirect the flow and settle the spinning. It's the same logic that makes a cold cloth on the back of the neck feel calming during a migraine — the peripheral input influences the central system. Acupressure does this more precisely, point by point, and it's a skill you can learn in an afternoon.

The 6-Point Vertigo Protocol

These six points are the most commonly used in TCM for dizziness and vertigo. Each has a specific job. You don't need to press all six every time — use the descriptions below to pick the two or three that match your situation.

Point 1: PC-6 (Neiguan, "Inner Gate") — the nausea point

Location: On the inner forearm, three finger-widths up from the wrist crease, between the two prominent tendons.

Best for: Vertigo that comes with nausea, queasiness, or a "seasick" feeling — the most common pairing in dizziness episodes.

How to use: Press with your opposite thumb into the hollow between the tendons. You should feel a dull ache, not sharp pain. Hold 60–90 seconds per arm, breathing slowly.

Point 2: GB-20 (Fengchi, "Wind Pool") — the master vertigo point

Location: In the hollows at the base of the skull, where the neck muscles meet the skull bone, about two finger-widths apart.

Best for: Virtually all types of vertigo — this is the classic point for "Wind" disturbances affecting the head, including dizziness linked to stress, tension, or fatigue.

How to use: With both middle fingers, press upward and inward into the hollows. Slow, firm circles for 60–90 seconds. Many people feel immediate loosening in the neck and head.

Point 3: DU-20 (Baihui, "Hundred Meetings") — the steadying point

Location: On the very top of the head, on the midline, about halfway between the tips of your ears.

Best for: The "floaty," light-headed kind of dizziness — feeling unsteady on your feet rather than a full room-spin. In TCM it's traditionally used to lift clear Qi and restore a sense of groundedness.

How to use: Press with the pad of your middle finger, gently but firmly, for 60 seconds. Keep your eyes softly closed.

Point 4: Yintang ("Hall of Impression") — the brow point

Location: Midway between your eyebrows, in the small depression where the bridge of the nose meets the forehead.

Best for: Dizziness with tension, eye strain, or stress — the kind that builds after a long day staring at screens or during anxious stretches.

How to use: Rest your index or middle finger on the point, apply light-to-moderate pressure, and hold for 60 seconds. This point has a well-earned reputation for calming the mind.

Point 5: KI-1 (Yongquan, "Bubbling Spring") — the grounding point

Location: On the sole of the foot, in the depression about one-third of the way down from the toes, where the foot arches.

Best for: Vertigo with a "rising heat" feeling — dizziness with a flushed face, irritability, or a pounding quality. In TCM, KI-1 is the classic point for drawing excessive Qi downward, away from the head.

How to use: Sit, cross one ankle over the other knee, and press firmly with your thumb. Hold 60 seconds per foot. Combine with slow breathing for the full grounding effect.

Point 6: LI-4 (Hegu, "Union Valley") — the general reset

Location: In the fleshy web between your thumb and index finger.

Best for: A general-purpose point used for many head symptoms in TCM — including dizziness that comes with tension or a headache. It's also a point many people find easy to reach mid-episode.

How to use: Press the web of your hand between the thumb and index finger of the other hand, squeezing gently for 60–90 seconds, then switch hands.

Note: LI-4 is traditionally avoided during pregnancy. If you are pregnant, skip this point and use the others instead.

The 3-Minute Rescue Sequence: When the Room Starts Spinning

When a vertigo episode hits, don't try to do everything at once. This short sequence is designed to be done sitting down, right where you are:

  1. Anchor first: Sit down immediately, feet flat on the floor, and fix your gaze on a single still object at eye level. This gives your brain a visual reference point.
  2. GB-20 for 60 seconds: Press the hollows at the base of your skull with both middle fingers. Slow circles. Let your jaw relax.
  3. PC-6 for 60 seconds: If nausea is part of the episode, move to the inner wrist point. Alternate arms if needed.
  4. Long exhales for 2 minutes: With one hand still on a point, breathe in for 4 counts and out for 6–8 counts. The extended exhale activates the calming branch of the nervous system, which in TCM is understood as helping Qi descend out of the head.

If the spinning doesn't settle within 10–15 minutes, or if it comes with any of the danger signals listed below, stop self-care and seek medical attention.

The Daily Maintenance Routine: Building a Steadier Baseline

Between episodes, a short daily practice may help reduce how often dizziness shows up and how intense it feels. This routine takes about five minutes and pairs well with the habits described in our vertigo pattern guide — especially steady sleep, regular meals, and staying hydrated.

  • Morning: 60 seconds on ST-36 (four finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width toward the outside of the shin) on each leg. This point is the classic "energy builder" in TCM, used when dizziness is tied to fatigue or skipped meals.
  • Midday: 60 seconds on Yintang after lunch or screen-heavy mornings, to release the brow tension that feeds unsteadiness.
  • Evening: 60 seconds on KI-1 (soles) before bed, combined with long exhales — a deliberate "power-down" for the head.
  • As needed: GB-20 any time you feel the early warning signs — the subtle sway, the first hint of light-headedness. Intervening early is far easier than during a full episode.

When to See a Doctor

Danger signals — seek medical attention right away: Vertigo that appears suddenly with any of the following is a medical emergency, not a self-care situation:

  • Slurred speech, facial drooping, or weakness/numbness on one side of the body (possible stroke)
  • Chest pain, palpitations, shortness of breath, or fainting
  • A severe headache unlike any you've had before — sometimes described as "the worst headache of my life"
  • Sudden hearing loss, double vision, or difficulty walking
  • Dizziness after a head injury, or while taking blood thinners

Also see a doctor if vertigo is frequent, lasts a long time, or interferes with daily life — especially if you're over 60, where falls are a serious risk. Recurrent vertigo should always be investigated medically; the most common inner-ear cause (BPPV) can often be resolved quickly with a repositioning maneuver performed by a clinician.

What Research Suggests

Acupressure is not a replacement for medical evaluation — but a growing body of research suggests it has a real role as a complementary tool:

  • PC-6 for nausea: The inner-wrist point is among the most studied acupressure points in the world. Multiple meta-analyses — including work in the Journal of Pain and Symptom Management and Anesthesia & Analgesia — have found PC-6 stimulation associated with reduced nausea and vomiting in settings ranging from postoperative recovery to chemotherapy. Since nausea is vertigo's most common companion, this evidence is directly relevant.
  • Acupressure for dizziness: Smaller clinical trials and systematic reviews have explored acupressure for dizziness and vertigo, with some studies reporting reduced symptom intensity and fewer episodes. Most trials are small and designs vary, so results should be read as promising rather than conclusive.
  • The repositioning-maneuver contrast: For benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) — the most common inner-ear cause — the Epley maneuver and related repositioning techniques have strong clinical evidence, often resolving symptoms in a single session. This is the clearest example of where a mechanical fix works better than general self-care, and why a proper diagnosis matters.
  • Vestibular rehabilitation: Research supports graduated balance exercises for chronic dizziness. Acupressure doesn't conflict with this — the points calm the acute signal while vestibular therapy retrains the system over weeks.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  • Start with two points: GB-20 (base of skull) + PC-6 (inner wrist) covers the most common vertigo experience — spinning plus nausea. Add KI-1 (soles) if you feel heat or flushing, DU-20 (top of head) for floaty unsteadiness.
  • Technique matters more than force: Firm, steady pressure into a hollow, held for 60–90 seconds while breathing slowly. Not pinching, not rubbing hard — a deep, dull ache is the target.
  • Use the 3-minute rescue sequence when an episode starts: anchor your gaze, press GB-20, press PC-6, then long exhales.
  • Build a 5-minute daily habit between episodes — ST-36 in the morning, Yintang midday, KI-1 at night.
  • Never press on broken, infected, or painful skin, and skip LI-4 during pregnancy.
  • Get a proper diagnosis first. If your vertigo is new, recurrent, or severe, see a doctor before relying on self-care — inner-ear causes like BPPV often have fast, specific fixes.

Vertigo can make you feel like your own body has become unreliable — but the steady, repeatable pressure of acupressure is something you can control. It won't answer every cause of dizziness, and it shouldn't be the only tool in your kit. Used alongside medical care, good sleep, and the other foundations covered in our vertigo guide, it gives you a practical way to steady yourself when the room starts moving.
