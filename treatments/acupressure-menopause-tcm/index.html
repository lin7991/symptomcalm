Acupressure for Menopause: Points That Support the Transition

Treatment Guide · 7 min read

You're in a meeting when it hits: a wave of heat rolls up from your chest, your face flushes, and you're suddenly fanning yourself with a notepad while trying to finish a sentence. That night, you throw off the covers at 2 AM, drenched — then shiver as the sweat cools. And somewhere in between, you snap at your partner over a dish in the sink. "Just part of aging," everyone says. But your body is sending very specific signals — and TCM has spent centuries mapping them.

Menopause is not a disease in TCM theory. It's a transition — a phase of life where the body rebalances itself. The tools TCM reaches for in this transition are gentle, practical, and self-directed. Acupressure — pressing points on the body with your own fingers — is one of the most accessible: no needles, no clinic visit, no prescription. Just you, a few points, and a few minutes a day.

This guide covers why menopause feels the way it does in TCM terms, the five acupressure points most traditionally associated with this transition, how to match points to your experience, and a simple daily routine you can start this week.

For a broader overview of the transition itself — the TCM view of why hot flashes, night sweats, and mood shifts happen — see our guide to menopause from a TCM perspective and our deep dive on hot flashes. This article focuses specifically on acupressure: which points, where they are, and how to use them.

The Transition in TCM: Why Your Body Feels Like a Thermostat

In TCM, the Kidneys store your deepest reserves — the "battery bank" of the body. Kidney Yin is the cooling, moistening, grounding half of that bank; Kidney Yang is the warming, activating half. As the menstrual years wind down, Kidney Yin naturally declines — and in TCM's image of balance, when the cooling fluid drops, heat has less to hold it in check.

That's the thermostat story: it's not that you're broken, it's that the system is recalibrating. The heat that used to be regulated by a full reservoir of Yin now flares upward — hence the flashes, the night sweats, the warm palms and soles some women describe as "five-palm heat" (五心烦热). At the same time, the transition asks the Liver — the organ associated with smooth flow and adaptability — to manage more emotional turbulence, and the Heart (which houses the Shen, the mind-spirit) to stay anchored through restless nights.

Acupressure fits this map perfectly: different points nudge different parts of the system. Some replenish the cooling reserve, some help the heat descend, some unkink the Liver's flow, and some anchor the mind at night.

Three Menopause Experiences, Three Point Prescriptions

No two menopause transitions look alike. Match your dominant experience to the prescription below — you can use one prescription, or combine points across them.

Experience 1: The Heat Machine

What it feels like: Hot flashes day and night, night sweats that wake you, warm hands and feet, dry skin or dry eyes, restless heat in the evening. In TCM terms, this is the classic Kidney Yin deficiency with Empty Heat rising.

The points: KI-3 and KI-6 — the two Kidney points that support the cooling reserve and are traditionally associated with night sweats. → See "The Five Key Points" below.

Experience 2: The Sleepless Mind

What it feels like: Falling asleep is hard, or you wake at 3–4 AM with the mind running. Palpitations or a fluttering sensation may accompany the insomnia. In TCM terms, the Shen is struggling to anchor — Heart Blood and Yin need support.

The points: HT-7 and SP-6 — one to calm the mind, one to nourish the Yin and Blood that hold it. → See "The Five Key Points" below.

Experience 3: The Pressure Cooker

What it feels like: Irritability that flares fast, feeling wound up or frustrated, tension headaches, sighing, a sense of being "stuck." In TCM terms, Liver Qi is constrained — the transition has loaded more onto a system that was already carrying stress.

The points: LV-3 and GB-20 — one to move the Liver Qi, one to release the neck and head tension that collects with it. → See "The Five Key Points" below.

The Five Key Points

These five points form the core toolkit for the menopause transition. Press each with firm but comfortable pressure — enough to feel a distinct sensation, never sharp pain — in small circles for 1–2 minutes per side.

KI-3 (Taixi) — "Supreme Stream"

Where: On the inside of the ankle, in the depression midway between the tip of the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon.

Why: KI-3 is the source point of the Kidney channel — the well of Kidney Qi and Yin. In TCM, it's the classic point for supporting the cooling reserve when Kidney Yin is running low, making it a first choice for the heat-dominant menopause experience.

KI-6 (Zhaohai) — "Shining Sea"

Where: Just below the inner ankle bone, about a finger-width down, in the hollow where the ankle meets the heel.

Why: KI-6 is traditionally the go-to point for night sweats — it belongs to the Yin Qiao vessel, one of the channels that govern the rhythm of sleep and waking. In the classic literature it appears repeatedly for the "hot at night, dry by day" pattern.

SP-6 (Sanyinjiao) — "Three Yin Meeting"

Where: On the inner lower leg, four finger-widths above the tip of the inner ankle bone, just behind the shinbone.

Why: This is where the Spleen, Liver, and Kidney channels meet — three systems all involved in the transition. SP-6 is traditionally used to nourish Yin and Blood, settle the mind, and support sleep. In many acupressure studies for menopause symptoms, SP-6 is the point most often included.

LV-3 (Taichong) — "Great Surge"

Where: On the top of the foot, in the hollow where the first and second toes meet, about two finger-widths back from the webbing.

Why: The main point for moving Liver Qi. When frustration, irritability, and "stuck" feelings dominate the transition, LV-3 is the release valve — it encourages the smooth flow that the Liver is meant to provide.

HT-7 (Shenmen) — "Spirit Gate"

Where: On the inner wrist crease, in the hollow on the little-finger side of the prominent tendon.

Why: The source point of the Heart channel, HT-7 is the classic point for calming the Shen — the mind-spirit that TCM locates in the Heart. For insomnia, racing thoughts at night, or that fluttering feeling of being unanchored, HT-7 is the point to reach for.

How to Practice: The 5-Minute Daily Routine

Consistency beats intensity with acupressure. A short daily practice builds on itself in a way occasional longer sessions don't.

The routine:

  1. Set aside 5 minutes — morning or evening, same time daily if you can. A regular slot turns the practice into a habit rather than a chore.
  2. Choose your prescription based on your dominant experience (or rotate: heat-focused points in the morning, sleep-focused points before bed).
  3. Press each point for 1–2 minutes in small circles, both sides. Use your thumb or index finger; breathe slowly and let your shoulders drop as you press.
  4. Breathe through it: a long exhale while pressing any point amplifies the settling effect — this pairs naturally with the breathing exercises we cover separately.
  5. Notice, don't judge: some days the points feel tender, some days you barely feel them. Both are fine — the practice is a conversation with your body, not a performance.
Weekend upgrade: if you enjoy the ritual, a warm menopause-focused foot bath before the evening points adds another layer — warmth draws blood and Qi to the feet, which TCM reads as helping the heat descend instead of rising.

What Research Says About Acupressure and Menopause

The evidence base is early but growing, and the pattern of findings lines up with the TCM logic:

  • Hot flashes and night sweats. Several small randomized trials of self-administered acupressure for menopausal symptoms have reported reductions in hot flash frequency and severity over several weeks. Points in these studies frequently include the ankle and foot points described above — the same KI-3, KI-6, and SP-6 territory TCM has used for this pattern for centuries.
  • Sleep. Small trials of acupressure for sleep disturbance in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women have reported improvements in sleep quality scores compared with no treatment, with SP-6 and HT-7 among the points used.
  • Mood and quality of life. Some studies of acupressure during the transition report lower anxiety scores and improved overall quality-of-life measures; sample sizes are modest and designs vary, so read these as promising rather than settled.
  • Mechanism. Research on acupressure and acupuncture more broadly suggests stimulation of points may influence the nervous system and endogenous signaling pathways — a plausible bridge to the TCM view that points adjust the body's internal balance rather than forcing a specific outcome.
What this means for you: acupressure is a self-directed, low-cost practice with a growing evidence base pointing the same direction as its traditional use. It's not a replacement for medical care — if symptoms are disrupting your life, a healthcare provider can help you weigh all the options, including hormone therapy and other established approaches. Think of acupressure as one gentle tool in a larger toolkit.

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:

  • Heat dominant → KI-3 + KI-6. Support the cooling reserve and address night sweats directly.
  • Sleep disrupted → HT-7 + SP-6. Calm the Shen and nourish the Yin that anchors it.
  • Irritable and wound up → LV-3 + GB-20. Move the Liver Qi and release the neck and head tension it collects.
  • Five minutes daily beats one long session. Same time, every day — consistency is the active ingredient.
  • Pair with the breath and warmth: a long exhale while pressing, and a foot bath before evening points, reinforce the settling effect.
  • Skip SP-6 and LI-4 if you might be pregnant — these points are traditionally avoided in pregnancy.

When to See a Doctor

When to See a Doctor: seek medical attention if you experience any of the following:

  • Bleeding that's heavy, prolonged, or happens again after 12+ months without a period — any post-menopausal bleeding needs evaluation.
  • Hot flashes or night sweats accompanied by unexplained weight loss, fever, or persistent fatigue.
  • Sudden severe headaches, chest pain, shortness of breath, or leg swelling — these can signal conditions that need prompt attention.
  • Mood changes that feel overwhelming, persistent low mood, or anxiety that interferes with daily life.
  • Any new or changing symptom that worries you — when in doubt, check it out.
