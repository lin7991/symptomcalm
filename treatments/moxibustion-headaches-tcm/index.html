Moxibustion for Headaches: Warming the Channels for Cold-Type Pain

Treatment Guide · 7 min read

Your headache arrives like clockwork on cold days — or after an afternoon under the air conditioner, or the morning after a drafty sleep. It settles in the back of the head and neck, feels tight and heavy, and a warm shower eases it, at least while the water runs. If that pattern sounds familiar, you are describing what Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) calls a cold-type headache: one where the problem is not too much heat, but a body that has literally contracted under cold influence.

Most headache advice you will find focuses on tension, stress, posture, or screens — all real, all worth addressing. But the cold-type headache has a different signature, and it responds to a different logic. In TCM, one of the classic tools for this pattern is moxibustion: the slow, focused application of heat to specific points on the body. This guide explains when moxibustion fits a headache, which points matter, what a session looks like, and where the evidence stands — so you can decide whether this traditional warming approach belongs in your toolkit.

Why Moxibustion for Headaches? The Warming Logic

Think of a water pipe on a freezing night. The cold does not damage the pipe directly — it shrinks it, slows the water inside it, and eventually stops the flow entirely. Your painkiller is like a pressure sensor that silences the alarm when the pipe is about to burst. It helps in the moment, but it does nothing about the temperature. Moxibustion takes the opposite route: instead of silencing the alarm, it melts the ice.

In TCM theory, this is the logic of Wind-Cold. Wind is the messenger of external change — it finds the gaps in your defenses, and the classic entry point is the back of the neck, just below the skull. Cold is the constrictor: it tightens the channels, and where the channels tighten, Qi and Blood slow down. The head feels the result as a dull, heavy, or band-like pain that worsens in cold air and improves with warmth.

Modern physiology describes the same territory in different words. Cold exposure causes cranial and neck blood vessels to constrict and muscles to guard and tighten; sustained muscle tension in the neck and shoulders is one of the most common contributors to tension-type headache. Heat does the opposite — it dilates vessels and relaxes contracted muscle. Moxibustion, from this angle, is simply a very old, very targeted way of applying that principle: warmth delivered to the exact regions where cold-type headaches live.

One distinction matters here. A heating pad also warms the surface, but moxibustion's slow-burning mugwort produces far-infrared radiation that penetrates several centimeters beneath the skin, reaching muscle and connective tissue rather than just the outer layers. It is surface warmth with a deeper target — which is why TCM considers it a channel-level tool rather than a comfort measure.

Which Headache Patterns Moxibustion Fits (and the One It Doesn't)

Not every headache benefits from heat, and in TCM the match between pattern and method matters more than a generic protocol. These four cards describe the headache patterns where moxibustion is most — and least — relevant.

Wind-Cold Locking the Neck

  • What it feels like: Pain at the back of the head and upper neck, stiff and achy, often starting after cold drafts, weather changes, air conditioning, or a chilly night. You may feel sensitive to cold generally, and a warm shower or a scarf around the neck brings noticeable — if temporary — relief.
  • Why moxibustion fits: This is the textbook cold-type headache. In TCM theory, Wind-Cold tightens the channels at the nape, and gentle heat is the direct counter-move: it relaxes the contraction and lets Qi and Blood move again. The back of the neck is also where the warming goes most easily and safely.
  • Where the warmth goes: The base of the skull (GB-20) and the upper back (GV-14), described below.

Yang Deficiency: The Slow-Burning Furnace

  • What it feels like: A dull, hollow, or nagging headache that is worse in the morning, on cold days, and after exhausting weeks. It travels with cold hands and feet, low energy, a pale complexion, and a lower back that aches — the classic portrait of a body whose internal furnace is burning low.
  • Why moxibustion fits: In TCM theory, Yang is the warming, activating force of the body — the furnace. When Yang is deficient, the head is the furthest "room" from the furnace, and it gets cold first. Moxibustion here acts as a relight: rather than demanding more output from a tired system, it adds warmth back at the source points that nourish Yang.
  • Where the warmth goes: The lower abdomen (CV-6) and upper back (GV-14) — the "reserve tank" areas that build the furnace, not just the head itself.

Blood Stasis Chilled by Cold: The Fixed Pain

  • What it feels like: A sharp, drilling, or stabbing pain in one familiar spot — the same side, the same location, often traceable to an old neck injury or whiplash. It worsens with cold exposure and has a fixed, stuck quality rather than a wandering one.
  • Why moxibustion may fit: In TCM theory, long-standing cold can congeal into Blood Stasis — like oil that thickens when it cools. Heat thins the movement of Blood and Qi, and warming therapy is traditionally one of the approaches used to support areas where flow has become sluggish and fixed.
  • Where the warmth goes: Over the specific tight bands in the neck and shoulders, plus a distant point (LI-4) that is traditionally paired with head and face complaints.

When Heat Is NOT the Fit: Fire and Heat Patterns

  • What it feels like: A throbbing, pounding headache that worsens with heat, anger, or stress — often with a red face, bitter taste, irritability, and a feeling of heat rising to the head. Or a headache with night sweats, hot flushes, and a dry mouth, suggesting Yin deficiency with internal heat.
  • Why moxibustion is not the first choice: These are patterns of excess heat or dryness, not cold contraction. Adding more warmth to a system already running hot is like adding fuel to a smoldering fire — in TCM theory it can aggravate rather than ease. This is the single most important distinction to draw before reaching for moxa.
  • What to do instead: If your headache feels hot rather than cold — throbbing, heat-aggravated, with a red face — cooling approaches and gentle acupressure are traditionally the better starting points. Our acupressure guide for headaches covers pressure-point options that suit heat patterns better.

The takeaway from these patterns: moxibustion is a warming tool. It shines when the headache feels cold, tight, and worse in cold — and it is a poor match when the system is already running hot. When in doubt, ask yourself the two-question test: Do cold air, air conditioning, or cold days make the headache worse? Does warmth — a shower, a scarf, a warm room — noticeably ease it? Two yeses point toward the cold-type pattern where moxibustion traditionally fits.

Where the Warmth Goes: Headache-Specific Points

Here is the most important thing to know: moxibustion for headaches does not mean applying heat to your head. The face, eyes, and temples are not standard moxa territory — the skin is thin, the structures beneath it are delicate, and a stray ember near the eye is a serious hazard. Instead, the warmth goes to the regions that feed cold-type headaches: the base of the skull, the upper back, and points farther down the body that regulate the channels running to the head. This is what makes moxibustion distinct from acupressure, which can work directly on points around the head.

  • GB-20 (Fengchi) — the Wind Pool: The hollows just below the base of the skull, where the neck meets the head. In TCM, this is the classic entry gate for Wind-Cold — the "door" through which cold reaches the head. Warming this zone is the most direct move for a headache that starts at the back of the head and neck. Because the area is close to the skull and hair, it is best warmed with a moxa stick held at a distance, or by a practitioner using indirect techniques — never with burning moxa pressed to the skin.
  • GV-14 (Dazhui) — the Meeting of Yang: Below the spinous process of the seventh cervical vertebra, roughly where the neck meets the upper back. In TCM theory, this is where all the Yang channels converge — a natural "bus bar" for the body's warming energy. Warming GV-14 is a common way to support the whole system against cold, not just the head.
  • BL-10 (Tianzhu) — the upper neck: About two finger-widths from the midline on the thick muscles that flank the upper neck. Frequently tight in people whose headaches arrive with neck stiffness, this is a complementary zone to GB-20 for the nape of the neck.
  • LI-4 (Hegu) — the distant control point: In the fleshy web between the thumb and index finger. The classic TCM mnemonic runs "face and mouth, Hegu collects" — a distant point that is traditionally paired with head and face complaints, useful when you cannot warm the neck directly. Note: LI-4 is traditionally avoided during pregnancy, so it is not recommended as a self-care point for pregnant readers.
  • CV-6 (Qihai) — the Sea of Qi: About one and a half cun below the navel on the lower abdomen. This is a "reserve tank" point — warming it is the move for the Yang-deficiency pattern, where the headache is a downstream symptom of a furnace running low, rather than a problem located in the head itself.

Two technical notes. First, warmth should be comfortable — a pleasant, penetrating heat, never a burning sensation. The moxa stick is held a few centimeters away from the skin and moved in slow circles or small up-down motions, and the skin is checked regularly. Second, the session should follow the logic of the pattern: neck and upper back for Wind-Cold, lower abdomen and upper back for Yang deficiency, and targeted bands plus a distal point for the fixed, stabbing pain.

A Step-by-Step Moxibustion Session for Headaches

If you are new to moxibustion, start with a licensed practitioner and use their session as a template. If you are already comfortable with a moxa stick at home, a headache-focused session follows a predictable shape:

  1. Prepare the room and yourself. Sit comfortably with your neck relaxed — or lie on your side if a partner is assisting. Open a window slightly; mugwort smoke is part of the practice, but the room should not be sealed. Keep a bowl of water nearby for extinguishing the stick.
  2. Start at the nape: GB-20 and BL-10. Light the moxa stick, let it burn to a stable glow, and warm each hollow below the skull for 3–5 minutes, keeping the stick 2–4 cm from the skin and moving it slowly. The goal is deep, even warmth across the whole nape, not a hotspot.
  3. Move to GV-14. Warm the base of the neck for 3–5 minutes. This step broadens the effect from "this spot" to "the whole channel system," which is the traditional logic for a channel-level tool.
  4. Add the distant point: LI-4. Two to three minutes on the web of the hand, alternating sides. This is the traditional "remote control" for head complaints and is especially useful when neck skin is sensitive.
  5. Close with CV-6 for the deficiency pattern. If your pattern is Yang deficiency — cold extremities, low energy, morning-aggravated pain — finish with 5–8 minutes over the lower abdomen. Skip this step if you are only treating an acute Wind-Cold headache.
  6. Aftercare. Extinguish the stick properly in the water bowl. Rest for 10–15 minutes, drink something warm, and avoid cold drinks, cold drafts, and cold showers for at least an hour afterward — the goal is to keep the channels open once the warmth has done its work.

Frequency follows the pattern. For an acute cold-type headache, one or two sessions as the headache builds can be enough. For the recurring, deficiency-driven pattern, a short session two to three times a week — often paired with dietary warmth and sleep — is the traditional rhythm, with a break week every few weeks. If sessions leave you feeling drained rather than eased, reduce frequency and duration; that response is a signal about dosage, not a failure.

Moxibustion vs. Heat Pack vs. Cupping vs. Acupressure

All of these tools work on the same underlying insight — that headache pain often lives in the neck, shoulders, and channel system rather than in the skull itself. They differ in how they act on it:

Tool What it does Best fit
Moxibustion Slow, penetrating warmth (far-infrared) delivered to specific points Cold-type and deficiency headaches — pain worse with cold, eased by warmth
Heating pad / hot towel Surface heat over a broad area Quick general relaxation of a cold, stiff neck; less targeted, shallower
Cupping Suction that lifts and loosens stagnation Tension and stagnation headaches — congested, tight, "stuck" quality
Acupressure Finger pressure on points, no heat involved Any pattern, including heat patterns; the most flexible self-care option

The practical rule: moxibustion is the warming choice for a cold headache; cupping is the releasing choice for a congested one. For a heat-aggravated headache, neither warming nor suction is the natural fit — pressure and cooling approaches suit it better. What the Research Says About Moxibustion and Headaches

Research on moxibustion specifically for headaches is less extensive than the research on acupuncture, but a few strands are worth knowing:

  • Migraine trials: Several small randomized trials have compared moxibustion — often at points including GB-20 and GV-14 — with sham or no treatment for migraine, and reported reductions in attack frequency and intensity over the study period. The studies are small and designs vary; reviewers consistently call for larger, more rigorous trials before firm conclusions are drawn.
  • Tension-type headache: Some clinical studies have explored warming therapies for tension-type headache and reported improvements in neck muscle tenderness and headache days, again in small samples. The mechanism proposed — increased local blood flow and reduced muscle guarding — aligns with what temperature research shows more broadly.
  • The broader heat evidence: Controlled studies of topical heat in musculoskeletal pain have repeatedly found that warmth reduces muscle spasm and increases tissue extensibility in the short term — a plausible foundation for why warming the neck may ease headache-related tension.
  • A caution on the cold-type distinction: The specific "cold-type headache" category is a traditional TCM classification rather than a formal Western diagnosis, so most of the research is on migraine and tension-type headache broadly. The practical translation: if your headache fits the cold-aggravated, warmth-relieved pattern, warming approaches are a reasonable complement to conventional care — and if it does not, skip them.

In short: the evidence is promising but preliminary. Moxibustion is best understood as a complementary layer within a broader headache-management picture — alongside sleep, hydration, posture, and whatever your healthcare provider recommends — not as a stand-alone answer.

Using Moxibustion Safely

Moxibustion is generally low-risk when performed correctly, but it has clear boundaries — and most of them matter more around the head and neck than anywhere else:

  • Never moxa the face, eyes, or scalp directly. The forehead, temples, and eye area are off-limits for direct moxa. The work happens at the base of the skull, upper back, and body points — never on thin facial skin or anywhere near the eyes.
  • Burns are the real risk. A moxa stick burns at several hundred degrees Celsius at the tip. Keep it 2–4 cm from the skin, keep moving, and check the skin frequently. If it feels too hot, it is too close. Never fall asleep during a session.
  • Reduced sensation. Anyone with diabetic neuropathy, peripheral nerve damage, or conditions that dull skin sensation should not use moxa without professional guidance — you may not feel a burn forming.
  • Pregnancy. If you are pregnant, avoid self-directed moxibustion entirely and discuss it with a qualified practitioner and your healthcare provider first — certain points and abdominal warming are traditionally avoided during pregnancy. (This is the opposite direction from the famous breech-baby use, which is a practitioner-supervised protocol, not home self-care.)
  • Heat and acute conditions. Skip moxibustion during a fever, an active infection, or any heat-aggravated condition. If your headache is throbbing, heat-aggravated, or accompanied by flushing, this tool is not the fit.
  • Skin conditions and medications. Avoid moxa over broken skin, rashes, or areas of eczema. If you take anticoagulant medication, check with a healthcare provider first.
  • Fire safety. Use a proper moxa holder or bowl, keep flammable items away, and always fully extinguish the stick — mugwort smolders for a long time and re-ignition is possible.
When to See a Doctor: Seek medical attention promptly if your headache is sudden and severe (the worst you have ever had), comes with fever and a stiff neck, follows a head injury, is accompanied by vision changes, weakness, numbness, confusion, or speech difficulty, or if you are over 50 and experiencing a new type of headache. Also see a doctor if your headache pattern changes distinctly from your usual pattern, or if self-care approaches do not reduce frequency or severity over several weeks.

Practical Takeaways

  • Match the pattern first: Moxibustion fits headaches that are worse with cold and eased by warmth — Wind-Cold locking the neck, Yang deficiency, or cold-chilled Blood Stasis. If your headache is throbbing, heat-aggravated, or comes with a red face, heat is not the move.
  • Two-question test: Does cold air or air conditioning make the headache worse? Does warmth noticeably ease it? Two yeses = the cold-type pattern where moxibustion traditionally belongs.
  • Heat the neck and back, not the head: GB-20 and BL-10 at the base of the skull, GV-14 at the base of the neck, LI-4 on the hand, and CV-6 on the lower abdomen for the deficiency pattern. Never moxa the face, eyes, or temples.
  • Short, warm, comfortable sessions: 3–5 minutes per point, stick held 2–4 cm away, moving slowly. One or two sessions for an acute cold headache; two to three times a week for the recurring pattern, with a break week every few weeks.
  • Layer it, don't isolate it: Use warming approaches alongside sleep, hydration, posture work, and whatever your healthcare provider recommends. If sessions leave you drained, reduce frequency and duration.
