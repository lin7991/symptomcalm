Tui Na for Headaches: 6 Self-Massage Moves for a Tense, Aching Head

Treatment Guide · 9 min read

It starts as a dull pressure behind your eyes. By mid-afternoon it has wrapped around your skull like a slowly tightening band. You rub your temples, roll your neck, reach for whatever is in the medicine cabinet. It helps for three or four hours, and then the ache creeps back — sometimes worse than before.

You've probably been told it's stress. Or posture. Or "one of those things." But here is a question Western medicine rarely asks: why does the tension always land in the same places — the base of the skull, the sides of the neck, the temples?

Tui Na (pronounced “twee-nah”), the ancient Chinese bodywork tradition, has a map for this. In TCM theory, the head is where the body's Yang channels converge, and the neck is the narrow corridor they all must pass through. When that corridor tightens, traffic backs up — and the traffic jam shows up as a headache.

The good news: Tui Na gives you hands-on tools to work that congestion from the outside in. No needles, no equipment, no appointment. These are techniques you can use at your desk, on the couch, or the moment you feel the first flicker of pressure behind your eye.

Before we dive in: this article is about Tui Na — the dynamic hand techniques (pressing, kneading, grasping, pushing) applied to the head and neck. If you prefer static pressure on specific points, see our acupressure guide for headaches. If you're curious about suction cups instead of hands, see cupping for headaches. And for the broader Tui Na foundation, start with self-Tui Na for beginners.

Why Your Head Hurts: The TCM Map of the Head and Neck

In TCM theory, the body's energy pathways — the meridians — are grouped into Yin and Yang systems. The Yang channels carry energy along the back, the outer surfaces, and upward. All of them converge at the head; classic TCM texts describe the head as “the meeting place of all Yang.” Think of it as a central interchange where every major highway comes together.

Two highways matter most for headaches. The Gallbladder channel starts at the outer corner of the eye, runs up the side of the head (straight through the temple), curves behind the ear, and drops down the side of the neck to the shoulder. The Bladder channel runs from the inner corner of the eye, over the top of the skull, and down the back of the neck — right over the thick muscles at the base of the skull.

Now the key insight: the neck is the corridor both highways must pass through. The suboccipital muscles at the base of the skull, the trapezius ridge, the muscles along the sides of the neck — these are the on-ramps. When they tighten (from screens, stress, cold drafts, poor sleep), the corridor narrows and traffic backs up into the head. In TCM language: Qi and Blood stagnate in the channels, and stagnation feels like pressure, aching, and tightness.

There is also Wind. In TCM, Wind is a factor with a signature: it arrives suddenly, it moves, and it attacks the upper body first. A headache that shows up with a weather front, after a cold draft, or following a blast of air conditioning is, in TCM terms, Wind entering the channels. That's why the most famous headache point in TCM is called Feng Chi — “Wind Pool” — the hollows at the base of the skull where Wind is said to pool before it moves upward.

What Makes Tui Na Different From Acupressure (and a Regular Massage)

Tui Na is often translated as “Chinese massage,” but it behaves differently from both a relaxation massage and acupressure:

  • Acupressure holds a point; Tui Na moves the channel. Acupressure is static pressure on specific points. Tui Na uses dynamic techniques — pressing, kneading, grasping, pushing — to physically encourage Qi and Blood to move along the channel pathways. If acupressure is pressing the elevator button, Tui Na is walking the stairs.
  • A relaxation massage soothes muscles; Tui Na follows channels. A spa massage follows the shape of the muscle. Tui Na follows the roadmap of the meridians, working the tissue in the direction the channel travels — including long, sweeping strokes down the sides of the neck that a typical massage doesn't include.

Five core hand techniques form the toolkit for headache work:

Technique What it looks like What it does
Shu (combing) Fingertips comb through the scalp like a wide-tooth comb Dredges the channels across the crown; moves Qi over the whole head
Rou (kneading) Small circular kneading with fingertips or thumb pads Softens local tension — the classic “working the dough” motion
An (pressing) Sustained, perpendicular press on a point Releases a specific congestion point, like the Wind Pool
Na (grasping) Pinch, lift, and release muscle between thumb and fingers Grabs and releases the big neck-and-shoulder knots
Tui (pushing) One-direction push along a channel with thumb or palm Sweeps stagnation ahead of it, like smoothing a rumpled carpet

The 6-Move Self-Tui Na Protocol for Headaches

Total time: about 10 minutes. Use it at the first sign of a headache, after a long screen session, or before bed on a tense day. Sit comfortably, shoulders relaxed, and breathe slowly through your nose throughout.

Move 1 — Scalp Combing (Shu Fa) — 2 minutes

Spread your fingers and place your fingertips firmly on your scalp at your hairline. Comb from the forehead back over the crown to the base of the skull — like combing a tangled mane, with enough pressure to feel the scalp move over the skull. Cover the whole head: front, sides, crown. Then make three slow passes dragging your fingertips in straight lines from the crown down the sides of the head. If a spot feels tender or tight, slow down and make small circles there. This single move is the workhorse of the protocol: it physically moves the tissue across every channel that crosses the top of the head.

Move 2 — Temple Kneading (Rou Fa) — 1 minute

Place the pads of your index and middle fingers on both temples simultaneously. Make slow, deliberate circles — pressure firm but never sharp. The temples are a classic pain-storage site, and the Gallbladder channel passes directly through them. If your headache is one-sided, spend extra time on the affected side.

Move 3 — Wind Pool Press (An Fa on GB-20) — 2 minutes

Find the hollows at the base of your skull, roughly two finger-widths apart, where the neck meets the skull. Press with your thumbs, angled slightly upward and inward, until you feel a firm-but-pleasant depth. Hold for 5–10 seconds, then make small circles, then release. Repeat three or four times. In TCM this is the classic move for releasing Wind at the gate — whether the wind came from outside (weather, drafts) or from rising internal pressure (stress, anger, overheating).

Move 4 — Neck Grasping (Na Fa) — 2 minutes

Pinch the muscle ridge between your neck and shoulder — the upper trapezius — between your thumb and fingers. Squeeze, lift slightly, hold for 3–5 seconds, then release. Walk your fingers along the muscle from the base of the neck toward the shoulder, then repeat on the other side. Three passes per side. Finish with a press on GB-21 (Jian Jing), the midpoint between the neck and the shoulder tip on the trapezius ridge — five slow circles per side. This is where neck tension and head pressure connect; releasing here often eases the band sensation around the head.

Move 5 — Gallbladder Channel Push (Tui Fa) — 1 minute

From the temple, use three fingers to push slowly back along the side of the head, behind the ear, down the side of the neck, and onto the shoulder — one smooth, continuous stroke. Repeat three to five slow passes per side. This is “dredging the highway”: you are moving in the exact direction the Gallbladder channel travels, sweeping stagnation ahead of your fingers instead of just pressing on it.

Move 6 — The Pattern-Matched Finish — 1–2 minutes

Finish with the point that matches your headache pattern (see the next section):

  • Yintang (between the eyebrows) — a slow press-hold for frontal pressure and to settle the mind
  • GV-20 (crown of the head) — a brisk palm rub for vertex headaches or general fogginess
  • LI-4 (webbing between thumb and index finger) — a firm pinch-hold for tension-type and general head pain
  • LR-3 (top of the foot, between first and second toes) — a press-hold for throbbing, stress-driven headaches

Matching the Protocol to Your Headache Pattern

Not all headaches are the same. TCM distinguishes patterns, and each responds best to a slightly different emphasis within the protocol:

Your headache feels like... TCM pattern Emphasize
A tight band around the head; neck and jaw tension; heavy screen days Channel constraint (Qi stagnation in the neck corridor) Moves 3 and 4, finish with LI-4
Throbbing, one-sided; worse with stress or frustration; light and sound sensitivity Liver Yang rising (pressure moving upward) Moves 1 and 5, finish with LR-3
Dull, heavy ache; worse with fatigue; better with rest; tends to hit late in the day Qi and Blood deficiency (low fuel at the top) Gentle versions of all moves, finish with a GV-20 rub and ST-36 (below the knee)
Sudden onset; weather-triggered; cold draft or air conditioning exposure; feels cold or “achy” Wind-Cold invasion Move 3 emphasized, warm hands, wrap your neck afterward
How to use this table. You don't need a diagnosis to start — just notice which description matches today's headache. Run the full 10-minute protocol, then spend your extra minute on the pattern-matched finish. Over a few weeks, you may notice which pattern shows up most often for you — that's your default emphasis.

What the Research Says About Tui Na and Headaches

Research on manual therapy for headaches is growing, and several lines of evidence are relevant to Tui Na:

  • The neck-head connection is anatomically real. The upper neck and the head share a communication hub in the brainstem called the trigeminocervical complex, where sensory input from the neck converges with the trigeminal nerve that carries sensation from the face and head. This overlap is why many researchers believe working the neck may influence head pain — and it aligns with TCM's insistence that the neck is the corridor into the head.
  • Manual therapy for tension-type headache. A 2012 systematic review in The Journal of Headache and Pain examined manual therapies, including massage, for tension-type headache and found some evidence of benefit compared with no treatment, while noting that study quality varied.
  • Massage therapy for headache frequency. Several small trials and reviews have reported that massage therapy is associated with reduced headache frequency and intensity, particularly for tension-type headache. Most studies are small and designs vary.
  • Tui Na specifically. Some randomized trials have compared Tui Na with routine care for tension-type headache and reported greater reductions in headache frequency and intensity over the study period. Most of these trials were conducted in China with modest sample sizes.
  • Proposed mechanisms. Manual pressure may help relax contracted muscles, raise pressure pain thresholds, and shift the nervous system toward a rest state — which, in TCM language, is Qi and Blood moving again through the channels.

The honest summary: the evidence is encouraging but preliminary. Tui Na is best used as a complementary self-care tool — a way to work with your body's own channels — not as a replacement for medical care. Some studies suggest it may support headache management; no technique is a guarantee.

Safety First: When Self-Tui Na Isn't the Right Tool

  • Never press the front of the neck. The carotid arteries run there, and firm pressure on the front or sides of the neck is dangerous. Keep all neck work on the back and outer edges — the trapezius ridge and the muscles beside the spine.
  • Check with a doctor first if you have cervical spine conditions, osteoporosis, vascular disease, a recent neck injury, or a history of stroke or TIA. Neck techniques are gentler than chiropractic adjustments, but they still involve force.
  • Pregnancy: the points GB-21 and LI-4 are traditionally avoided during pregnancy. If you are pregnant, stick to gentle scalp combing and temple kneading, and skip the neck grasping and distal point work.
  • Stop if it makes things worse. If pressure increases the headache, or you feel dizzy or nauseous during the moves, stop immediately.
  • Skip broken or irritated skin. Don't work over rashes, sores, or areas that are hot, swollen, or inflamed.
  • Gentle beats strong, every time. Self-Tui Na is not “the harder the better.” If a technique is painful, lighten up. The goal is release, not resistance.

When to See a Doctor

Some headaches are warning signals. See a healthcare provider promptly if you experience any of the following:

  • A headache that came on suddenly and is the worst you've ever had (sometimes called a thunderclap headache)
  • A headache accompanied by fever and a stiff neck
  • A headache that began after a head injury
  • A headache with vision changes, weakness, numbness, slurred speech, or confusion
  • A new or different headache after age 50
  • A headache that keeps worsening, or that won't go away
  • A headache that wakes you from sleep or is worse when lying down
  • A headache with jaw pain or pain when chewing (a possible sign of temporal arteritis, especially over 50)

Practical Takeaways

Practical Takeaways:
  • The 10-minute protocol: comb the scalp (2 min) → knead the temples (1 min) → press the Wind Pool (2 min) → grasp the neck (2 min) → push the Gallbladder channel (1 min) → pattern-matched finish (1–2 min).
  • Match your pattern: tight band → neck emphasis plus LI-4; throbbing one-sided → scalp plus LR-3; dull and fatigued → gentle moves plus GV-20 and ST-36; weather-triggered → Wind Pool plus warmth.
  • Timing matters: use the protocol at the first flicker of pressure. The earlier you release the congestion, the easier it is to shift.
  • Safety in three lines: back and sides of the neck only (never the front); skip GB-21 and LI-4 during pregnancy; stop if the moves make things worse.
  • Think of Tui Na as one layer: it pairs well with sleep, hydration, and screen breaks — and it's a complement to care, not a substitute for it.
