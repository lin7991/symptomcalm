Autumn Health in TCM: Dryness, the Lung, and the Art of Letting Go

TCM Basics · 9 min read

You know the exact day it happens. The air turns crisp, the light goes gold, and within about 48 hours your lips are cracked, your throat is scratchy by evening, your skin feels two sizes too tight, and there is a cough that announces itself as a dry tickle with nothing to cough up.

Then comes the second thing — the one nobody warns you about. A low-grade melancholy that arrives with the shorter days. You start wanting to cancel plans, sleep an hour earlier, clear out closets. Maybe you pick up that first cold of the season, and the dry, tickly cough hangs around for three weeks like a guest who won't leave.

Western medicine will hand you a humidifier, a lip balm, and a shrug. There is no real framework for why your body behaves differently in October than it did in July. But Traditional Chinese Medicine has been mapping this exact seasonal transition for well over two thousand years — and autumn has its own signature, its own organ system, and its own internal logic. Once you see the pattern, the seasonal advice stops sounding like folklore and starts sounding like maintenance.

Think of it like this: your body runs a climate system on a yearly cycle, the way a house does. In summer, the humidifier is on full blast and the windows are open — everything is loose, damp, and at the surface. By winter, the heat is locked in and everything is stored deep and still. Autumn is the awkward interval in between: the humidifier has switched off but the heat hasn't kicked in yet. It is the driest moment of the building's year — and that is not a metaphor. It is the whole reason autumn is Dryness season in TCM.

This guide covers what TCM theory says about autumn health: the Metal element that governs the season, why the Lungs are its focal organ, the three most commonly described autumn dryness patterns, the emotional dimension of the season, and what you can practically do with food, daily rhythm, and acupressure. For the bigger picture of how all five seasons fit together, see our Seasonal Living in TCM guide.

The Metal Element: Autumn's Signature Isn't Cold — It's Dry

Every season in the TCM five-phase framework carries a distinct quality — not just a temperature. Summer's quality is heat. Winter's is cold. Autumn's is dryness. And this distinction explains something most people get wrong about the season.

People prepare for winter by thinking about cold. But the discomfort of September and October — cracked lips, scratchy throat, tight skin, tickly cough — is not a cold problem. It is a moisture problem, arriving weeks before the temperature is the issue.

Here is how the seasons map onto organs and qualities in TCM theory:

Season Element Organ pair Signature quality
Spring Wood Liver & Gallbladder Wind / rising, expanding
Summer Fire Heart & Small Intestine Heat / outward, expansive
Late summer Earth Spleen & Stomach Dampness / heavy, ripening
Autumn Metal Lung & Large Intestine Dryness / contracting, inward
Winter Water Kidney & Bladder Cold / deep, storing

Metal, in the five-phase view, is the phase of contraction, refinement, and boundaries. It is what remains after everything extraneous has been burned away or fallen off: the blade, the coin, the clear structure. Metal is the element of standards and of sorting — of deciding what stays and what goes.

Autumn's work in nature is subtraction. The tree drops what it can no longer afford to maintain. This is why the Metal element is assigned to the Lungs and the Large Intestine — two organs whose shared job is boundaries and release. One takes in (breath), the other sends out (elimination). Both are about exchange at the edge of the body.

Analogy: picture the end of the season at a lake house. In July the windows are open, the dock is in the water, everything is loose and outward-facing. By October somebody has to close the windows, pull the dock in, and drain the pipes — not because anything is broken, but because the season changed. Autumn in TCM is that closing-down work happening inside your body. And just as draining the pipes is what leaves them most vulnerable to a cold snap, the body's surfaces — nose, throat, skin — become most vulnerable exactly when the moisture withdraws from them.

The Invisible Dehumidifier: How Dryness Reaches Your Lungs and Skin

Dryness — zao in Chinese — is one of the six external influences in TCM theory, alongside Wind, Cold, Heat, Dampness, and Summerheat. It is the only one defined by an absence rather than a presence. Cold arrives. Dryness is what happens when something leaves.

Analogy: a houseplant in a room where the air conditioning ran all night. Nothing attacked the plant. Nobody forgot to water it yesterday. But by morning the leaf edges have curled, because the surrounding air simply stopped holding moisture. Dryness works by subtraction — which is exactly why it's easy to miss until your body starts complaining.

So why the Lungs? In TCM, the Lung is described as the "tender" organ — the only Yin organ that opens directly to the outside world through the nose and airways. In TCM theory it also governs the skin and body hair. That means the Lung's territory is effectively your entire outer surface: nose, throat, bronchi, and skin form one continuous frontier between you and the air.

Dry air reaches that frontier first. This is also why TCM reads a dry cough, a dry nose, and dry, flaky skin as related rather than as three unrelated annoyances.

There is a second piece of the mechanism worth knowing. In TCM theory, Lung Qi has a dispersing function: it circulates a fine mist of moisture and defensive energy out to the body's outermost layer. Think of the misting system in a greenhouse, keeping every leaf hydrated from a central reservoir. Dryness doesn't break the misting system — it simply raises the evaporation rate until the mist can't keep up with demand. For a fuller picture of how this system works, see our guides to the Lungs in TCM and Lung Qi.

And then there is the Large Intestine. TCM pairs it with the Lung because the Lung's natural direction of movement is descending — and that downward motion is traditionally thought to assist elimination. When the Lung system dries out, the bowel often follows. This is why autumn constipation is such a classic seasonal complaint in TCM, and why the seasonal advice for it is about moisture rather than fiber alone.

Three Faces of Autumn Dryness

Dryness doesn't land identically in everyone. Where it shows up depends on your constitution and on which part of the Lung's territory has the thinnest reserve. In TCM theory, three presentations are most commonly described at this time of year. Read them as a self-assessment, not a diagnosis.

🍐 Pattern 1: Dry Lung — The Tickly Cough With Nothing to Cough Up

What it is: the upper-airway version of the season, most typical of early autumn when the days are still warm but the air has gone dry. In TCM terms, Dryness has reached the Lung at the level of the nose, throat, and bronchi.

Signs may include: a dry, unproductive cough that is worse at night and on waking; a scratchy tickle in the throat, as though you need to clear something that isn't there; a dry nose, sometimes with a spot of blood when you blow; a voice that goes hoarse by late afternoon; thirst that small sips don't quite satisfy.

Why it happens: the thin moisture film lining your airways is both a physical barrier and a cleaning mechanism. When it dries, the lining becomes more sensitive to ordinary stimuli — dust, a temperature change, a breath of cold air — and the cough reflex fires on triggers it would normally ignore. TCM names this Dryness in the Lung.

What traditionally may help: humidified air, warm fluids sipped steadily, honey in warm water, steamed pear, and avoiding dry roasted snacks. Also: talk less, and don't force the cough — a dry cough that is repeatedly forced tends to irritate rather than clear.

Important: a cough lasting more than three weeks, bringing up blood, or accompanied by breathlessness or chest pain needs medical assessment. That is not a seasonal adjustment matter.

🥣 Pattern 2: Dry Stomach and Intestines — The Tank Runs Low Downstream

What it is: the lower version of the same theme — less moisture reaching the digestive tract and the bowel. In TCM theory the Stomach prefers moisture, and the Large Intestine depends on both fluids and the Lung's descending motion.

Signs may include: a dry mouth, especially at night; thirst for small amounts rather than large gulps; an appetite that's present but loses interest in dry food; constipation with dry, hard stool; a sense of the gut being "irritable" or sluggish rather than painful.

Why it happens: three autumn factors stack up. The air is drier. The diet shifts toward drier, baked, and roasted food. And you simply drink less, because cool weather dials down the thirst signal — so intake falls while need has not. The result, in TCM terms, is a system running on a lower water line.

What traditionally may help: congee or porridge, stews and soups, a soaked tablespoon of chia or ground flaxseed daily, pears, spinach, sweet potato, and water drunk on a schedule rather than on thirst. Ease off dry crackers, chips, heavily roasted food, and very salty meals — salt is drying in TCM's flavor framework.

🌬️ Pattern 3: Dry Skin and Thin Blood — The Surface Loses Its Coating

What it is: the outer-surface presentation, where dryness shows up on the skin, eyes, and hair. In TCM theory, Blood moistens and nourishes the skin; when fluids are depleted, the surface loses its coating and can start to "stir Wind" — the TCM way of describing itching that moves and shifts rather than staying put.

Signs may include: skin that feels tight after washing; flaking, especially on the lower legs and forearms; eczema that flares as indoor heating comes on; dry eyes and dry mouth; brittle nails and dull hair; chapped lips that never fully recover.

Why it happens: people whose reserves were already thin — a pattern TCM calls Blood deficiency — tend to feel autumn hardest, because dryness is a subtraction from an account that was not full to begin with. If that description sounds familiar, see our explainers on Blood Deficiency and Liver Blood.

What traditionally may help: healthy fats at most meals (olive oil, nuts and seeds, fatty fish, avocado), cooked dark leafy greens, eggs, shorter and cooler showers, moisturizer applied within three minutes of toweling off, and a humidifier in the bedroom.

Important: if you use prescribed medication for eczema, asthma, or allergies, keep using it. Seasonal adjustments are an addition, not a replacement.

Autumn's Second Theme: Gathering Inward and Letting Go

The physical story of autumn is dryness. The other half of the story is direction. In the TCM seasonal model, summer's energy sits at the surface — expansive, social, outward. In winter it is stored deep. Autumn is the season of gathering in (收, shou): bringing the harvest in, closing the gates, turning toward home.

You have probably already felt this without naming it. Wanting to sleep an hour earlier than you did in June. Declining plans you would have accepted in July. A sudden urge to declutter, finish things, make lists. TCM reads these as the season's natural current, not as a personal failing of motivation or sociability.

And here is where the Metal element gets interesting emotionally. Each element in TCM carries an emotional assignment. Summer's is joy. Winter's is fear. Autumn's is grief — and its quieter cousin, sadness. The Lung is the organ most associated with grief in TCM theory, which is not as strange as it sounds: when you cry, your chest tightens and your throat closes. The emotional and physical experience of grief happens in exactly the same real estate as breathing.

Analogy: a tree dropping its leaves is not the tree grieving. It is the tree running a calculation — this leaf is no longer profitable, so it is being cut loose. TCM's observation is that humans tend to hold on to what the tree lets drop: the unsaid word, the unfinished argument, the version of your life from five years ago. Autumn is traditionally considered the season that makes setting those down easier. Not because the season does the work for you, but because the current is running in that direction.

A five-minute closing ritual for the season. Traditional seasonal practice often included a deliberate act of release rather than waiting to feel released. If that appeals to you, try this once a week through autumn:

  1. Breathe first (2 minutes). Inhale through the nose for a count of four, exhale for a count of six. Ten rounds, unhurried.
  2. Name one thing (30 seconds). Say out loud one thing you are done carrying — a commitment, a grudge, a story about yourself.
  3. Write it down. Put it on paper and leave the paper somewhere for the season — a drawer, a jar, a book. You are not making a decision. You are just putting it down for now.
  4. Finish with the breath (1 minute). Ten exhales, each one longer than the inhale that preceded it.

On waking in the small hours. On the TCM organ clock, the Lung's assigned window is 3 to 5 AM. If you notice you are surfacing in that window with a heavy chest or a low, formless mood, that timing is meaningful within the framework — see our guide to waking between 3 and 5 AM, and for the broader emotional map, Emotions and the Organs.

A clear line, because it matters: seasonal low mood is common and usually mild. But if low mood lasts most of the day for more than two weeks, comes with hopelessness, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, take that as a medical situation and get help the same day — in the US, call or text 988. TCM is a complementary perspective. It is not a plan for depression.

Autumn Eating: Moistening Foods and the Return of Cooked Meals

In summer, the body tolerates raw, cool, and light. Come autumn, the traditional shift is back toward warm and moist. The underlying rule is simple matchmaking: respond to the season's deficit with the opposite quality. Dry air, so eat watery. Cooling air, so eat warm.

Analogy: your dinner plate as a sponge. A dry sponge is brittle, tears easily, and can't absorb anything you give it. A damp sponge is flexible and takes up whatever it's offered. Autumn eating is mostly about keeping the kitchen sponge damp — choosing food with moisture built in rather than moisture baked out.

Here is how the traditional categories sort out:

Traditionally moistening — bring in Traditionally drying — ease off
Pears (especially steamed), apples, white fungus, honey, sesame seeds, walnuts, almonds, eggs, spinach, daikon radish, tomato, seaweed, oats and congee, olive oil, bone broth, tofu, milk Heavy dry-roasted nuts and seeds, crackers and chips, deep-fried food, large amounts of chili and dry pungent spice, very salty meals, excess coffee and alcohol, baked goods with no moisture in them

Add water to the meal itself. This is the single most practical autumn rule: soup, stew, congee, steamed vegetables. A test you can apply at dinner: if everything on the plate crunches and nothing is wet, you've eaten a summer meal in an autumn body.

The classic autumn dish: steamed pear. Across Chinese households, the seasonal home remedy that shows up every year is a pear — peeled, cored, steamed about fifteen minutes, finished with a small drizzle of honey, and eaten warm, spoonful by spoonful, when the throat feels dry. It is about as low-tech as seasonal eating gets, and it is the first thing many families reach for in October.

Safety notes for autumn eating: honey should not be given to infants under one year. If you manage diabetes, keep honey and fruit portions within your plan. If you have kidney disease, high-potassium foods such as daikon, tomato, and seaweed need provider guidance rather than a general rule. And if you are pregnant or managing an autoimmune condition, check significant dietary changes with your provider first.

Your Autumn Daily Rhythm: Sleep Earlier, Breathe Deliberately

Seasonal eating is one lever. The daily rhythm is the other — and in TCM's view, it is the one that matters more, because it changes how much reserve you carry into winter.

1. Shift your bedtime earlier, by 30 to 60 minutes. The classical advice for autumn and winter is to sleep earlier and rise with the light — extending sleep relative to the summer months. Both the TCM logic (a contracting season is a storing season) and the physiology (shorter photoperiod, shifted melatonin timing) point the same direction. If you change one habit this season, change this one.

2. Fix the air in the room you sleep in. Aim for roughly 40 to 60 percent relative humidity — an inexpensive hygrometer tells you where you actually stand, which is usually lower than people assume once heating begins. A humidifier at night helps. Keep the vent or fan from blowing directly on your face, and use a warm compress over the chest and upper back in the evening if the "closed-down" feeling is strong.

3. If you use a saline nasal rinse, use the right water. This is a genuine safety point, not a formality: use distilled water, sterile water, or water that has been boiled and then cooled. Never use untreated tap water for nasal rinsing, and clean the device after each use.

4. Two minutes of slow nasal breathing, twice a day. Inhale through the nose for four counts, exhale for six, ten rounds — on waking and before bed. Nasal breathing warms and moistens air before it reaches the lower airways, which is precisely the job TCM assigns to the Lung's dispersing function. It is also the cheapest intervention in this article.

5. Add a few minutes of humming. Humming a comfortable note for three to five minutes is a traditional voiced practice, and it has an intriguing modern wrinkle: research suggests humming substantially increases nasal nitric oxide output. We'll come back to that in the research section below.

6. Keep moving — and build, don't coast. Autumn is a good season to add gentle strength work or qigong to a daily walk, building the "shelf" of fitness before winter's storage phase. The seasonal trap is going from summer athlete straight to winter couch; steady beats dramatic.

7. Adapt to cool air gradually, not by shock. Cool-weather walks are a reasonable first step for most people. Cold plunges are a separate decision with their own contraindications, and they are not required for anything described here.

Acupressure for Autumn: Four Points That Support the Lung System

Acupressure is the home-friendly cousin of acupuncture — sustained finger pressure instead of needles. In TCM theory, these four points influence the Lung channel and its paired relationships with the throat and the Large Intestine. Think of this as self-care you can experiment with, not as a substitute for medical care.

LU-9 (Taiyuan) — the master valve on the Lung line

Where: at the wrist crease on the thumb side, in the hollow just below the base of the thumb bone.
Why it's used: in TCM theory LU-9 is the point where the Lung channel's Qi gathers and is described as influential over the vessels and the Lung's moisture. Think of it as the master valve on the Lung channel's water line — opening it is traditionally thought to help the whole system distribute rather than dry out.
How: thumb pad, slow small circles, about 60 seconds per wrist, breathing evenly. A mild ache or warmth is normal; sharp pain is a signal to stop.

LU-7 (Lieque) — the point for the throat and the surface

Where: roughly one and a half finger-widths above the wrist crease on the thumb side, in the bony notch you can feel there.
Why it's used: LU-7 is traditionally associated with the throat, the head and neck, and the body's outer surface — a common choice when the throat is raw or when you feel something coming on.
How: press with the thumb pad angled toward the bone, 30 to 60 seconds each wrist, then switch.

KI-6 (Zhaohai) — LU-7's classic partner

Where: in the hollow just below the inner ankle bone, about a finger's width down from its lowest point.
Why it's used: KI-6 and LU-7 are one of the traditional paired combinations, used in TCM theory for the throat and for dryness patterns. Working the pair together is more common than working either alone.
How: both thumbs, 60 seconds on each ankle, slow exhales.

BL-13 (Feishu) — the Lung's back door

Where: on the upper back, about one and a half finger-widths out from the spine, at roughly the level where the shoulder-blade ridge meets the spine.
Why it's used: BL-13 is the Lung's associated point on the back — the "back door" to the organ system in TCM theory. It's a common point in seasonal and respiratory practice.
How: have a partner press with thumbs or knuckles for 60 to 90 seconds while you breathe slowly, or lie on a tennis ball placed in the muscle between the shoulder blade and the spine. Never place pressure directly on the spine itself.

Acupressure safety: avoid broken, bruised, or infected skin. Avoid deep pressure during pregnancy unless your provider has cleared it, and use only light pressure in the area below the inner ankle if you are pregnant. Stop if you feel sharp pain, numbness, tingling, or dizziness. Do not use pressure on the upper back if you've had recent spinal or rib injury or surgery without medical clearance. And acupressure is not for emergencies — chest pain or difficulty breathing needs immediate medical attention.

What Research Says About Dry Air, Mucosal Health, and Seasonal Shift

None of the following validates the entire TCM framework — no single study can. But several lines of modern research happen to land on the same practical terrain that the seasonal model has been pointing at for centuries.

Dry air and the airway's first line of defense. A 2019 study published in PNAS found that low ambient humidity impaired airway barrier function and the innate resistance of the respiratory tract to influenza infection in animal models, while moderate humidity supported it. Related work — Lowen and colleagues, 2007, in PLoS Pathogens — showed that influenza transmission in guinea pigs was markedly more efficient in dry air than in humid air. In TCM terms, dry air weakens the Lung's outer defense; these findings suggest something measurable is happening at the level of the airway lining.

Why respiratory infections cluster in the cool, dry months. The seasonal pattern of respiratory illness is one of the oldest observations in medicine, and TCM's traditional attention to the Lung in autumn aligns with it. Some studies suggest the pattern reflects both humidity effects on the airways and behavioral changes such as indoor crowding and reduced ventilation.

Light, circadian rhythm, and seasonal mood. Shorter autumn days shift melatonin timing and circadian phase. The seasonal pattern of low mood was formally described in the 1980s (Rosenthal and colleagues) and is recognized clinically as a seasonal specifier for depression. TCM's "gather inward" advice lands in adjacent territory — different mechanisms, overlapping advice about light exposure, sleep timing, and routine.

Vitamin D. As the sun's angle lowers, cutaneous vitamin D synthesis declines. Some studies suggest a seasonal dip in vitamin D status from autumn through winter. If you are at risk of deficiency, that is a conversation to have with your provider rather than a self-diagnosis.

Humming and nasal nitric oxide. Weitzberg and Lundberg (2002) reported that humming increased nasal nitric oxide output by roughly an order of magnitude compared with quiet exhalation, and suggested this may help keep the sinuses ventilated. It's a striking bridge to the traditional emphasis on voiced breathing practices for the Lung — a low-cost habit worth three minutes of your evening.

What this means for you: the useful takeaway isn't that TCM predicted modern physiology. It's that a handful of the season's practical recommendations — humidify your air, protect and moisten your airway lining, keep your light and sleep rhythm regular, move every day — line up with things research supports for its own reasons. Think of the seasonal framework as an organizing system for habits that hold up either way.

Practical Takeaways: Your Autumn Wellness Checklist

Your autumn checklist — pick two to start this week:

  • Fix the air before you fix yourself. Get a hygrometer, aim for 40 to 60 percent humidity in the room you sleep in, and run a humidifier at night.
  • Eat one wet thing at every meal. Soup, stew, congee, steamed vegetables, or warm fruit — the meal should not crunch all the way through.
  • Steam a pear. Peeled, cored, fifteen minutes, a drizzle of honey. Eat it warm in the evening when your throat feels dry.
  • Move bedtime 30 to 60 minutes earlier than your summer schedule. This is the highest-leverage single change of the season.
  • Two minutes of 4-6 nasal breathing morning and night, plus three minutes of humming if you enjoy it.
  • Four points, about a minute each: LU-9 and LU-7 on both wrists, KI-6 on both ankles, and BL-13 with a partner or a tennis ball.
  • Name one thing you're putting down this season. Write it. That is the Metal element's homework — and arguably the most autumnal thing on this list.
  • Keep walking daily and add gentle strength work. Don't let the season turn you into a couch.

When to See a Doctor

The seasonal advice in this article is for ordinary, mild, run-of-the-mill autumn discomfort. The following signs are not seasonal adjustments — they need medical assessment:

  • A cough lasting more than three weeks, bringing up blood, or accompanied by breathlessness or chest pain
  • Fever above 101°F (38.3°C), or fever with confusion, severe headache, or a stiff neck
  • Wheezing that doesn't settle, or needing a rescue inhaler more often than usual — if you have asthma or COPD, keep using your prescribed medication, as acupressure is not a replacement
  • Unexplained weight loss, drenching night sweats, or a hoarse voice lasting more than two weeks
  • Severe dry eyes with pain or a change in vision, or cracked skin showing signs of infection — spreading redness, swelling, pus, or warmth
  • Low mood lasting most of the day for more than two weeks, or any thoughts of harming yourself — seek help urgently (in the US, call or text 988)
  • If you are pregnant, or managing diabetes, kidney disease, an autoimmune condition, or a chronic respiratory condition, check with your provider before making significant dietary or supplement changes

Cold and flu season is also vaccination season for many people. TCM is a complementary perspective that can sit alongside conventional care — it isn't an either-or choice, and your provider is the right person to help you decide what belongs in your plan.
