Internal Wind in TCM: When the Body's Stability Falters

TCM Basics · 7 min read

You reach for your coffee cup and your hand is trembling — just slightly, but you notice it. Or your eyelid has been twitching for three days straight. Or you stand up from the couch and the room tilts for a second, then settles. You mention it to your doctor. The scan is clean. The blood work is normal. "Probably stress," you're told. "Maybe fatigue. It happens."

And it does keep happening — small tremors, muscle twitches, moments of dizziness that come and go without warning.

Western medicine is good at naming these experiences: essential tremor, benign fasciculations, vertigo of unknown origin. What it's less good at is explaining why they come and go, why stress makes them worse, and why some bodies seem to generate instability from the inside — like a wobble that never quite settles.

In TCM theory, that wobble has a name: Internal Wind (Nei Feng, 内风). It's not a diagnosis you'll find in a Western textbook, and it's not a claim about a physical substance. It's a functional description — a way of saying that the body's stability system is being disturbed from within, and that this disturbance moves, shifts, and flares the way wind does.

Think of a spinning top. A top stays upright when it spins fast and evenly. Slow it down, tilt its axle, wear down one side — and it begins to wobble. That wobble is internal wind: the body's version of a gyroscope losing its stability. The tremors, the twitches, the sudden dizziness — they're the wobble, not the cause.

What Is Internal Wind in TCM?

Wind is one of the most important concepts in the whole TCM system — the ancient texts call it "the mother of a hundred illnesses," because wind-like symptoms are sudden, mobile, and changeable. A headache that moves from temple to temple. A rash that appears in one spot and reappears in another. A tremor that shows up only when you try to hold still. Wherever symptoms move, TCM suspects wind.

But there are two very different kinds of wind, and confusing them is the most common mistake newcomers make:

  • External Wind comes from outside — the cold draft on the back of your neck, the chill after getting soaked, the spring breeze that brings a stiff neck or a head cold. It's an invader, and TCM's classic response is to expel it from the surface of the body. For the full story on external wind and the other five pathogens, see our guide to the Six Pathogens.
  • Internal Wind doesn't come from anywhere outside. It's generated inside the body, like a flag whipping in a sealed room. There's no draft — the movement comes from the room itself.

Think of it this way: external wind is a weather system. Internal wind is a structural problem in the building — a floor that tilts, a beam that has lost its tension. You can dress for the weather, but you can't dress for a structural wobble; you have to address the structure.

In TCM theory, internal wind is almost always connected to the Liver (Gan, 肝) — the organ system responsible for smooth, even movement throughout the body. The classic formula from the ancient medical text Huang Di Nei Jing states it directly: "All wind with tremor and dizziness belongs to the Liver." When the Liver's ability to keep things smooth fails, the body starts to wobble.

How Internal Wind Forms: The Four Generators

In TCM theory, internal wind doesn't appear out of nowhere. It's generated — usually slowly, over months or years — by one of four underlying conditions. Each has a different origin story, a different flavor of symptom, and a different practical response. Most people find one of these closer to home than the others.

🔹 Generator 1: Liver Yang Rising — The Pressure Cooker

The most common generator in modern life. Years of stress, bottled frustration, long working hours, and poor sleep gradually turn the Liver's smooth flow into a build-up. In TCM theory, stuck Qi generates heat; heat rises; and rising Yang rattles the lid on a pot of steam. Symptoms: a hand tremor that's worse when you're stressed or tired, tension headaches, ringing in the ears, irritability, and a flushed, "wound up" feeling. Flare triggers are almost always on a short list: stress, anger, caffeine, alcohol, and sleep debt.

🔹 Generator 2: Liver Fire Generating Wind — The Heat Shimmer

On a hot day, heat rising off asphalt makes the road appear to ripple and shake. Nothing is actually moving — the heat is distorting the air's stability. In TCM theory, extreme internal heat does the same thing to the body. This generator usually follows a period of intense fire: a high fever, a burst of volcanic anger, or years of heat-fueling habits such as heavy alcohol, very spicy food, and chronic rage. Symptoms: intense agitation, a red face and eyes, pounding headaches, muscle twitches, and in its most severe form, convulsions — the classic "wind from extreme heat" medical emergency.

🔹 Generator 3: Yin Deficiency Generating Wind — The Light Anchor

Picture a boat at anchor. With a heavy anchor, even a rough swell barely moves it. With a light anchor, the smallest wave rocks the whole hull. Yin in TCM is the body's cooling, moistening, anchoring substance — and it's the first thing to drain under aging, overwork, chronic stress, and insufficient rest. When Yin runs low, the body loses its ballast, and small disturbances become big wobbles. Symptoms: dizziness that comes in waves, an unsteady gait, night-time twitching, tinnitus, dry eyes and skin, and heat in the palms at night. This generator becomes more common with age — and in people who have spent decades running on empty.

🔹 Generator 4: Blood Deficiency Generating Wind — The Dry Leaf

In TCM theory, Blood nourishes and moistens the muscles and sinews. When Blood is insufficient — after illness, heavy menstrual losses, a poor diet, or long-term overwork — the tissues dry out, like a leaf that has lost its moisture. A dry leaf trembles in the gentlest breeze. Symptoms: twitching eyelids or muscles, numbness or tingling that moves around, dry itchy skin, and muscle cramps at rest. Blood-deficiency wind tends to be worse at night and after exertion, and it often shows up together with fatigue, a pale complexion, and dizziness. (For a deeper dive, see our guide to Blood Deficiency.)

The signature of wind is movement — symptoms that come on suddenly, shift location, and fade just as quickly. Here's how the four generators typically show up in daily life:

Important: internal wind rarely shows up as just one symptom. It's the pattern — instability that comes and goes, worsens with stress or exhaustion, and never quite resolves — that points toward wind.

Why the Liver Is at the Center of It All

The Liver in TCM is the system that keeps everything moving smoothly — energy, blood, mood, and muscle tone. It also stores Blood (the raw material of nourishment) and rules the sinews and tendons. When its flow is smooth, the body moves smoothly; when it's stuck, heat builds; when it's depleted, the body loses its anchoring substances. Every one of the four generators traces back to the Liver's state — too much pressure (Yang rising), too much heat (Fire), too little cooling fluid (Yin deficiency), or too little fuel (Blood deficiency). For a full tour of how the Liver works in TCM theory, see our guide to the Liver in TCM.

This is also why stress is the single most common trigger for wind-like symptoms in the TCM framework. The Liver is the organ system most sensitive to frustration and pressure — the emotional equivalent of a traffic jam. When the jam persists long enough, the whole system starts to vibrate.

What the Research Says

Internal wind is a traditional framework, not a laboratory finding — but several lines of modern research run parallel to its observations. Here are a few bridges, with the caveat that the evidence is preliminary and the comparisons are conceptual:

  • Tremor is common and often "invisible" to scans. Essential tremor is one of the most common movement disorders, with prevalence estimates ranging from roughly 1% of the general population to 4–5% in people over 65, depending on the study. It's believed to involve circuits between the cerebellum and the thalamus — a kind of motor wobble with no structural damage visible on standard imaging. That matches the TCM picture of a stability system disturbed without anything being visibly "broken."
  • Stress amplifies tremor. Studies consistently show that stress and anxiety increase the amplitude of physiological tremor — adrenaline directly boosts the tiny oscillations every muscle has. Some research suggests caffeine does the same. The recognizable "stress plus coffee" tremor is a perfect everyday match for Liver Yang Rising and its trigger list.
  • Sleep debt and twitching. Research notes that sleep deprivation increases physiological tremor and can trigger visible muscle twitches (fasciculations), as motor units fire erratically. In TCM terms, that's wind feeding on depleted reserves — the Yin and Blood deficiency generators.
  • Dizziness and stress. Dizziness affects a large share of adults — some studies estimate 20–30% experience it at some point. Research on persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD) describes chronic unsteadiness that flares with stress and fatigue while standard tests come back normal — a surprisingly close mirror of the "light anchor" pattern.
  • Acupuncture for tremor and vertigo. A number of small trials and systematic reviews report preliminary evidence that acupuncture may help reduce tremor severity in essential tremor and vertigo recurrence in some vestibular conditions. Most studies are small and results are mixed, so this is emerging rather than established.
  • Blood deficiency has a modern echo. Iron-deficiency anemia is associated with restless legs and muscle cramps in some studies — a rough parallel to the TCM idea that under-nourished muscle tissue becomes dry and twitchy. TCM's "Blood deficiency" is broader than anemia, but the shared intuition is interesting.

Practical Ways to Support Stability

Internal wind is a pattern, not a single fix. In the TCM framework, support works on the four things that feed wind: pressure (Liver Yang), heat (Liver Fire), depleted reserves (Yin), and under-nourishment (Blood). These are everyday, evidence-curious steps:

1. Sleep is the anchor

Yin rebuilds during deep rest — and every generator of internal wind is worsened by sleep debt. Wind-friendly sleep hygiene: a consistent bedtime, a dark cool room, and no screens for 60–90 minutes before bed. If you're a night owl running on empty, this single change does more than any other step in the TCM toolkit.

2. Give the pressure a release valve

Acupressure is the at-home version of the classic approach. Two points matter most for wind:

  • LV-3 (Taichong) — on the top of the foot, in the soft hollow between the big toe and second toe. Its name means "great surge," and it's the classic point for moving stuck Liver Qi and settling rising Yang. One to two minutes of small circles per side when you feel wound up.
  • GB-20 (Fengchi) — literally "wind pool," in the hollows at the base of the skull where the neck meets the head. It's the classic point for wind of any kind, external or internal. Gentle fingertip circles for a minute or two.

3. Cool the system down

Caffeine and alcohol are the two most common wind fuels in modern life — both stir Liver Yang and both drain Yin over time. Reducing them is the single most practical "cooling" step. For the light-anchor pattern, KI-3 (Taixi) — in the depression between the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon — is the point most associated with steadying Kidney Yin, the body's deepest anchor.

4. Feed the reserves

Traditional Yin- and Blood-supporting foods include black sesame, goji berries, eggs, cooked leafy greens, oats, black beans, walnuts, and bone broth. The TCM digestion system favors warm, cooked meals over cold raw fare. And in the opposite direction: excess caffeine, alcohol, and very spicy food are the classic wind-fans.

5. Train the wobble out

Slow, grounded movement — walking, qigong, tai chi — is the traditional answer to unsteadiness. It's also one of the best-researched: tai chi in particular is among the most-studied movement practices for balance and fall risk in older adults. You don't need to be unsteady to benefit; the practice itself teaches the nervous system to stay calm while the body moves.

6. The 90-second settle

When a tremor or twitch flares with stress: 60 seconds of extended-exhale breathing (in for 4, out for 6–8) followed by 30 seconds of gentle pressure on LV-3 on each side. It won't rewire the pattern overnight, but it gives the nervous system a brake pedal in the moment — and it's a small, repeatable habit that stacks over weeks.

When to See a Doctor

Internal wind is a lens, not a diagnosis — and some symptoms need urgent medical evaluation regardless of framework. See a healthcare provider promptly if you experience:

  • Sudden, severe dizziness with vomiting, or a new severe headache
  • Sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body, facial drooping, slurred speech, or difficulty walking — these are stroke warning signs; call emergency services immediately
  • A tremor that appeared suddenly, is getting worse, or interferes with eating, writing, or daily tasks
  • Seizures or convulsions of any kind
  • Muscle twitching that doesn't stop, or that comes with weakness or muscle wasting
  • Dizziness paired with chest pain, palpitations, or fainting

TCM is a complementary perspective, not a replacement for medical care. New or worsening neurological symptoms always deserve a proper medical workup first.

Practical Takeaways

What to remember about internal wind in TCM:
  • Internal wind describes instability generated from within — tremors, twitches, dizziness, and shifting numbness that come and go
  • It's different from external wind: external wind is a weather system; internal wind is a structural wobble
  • Four generators: Liver Yang Rising (pressure), Liver Fire (heat), Yin Deficiency (light anchor), Blood Deficiency (dry leaf) — each with different triggers and different support approaches
  • Stress, sleep debt, caffeine, and alcohol are the most common wind fuels in modern life
  • The TCM support toolkit: consistent sleep, stress release (LV-3, GB-20), Yin support (KI-3), warm cooked meals, and grounded movement like qigong or tai chi
  • New, sudden, or worsening tremor or dizziness needs a medical evaluation — TCM is a complementary lens, not a replacement for care
