Yuan-Source Points: The Body's Reserve Power Outlets

TCM Basics · 6 min read

You've probably noticed something odd about acupuncturists: they always seem to end up at your wrists and ankles. The pulse is taken at the wrist. Needles go into the web between your thumb and index finger, the crease of your wrist, the hollow behind your inner ankle bone, the top of your foot between your first two toes. If you've ever wondered why these specific neighborhoods get so much attention, the answer is one of TCM's best-kept organizational secrets: the Yuan-Source points (原穴, yuan xue) — twelve specific acupoints, every single one sitting at your wrists or ankles, that act as access ports to the deep Qi of each organ system.

Think of the last time a mechanic checked your car. They didn't tear the engine apart to know it was struggling — they plugged into the diagnostic port, a small outlet near the steering wheel, and the car's own systems reported what was wrong. Yuan-Source points work on similar logic: they are the body's built-in diagnostic ports, the places where the deepest layer of an organ's energy surfaces close enough to touch.

Before we dive in: Yuan-Source points are the practical "ports" that access Yuan Qi — the deep reserve Qi you were born with. If you haven't read our explainer on Yuan Qi (Original Qi) yet, start there for the concept, then come back here for the map of where it surfaces. For the bigger picture of how all points fit into the channel system, see How the Meridian System Works. This article focuses on one specific category of points — the source points — and what they actually do.

The 12 Yuan-Source Points: All of Them at Your Wrists and Ankles

Each of the twelve main meridians has exactly one Yuan-Source point. In classical TCM texts — most famously the Nan Jing (Classic of Difficulties), compiled around the first century — source points are described as the places where the organ's original Qi "pools" or "resides" close to the surface. Here is the complete map:

Notice the pattern: six at the wrists, six at the ankles. No source point lives in the middle of your torso or buried deep in a muscle. They all sit at the extremities — and that is not an accident.

Why Wrists and Ankles? The Logic of the Access Port

In TCM theory, each meridian is like a river that runs from an organ deep inside the body out to the surface of the hands and feet. The deep current — the organ's original Qi — flows beneath the surface for most of the journey. But at the wrists and ankles, the channel rises close to the skin, like a river widening into a shallow delta where the water is easy to reach.

There is an elegant engineering logic here. The deepest energy of an organ is, by definition, hard to touch directly — you can't reach into the Kidney or Liver with a fingertip. So the classical system placed a "port" at the most accessible point of each channel: far from the torso, at the ends of the limbs, where the energy runs near the surface. A practitioner works at the port to reach the organ — no surgery required. It is the same design principle as the external maintenance panel on a machine: the part you interact with is exactly where the maker put it, because that is where access is easiest.

What a Source Point Can Tell You: The Diagnostic Side

Because a source point is where an organ's deep Qi surfaces, it also becomes a place to listen. In classical practice, a practitioner gently palpates (presses) the source points and reads what they feel:

  • Tenderness or a "gritty" sensation — a common clinical observation is that the point associated with a strained organ feels tender, tight, or unusually sensitive, like pressing on a bruise you didn't know you had.
  • A sunken or hollow feeling — traditionally read as the organ's Qi running low, similar to the soft spot on an underinflated tire.
  • A full, swollen, or congested feel — traditionally read as stagnation, like a hose that is firm with pressure behind a kink.

This is why TCM assessment often includes the wrists and ankles even when the complaint is in the chest or belly: the source points act as a dashboard of twelve indicator lights, one per organ system. A skilled practitioner doesn't just read the lights — they use them to decide which system deserves attention. (For more on the classic assessment toolkit, see our guide to TCM diagnosis methods.)

How Source Points Are Used: Three Access Layers

Source points are one of three classic "access layers" TCM uses to reach the organs from the surface. Knowing the other two makes the whole system easier to picture:

  • Back-Shu points — on the back along the spine, one per organ. These are like direct phone lines to each organ, accessed from behind (we mapped them in our article on mid-back pain).
  • Front-Mu points — on the chest and abdomen, where organs sit closest to the front surface. Like service hatches on the front of the machine.
  • Yuan-Source points — at the wrists and ankles, the remote diagnostic ports where each channel's deep current surfaces.

In practice, the layers are used together: a practitioner may check the source points to gauge overall organ state, the Back-Shu points to pinpoint which organ is signaling, and then work through whichever layer responds best. The Nan Jing states it plainly: when the organs are unwell, the source points are where to intervene. Modern acupuncturists still rely on them constantly — HT-7 for the Heart, LV-3 for the Liver, KI-3 for the Kidney, and so on.

A Simple Self-Care Routine with Source Points

You don't need needles to work with source points. Firm, gentle pressure with a fingertip or thumb — one to two minutes of slow small circles per point, on both sides — is the traditional at-home approach. A few practical pairings for common situations:

  • For a restless, wired mind at bedtime: HT-7 (Shenmen), at the inner wrist crease on the little-finger side. A classic point for calming the Heart and settling the Shen (the spirit-mind).
  • For stress, frustration, or tension headaches: LV-3 (Taichong), on the top of the foot between the first and second toes. Traditionally used to move Liver Qi that has become stuck.
  • For deep fatigue or achy lower back: KI-3 (Taixi), between the inner ankle bone and the Achilles tendon. The Kidney's source point, used in TCM practice to support the deepest reserve.
  • For sluggish digestion after heavy meals: ST-42 (Chongyang), at the highest point of the instep. The Stomach's source point.
Pregnancy caution: LI-4 (Hegu, between thumb and index finger) is a source point that is traditionally avoided during pregnancy — strong stimulation there is classically contraindicated. If you are pregnant, work only with a qualified practitioner and skip self-stimulation of LI-4 and SP-6 (Sanyinjiao, inner lower leg).

What Research Suggests About Source Points

Yuan-Source points don't have a direct Western equivalent, but several research threads produce interesting parallels:

  • HT-7 and sleep. Small clinical trials have explored acupressure at HT-7 (Shenmen) for sleep quality, with some studies suggesting improvements in sleep measures. The evidence base is modest — a promising area, not a settled one.
  • LV-3 and the emotional brain. Several fMRI studies have examined acupuncture at LV-3 (Taichong), noting changes in brain regions associated with emotional regulation and pain processing. This parallels the traditional use of LV-3 for stress and frustration patterns.
  • Electrical properties of points. A line of research stretching back decades has explored whether acupoints — including wrist and ankle points — show different electrical characteristics (such as lower skin resistance) than surrounding skin. Results have been mixed and methodologically debated, but the question of whether acupoints are physically distinct sites remains actively studied.
  • Point specificity. Systematic reviews of acupuncture trials have examined whether the choice of point matters versus pressing anywhere — the "specificity" question. Findings are mixed, but some meta-analyses report differences favoring traditional point selection in certain conditions, which keeps the source-point system an active research topic.

As always with TCM, the honest framing is that these are interesting parallels, not proof of the classical model. The practical value of the source-point system is that it gives you a reliable map — a repeatable place to check in with each organ system, using signals that Western medicine measures differently.

Practical Takeaways:

  • Memorize the map. Twelve source points, six at your wrists and six at your ankles — one access port per organ system.
  • Use them as a listening tool. A tender, hollow, or congested-feeling source point is a signal worth noticing, not a problem to suppress.
  • Build a two-minute ritual. HT-7 for a wired mind before bed, LV-3 after a stressful day, KI-3 when energy runs low — gentle circles for 1–2 minutes per point.
  • Read the concept layer first. Yuan Qi is the deep reserve; source points are where it surfaces. The two ideas belong together.
  • Pair with the full system. Back-Shu points locate, source points assess, and practitioners read both together — no single layer tells the whole story.
When to See a Doctor: If you are using acupressure for a symptom — especially fatigue, pain, anxiety, or digestive issues that persist — check with a healthcare provider first to rule out medical causes. See a doctor promptly for unexplained weight loss, fever, or night sweats; chest pain or shortness of breath; sudden severe pain; numbness or weakness; or any symptom that is new, worsening, or interfering with daily life. Never delay or stop prescribed medical care in favor of a complementary approach.
